## MDPs

* Sequential Decision Problems
- **Goal**: Agents need to make decisions in the real world:
  - In a _stochastic environment_
  - Where utility depends on a _sequence of decisions_

- **What is involved**
  - _Uncertainty_: Represent the unpredictability in outcomes, modeled
    using probabilities
    - E.g., weather forecasts often include uncertainty (70% chance of rain)
  - _Utility functions_: Measure the desirability of outcomes by quantifying
    preferences
    - E.g., assign higher values to outcomes with more profit and lower risk
  - _Rewards_: Yielded by the environment as feedback for actions taken
    - E.g., receive points in a game for completing a level
  - _Sensing_: Gather information about the environment using sensors
    - E.g., a robot using a camera to detect obstacles in its path
  - _Search and planning_: Find a sequence of actions to achieve a goal
    - E.g., a navigation system planning the shortest route to a destination

* Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)
::: columns
:::: {.column width=70%}
- **MDPs** are a model for sequential decision making

1. **Initial state** $s_0$
2. **Environment**: _fully observable_ but _stochastic_
   - Sensors give complete state of the environment
   - Next state is not completely determined by current state and agent's action
3. **Action**: agent chooses $a \in Actions(s)$ in each $s$
4. **Transition model**
   - $\Pr(s'|a, s)$ is probability of reaching state $s'$, if action $a$ is
     done in state $s$
   - Markov assumption: probability depends on $(s, a)$, not on history
5. **Reward function**
   - For every transition $s \to s'$ via $a$ the agent receives a reward
     $R(s',a,s)$
   - Total reward depends on sequence of states and actions
6. **Goal states**

* MDP: Solution

- **Definition**: The solution of an MDP is a _policy_ _"in state $s$ take action $a$"_
  $$
  \pi(s): s \to a \in Actions(s)
  $$

- **Definition**: _Environment history_ is a sequence of states and actions
  $$(s_0, a_0) \to (s_1, a_1) \to ... \to (s_i, a_i) \to ...$$
  - Any execution of the policy leads to a different _environment history_
    because of the stochastic nature of the environment

- **Definition**: A policy is measured by the _expected utility_ of the
  environment history
  $$
  U(\pi(s)) = \EE[f(s_0, \pi(s), R(s',a,s))]
  $$
  - The _optimal policy_ $\pi^*(s)$ yields the highest expected utility
    $$
    \pi^*(s) = \argmax_{\pi} \; \EE[f(s_0, \pi(s), R(s',a,s))]
    $$
  - The optimal policy is function of the reward function

## Utilities Over Time

* Utility Function
- **Definition**: The _utility function_ for environment histories (finite or
  infinite) $h$ is expressed as:
  $$
  U_h([\blue{s_0, a_0}, \green{s_1, a_1}, ..., s_n, ...])
  $$

- **Definition**: _Finite horizon_ indicates a fixed time $N$ after which nothing
  matters:
  $$
  U_h([s_0, a_0, s_1, ..., \red{s_N, ...s_{N+k}}])
  = U_h([s_0, a_0, s_1, ..., \red{s_N}]) \; \forall k > 0
  $$
  - Actions are chosen based on the current state and remaining steps
  - Time matters, so it leads to non-stationary policies

- **Definition**: _Infinite horizon_ indicates that there is no fixed time limit,
  i.e., the process continues indefinitely
  - The optimal policy can be _stationary_
    - Same action is chosen whenever the agent visits the same state
    - Policies do not depend on the specific time step

* Additive Rewards
- **Definition**: _Additive rewards_ means that rewards for each transition
  $s_i \xrightarrow{a_i} s_{i+1}$ are summed:
  $$
  U_h([s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \ldots]) = \sum_{i=0} R(s_i, a_i, s_{i+1})
  $$

- **Definition**: _Additive discounted rewards_ include a factor $\gamma \in [0, 1]$:
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  U_h([s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \ldots])
  &= R(s_0, a_0, s_1) + \gamma R(s_1, a_1, s_2) + \gamma^2 R(s_2, a_2, s_3) + \ldots \\
  &= \sum_{i=0} \gamma^i R(s_i, a_i, s_{i+1}) \\
  \end{aligned}
  $$
  where:
  - $\gamma \to 0$: future rewards are negligible
  - $\gamma \to 1$: future rewards significant

- **Pros**: Discounted rewards
  - Reflect human tendency to prioritize near-term rewards
  - In economics, early rewards can be reinvested, compounding further rewards
  - Support infinite horizons, preventing unbounded rewards

* Expected Utility of a Policy
- We said that the same policy leads to different environment histories, because
  of stochastic behaviors

- **Definition**: the expected _utility of executing policy_ $\pi$ from state
  $s$:
  $$
  U^\pi(s) = \EE[ \sum_{i=0}^\infty \gamma^i R(S_i, \pi(S_i), S_{i+1}) ]
  $$
  where the expectation $\EE[\cdot]$ is:
  - Over state sequences determined by $s$, $\pi(s)$
  - The environment's transition model $\Pr(s'|a, s)$

- **Definition**: the _optimal policy_ is the one with maximal utility
  $$
  \pi_s^* = \argmax_{\pi} U^{\pi}(s)
  $$
  - With discounted utilities and infinite horizons, the optimal policy is
    independent of the starting state: $\pi_s^* = \pi^*$
  - This is not true in general (e.g., for finite-horizon policies or
    non-additive rewards)

* Principle of Maximum Expected Utility (MEU)

- **Intuition**
  - MEU posits: _"A rational agent should choose the action that maximizes its
    expected utility based on its beliefs"_

- **Example**: An agent must choose between:
    - `Action A`: 80% chance of reward 10; 20% chance of reward 0
    - `Action B`: 100% chance of reward 6
  - By MEU
    - $\EE[U(A)] = 0.8 \cdot 10 + 0.2 \cdot 0 = 8$
    - $\EE[U(B)] = 1.0 \cdot 6 = 6$
    - $\EE[U(A)] > \EE[U(B)]$
    - Choose `Action A`

* Maximum Expected Utility (MEU): Definition
- **Assumptions**:
  - Possible actions: $a \in A$
  - Possible outcomes: $s'$
  - Probability distribution: $\Pr(s'|a)$ for each action
  - Utility function $U$: assign a numerical value $U(s')$ to each state

- The expected utility of action $a$ is:
  $$
  \EE[U(a)] = EU(a) = \sum_{s'} U(s') \Pr(s'|a)
  $$

- A rational agent chooses the action:
  $$a^*
  = \argmax_{a \in A} \sum_{s'} U(s') \Pr(s'|a)
  $$

* Utility of a State

- **Remark**: The utility of a state $s$ is not absolute but it depends on the
  policy
  $$
  U^\pi(s) = \EE[ \sum_{i=0}^\infty \gamma^i R(S_i, \pi(S_i), S_{i+1}) ]
  $$
  - To remove the dependency from the policy, use the optimal policy

- **Definition**: the utility of a state $s$, $U(s)$, is the expected sum of
  discounted rewards under an optimal policy from $s$
  $$U(s) = U^{\pi^*}(s)$$
  - Of course it still depends on the expected rewards $R()$ and the discount factor
    $\gamma$

- **Intuition**: The utility of a state reflects the long-term desirability of
  that state under optimal behavior

* Bellman Equation
- **Recall**: The utility of a state $s$, $U(s)$, is the expected sum of
  discounted rewards under an optimal policy $\pi^*$ from $s$
  $$
  U(s) = U^{\pi^*(s)} = \EE[ \sum_{i=0}^\infty \gamma^i R(S_i, \pi^*(S_i), S_{i+1}) ]
  $$

- **Theorem** (Bellman Equation): the _utility of a state_ $s$ is the expected
  reward for the next transition plus the discounted utility of the next state,
  assuming the agent chooses the optimal action:
  $$
  U(s) = \max_{a \in A(s)} \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|a, s)[R(s',a,s) + \gamma U(s')]
  $$
  where:
  - $A(s)$: set of actions available in state $s$
  - $\Pr(s'|a, s)$: probability of transitioning to state $s'$ from state $s$
    by action $a$
  - $R(s',a,s)$: reward after transitioning from state $s$ to $s'$ using $a$
  - $\gamma$: discount factor, where $0 \leq \gamma < 1$

* Bellman Equation: Computing Utility of States
- Bellman equation for $s$ is:
  $$
  U(s) = \max_{a \in A(s)} \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|a, s)[R(s',a,s) + \gamma U(s')]
  $$
- Each state has its own equation based on its possible actions and
  transitions
- Each equation is recursive: utility of $s$ depends on utilities of its
  successor states

- Write Bellman equations for all states gives a system of equations

- Under certain conditions (e.g., finite state/action spaces, $\gamma < 1$):
  - This system has a unique solution
  - The utility function $U(s)$ is well-defined
  - E.g., in a grid world with a finite number of cells and actions

* Bellman Equation: Intuition
- **Recall**: Bellman equation for $s$ is:
  $$
  U(s) = \max_{a \in A(s)} \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|a, s)[R(s',a,s) + \gamma U(s')]
  $$

- **Remarks**:
  - Says: _"Utility of a state = Best immediate action + Future potential"_
  - Balances short-term gain and long-term value when outcomes are partly under
    the control of a decision-maker and partly random

- **Example**: Maze
- To find the fastest path to the goal in a maze, the Bellman equation
  prescribes:
  - _"Your current position is only as valuable as the best path out of it"_
  - Best path combines current proximity (reward now) and future position
    quality (reward later)
  - Value backs up from future to present, similar to tracing a route from
    finish to start

- **Example**: Chess game
  - The optimal strategy involves making the best move at each turn while
    considering future moves and potential outcomes

* Q-Function
- Aka "Action-utility function"

- **Definition**: the _Q-function_ $Q(s, a)$ is the expected utility of taking an
  _action_ $a$ in _a given state_ $s$, and acting optimally afterward

- **Remark**: Q-function $Q(s, a)$ is the "dual" view of utility of states $U(s)$
  - Express the utility of a state in terms of utility of actions:
    $$
    U(s) = \max_a Q(s, a)
    $$

- **Theorem**: Bellman equation for Q-functions
  $$
  Q(s, a) = \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|s, a)[R(s',a,s) + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a')]
  $$
  - Note the switch of $\max_{a'}$ with respect to Bellman equation for utility
    of state

- **Theorem**: The optimal policy picks the best action at each step
  $$
  \pi^*(s) = \argmax_a Q(s, a)
  $$

* Shaping Theorem
- **Theorem**: For discounted sums of rewards, the _scale of utilities_ is
  arbitrary
  - Affine transformation $U'(s) = m \cdot U(s) + b$ doesn't change optimal
    policy $\pi^*(s)$
  - Only relative ordering of utilities matters for decision-making

- **Theorem**: Potential-based reward shaping
  - Function $\Phi(s)$ of state $s$ doesn't change optimal policy:
    $$
    R'(s',a,s) = R(s',a,s) + \gamma \Phi(s') - \Phi(s)
    $$
  - Ensures consistent value difference between states

- **Pros**
  - _Speed_: Significantly speeds up learning by guiding the agent
    - E.g., potential function increasing with proximity to goal encourages
      faster convergence
    - E.g., animal trainers provide small treat for each step in target sequence
  - _Safety_: Prevents misleading agent into suboptimal policy
    - E.g., agent might prioritize short-term rewards over long-term gains

* Representing MDP
- The _transition model_ $\Pr(s'|a, s)$ and the _reward function_ $R(s',a,s)$
  can be represented with:
  - Three-dimensional tables of size $|S|^2 \cdot |A|$
  - Table with size $O(|S| \cdot |A|)$ for sparse MDPs (i.e., each $s$
    transitions to only a few states $s'$)

- **Definition**: _Dynamic Decision Networks (DDNs)_ can represent MDPs
  - Probabilistic graphical model extending Bayesian networks for sequential
    decision problems under uncertainty
  - Factored representation, compactly encoding state variables and dependencies
  - More scalable and expressive than atomic (flat) representations
    - E.g., in a large MDP with many states, a DDN can efficiently represent the
      problem without explicitly listing every possible state transition

## Solving MDPs

* MDP Solution: Overview

- **Problem**: An MDP defines a sequential decision problem, but how do we
  actually compute an optimal policy $\pi^*$?
  - Two classic dynamic programming algorithms exist
    - _Value Iteration_: directly computes the optimal value function $U(s)^*$
      and derives policy at the end
    - _Policy Iteration_: iteratively evaluates and improves a candidate
      policy $\pi(s)$

- **Key difference**: what each algorithm updates at each step

- **Analogy**: planning a road trip
  - _Value Iteration_:
    - You repeatedly update _"What is the best possible travel time from each
      city?"_
    - Start with guesses, then refine using neighbors' estimates
    - Eventually all estimates become accurate $\to$ then choose the best road
      from each city

  - _Policy Iteration_:
    - Start with a route plan: _"Always take highway A"_
    - Compute how good this plan is
    - Switch to better roads where highway A underperforms
    - Repeat until the route plan stops changing

* Value Iteration (1/2)
- **Algorithm**: _Value iteration_
  1. Compute optimal utility $U^*(s)$
     - Write $n$ Bellman equations in $n$ unknowns $U(s)$
     - Solve the simultaneous system of equations
  2. Extract optimal policy $\pi^*$ from utilities $U(s)$ by choosing the action
     with the highest value

- **Step 1**: Compute optimal utility for each state
  - There are $n$ possible states $\to$ $n$ Bellman equations
    $$
    U(s) = \max_{a \in A(s)} \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|a, s)[R(s',a,s) + \gamma U(s')]
    $$
  - Each equation relates the utility of a state $s$ to the utilities of its
    successors $s'$
  - Solve these equations $n$ equations with $n$ unknowns simultaneously
    - Equations are non-linear due to max operator $\to$ use an iterative approach

* Value Iteration (2/2)

- **Step 1**: Solve system of Bellman equations iteratively
  - Start with arbitrary values for utilities $U(s)$
  - Perform Bellman updates:
    $$
    U_{i+1}(s) \leftarrow \max_a \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|a,s)[R(s',a,s) + \gamma U_i(s')]
    $$
  - Calculate the right-hand side and plug it into the left-hand side
  - Repeat until convergence: $|| U_{i+1} - U_i || < \epsilon$

  - _Convergence_ to single fixed point is guaranteed for additive discounted
    rewards and $\gamma < 1$
    - No strict update order required for convergence
    - Intelligent ordering can improve speed especially in large or structured MDPs

- **Step 2**: Compute optimal policy
  - Derive optimal policy by choosing action $a$ that maximizes expected utility
    for each state $s$:
    $$
    \pi^*(s) = \argmax_a \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|a,s) [R(s',a,s) + \gamma U(s')]
    $$

* Policy Iteration

- **Algorithm** solves MDPs by iteratively improving a policy
  1. Evaluate current policy by computing $U(s)$ for each state
  2. Improve current policy $\pi(s)$ using Bellman equations
  3. Iterate until convergence

- **Step 1**: Policy Evaluation
  - Start with an initial (random) policy $\pi$
  - Compute $U^\pi(s)$ by solving:
    $$
    U^\pi(s) = \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|s,\pi(s))[R(s,\pi(s),s') + \gamma U^\pi(s')]
    $$

- **Step 2**: Policy Improvement
  - For each state, find:
    $$
    \pi'(s) = \argmax_a \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|a,s)[R(s',a,s) + \gamma U^\pi(s')]
    $$

- **Step 3**: Convergence
  - Repeat until policy is unchanged or close to convergence
  - _Convergence_ to an optimal policy for finite MDPs is guaranteed
    - Each iteration strictly improves or maintains policy performance

* Value vs Policy Iteration

- Value Iteration: _"Learn how good every state is, then derive the best policy"_
- Policy Iteration: _"Start with a policy, evaluate it, improve it, and repeat
  until it can't get any better"_

\begingroup small
| **Value Iteration** | **Policy Iteration** |
|----------------|------------------|
| Updates values directly | Updates policies directly |
| Policy is extracted at the end | Policy is improved every iteration |
| Simpler update rule | Two-step process (evaluate + improve) |
| Many cheap iterations | Fewer but more expensive iterations |
| Often preferred for large problems | Often converges in fewer iterations |
\endgroup

* Off-Line vs On-Line Solution of MDPS

- **Offline methods** precompute full solutions
  - E.g., value iteration, policy iteration
  - _Pros_:
    - Compute entire optimal policy $\pi^*(s) \; \forall s$ before action
  - _Cons_:
    - Assumes full knowledge of transition probabilities $\Pr(s'|a, s)$ and
      reward function $R(s',a,s)$
    - Not feasible for large MDPs (e.g., Tetris with $10^{62}$ states)

- **Online methods** compute actions at runtime, using only reachable parts of
  the state space
  - Agents interleave planning and acting
  - _Pros_:
    - Focuses computation on relevant parts of the state space
    - Allows adaptive, real-time decision-making
    - No need for full model of the MDP
  - _Cons_:
    - Requires fast, accurate state evaluation functions
    - May need significant computation at each decision point
    - Needs exploration and careful tradeoff with exploitation

## POMDPs

* Partially Observable MDPs (POMDPs)

- **Motivation**
  - _Traditional MDPs_ assume full observability (i.e., agent knows its state)
  - _Real-world situations_ often lack precise state knowledge
  - _POMDPs_ extend MDPs to handle state uncertainty

- **Definition**: _POMDP_ defined by $(S, A, P, R, E)$
    - States: $S$
    - Actions: $A$
    - Transition model: $P = \Pr(s'|a,s)$
    - Reward function: $R(s',a,s)$
    - Sensor model: $E = \Pr(e|s)$: probability of observing evidence $e$ in state
      $s$

- **Belief States**
  - Belief state $b(s)$: probability distribution over possible states $s$
  - Agent maintains $b(s)$ as internal environment representation
  - Policies depend on belief states (and not actual state $s$): $\pi(b)$

* MDP vs POMDP: Comparison

\begingroup \scriptsize
| **Aspect** | **MDP** | **POMDP** |
|----------|----------|----------|
| _State Observability_ | Fully observable state | Partially observable state |
| _Formal Definition_ | $(S, A, P, R)$ | $(S, A, P, R, E)$ |
| _State Information_ | Agent knows the exact current state | Agent receives incomplete or noisy observations |
| _Decision Basis_ | Current state $s$ | Belief state $b$ |
| _State Representation_ | State $s$ | Hidden state $s$ and observation $e$ |
| _Transition Model_ | $\Pr(s'|a, s)$ | $\Pr(s'|a, s)$ |
| _Observation Model_ | Not required | $\Pr(e|s', a)$ |
| _Policy_ | $\pi(s)$ | $\pi(b)$ |
| _Memory Requirement_ | Only current state needed (Markov property) | Must maintain a belief state $b(s)$ |
| _Uncertainty Source_ | Uncertainty only in state transitions | Uncertainty in both transitions and observations |
| _Complexity_ | Lower computational complexity | Higher computational complexity |
| _Typical Example_ | Chess, fully observable grid world | Robot navigation with noisy sensors |
\endgroup

* Belief State Transitions

- **Belief Update**
  - After action $a$ and observation $e$, update belief state $b$:
    $$
    b'(s') = \alpha \Pr(e|s') \sum_s \Pr(s'|a,s) b(s)
    $$
    where $\alpha$ normalizes the distribution
  - Same as filtering task to compute new belief state $b'(s)$

- **Belief Space**
  - Policy, transition, and reward models:
    - Depend on belief state $b$
    - Can't depend on actual state $s$, as agent doesn't know it
  - Information-gathering actions can improve future decision quality

- **Transition and Reward Models in Belief Space**
  - Transition: 
    $\Pr(b'|b,a) = \sum_e \Pr(b'|e,a,b) \Pr(e|a,b)$
  - Reward:
    $\rho(b,a) = \sum_s b(s) \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|a,s) R(s',a,s)$

- **Decision Cycle for POMDP**
  - Repeat
    1. Given belief state $b$, execute action $a = \pi(b)$
    2. Observe percept $e$
    3. Update belief state $b'$

* Solving POMDPs
- Convert POMDP on state space to MDP on belief space

- **Value Iteration for POMDPs**
  - Maintain conditional plans $p$ with utility vectors $\alpha_p$
  - Expected utility in belief state $b$: $b \cdot \alpha_p$
  - Optimal utility is piecewise linear, convex over belief space

- **Recursive Plan Evaluation**
  $$
  \alpha_p(s) = \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|a,s) [ R(s',a,s) + \gamma \sum_e \Pr(e|s') \alpha_{p.e}(s') ]
  $$

- **Challenges**
  - Plans grow exponentially with depth
  - Small problems generate many plans (e.g., $2^{255}$ plans for two-state
    POMDP at depth 8)
  - Use approximation techniques

* Hidden Confounders vs Hidden State

- POMDPs hide state because the agent can't observe it — more sensing would
  resolve the ambiguity
- Confounding hides a cause because it influences both action and reward outside
  the model entirely — no amount of sensing the modeled variables fixes it
- A confounded MDP can look just like a fully observable one: the agent sees
  state, action, and reward, yet still draws the wrong conclusion about what
  caused what
- This distinction motivates why "solve the POMDP" and "find the causal
  structure" are different problems, even though both involve something the agent
  can't directly see

* Problem with Supervised Learning
- _In supervised learning_ an agent learns by observing input/output examples

- **Problem**
  - Hard to find labeled data for all situations

- **Example**: apply supervised learning to play chess
  - Take board position as input $\vx$ and return move $m$
  - Build training set of grandmaster games with positions and winner (assuming

* Reinforcement Learning

- **Definition**: Reinforcement Learning (RL) Paradigm
  - Agent learns from interaction with environment
  - _Receives_ reward signals indicating success or failure ("reinforcements")
  - _Learns_ policy $\pi$
  - _Goal_: maximize expected sum of future rewards

- **RL vs supervised learning**
  - Easier to provide reward signal than inputs/outputs
  - RL is active: agent explores environment, learns from actions and
    consequences

- **RL vs MDP**
  - Both aim to maximize expected sum of rewards
  - In RL, the agent:
    - Doesn't know transition model or reward function (rules)
    - Acts to learn more

* Sparse vs Immediate Rewards
- **Definition**: _sparse rewards_: most states lack informative reward
  - E.g., win/lose at the end of a chess game
  - Explore many states to find rewarding ones
  - Requires sophisticated exploration strategies

- **Remark**: Immediate/intermediate rewards guide learning
  - E.g.,
    - Tennis: rewards for points scored
    - Crawling: forward motion is a reward
    - Video game: coins or power-ups as intermediate rewards
  - Provides continuous feedback to agent

- **Finance and Trading**
  - Learn portfolio management and trading strategies
  - Maximize returns under uncertainty and risk

- **Healthcare**
  - Learn personalized treatment policies from patient data

- **Industrial Control**

- **Natural Language Processing**
  - Dialogue systems and chatbots learn conversational strategies

## Model-Based vs Model-Free RL

* Model-Based vs Model-Free RL: Intuition

- **Model-based RL** is like learning to ride a bike by studying manual and
  physics
  - Plan a sequence: "to make this turn at the corner, I should steer by 5
    degrees..."
  - When I change bike, you can update the parameters of the model, instead of
    learning from scratch
  - _"I understand how the world works, so let me plan ahead"_

- **Model-free RL** is like learning to ride a bike purely through trial and error,
    and repetition
  - You don't have an explicit theory of physics, balance, or torque
  - You just adjust your actions based on whether you fell down or stayed
    upright
  - Over many trials, you develop good intuitions about what to do in each
    situation, but you never explicitly model why leaning works
  - _"I know it works but I don't know why"_

* Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

- **Definition**: Model-based RL
  - Collect experience tuples $(s',a,s, r)$
  - Update environment model
    - _Transition model_: estimates $\Pr(s'|a,s)$
    - _Reward model_: estimates $R(s,a)$
  - Plan using model to improve policy (e.g., value iteration, policy iteration)
  - E.g., Dyna-Q

- **Pros**
  - Efficient sample usage: fewer real-world interactions
  - Enables planning by simulating outcomes

- **Cons**
  - Learning accurate model is challenging
  - Model errors can propagate, leading to poor decisions

* Model-Free Reinforcement Learning

- **Definition**: Model-Free RL
  - Learns directly from interactions with the environment without building a
    model of dynamics
  - Agent observes $(s',a,s, r)$ and updates value or policy estimates based
    on observed outcomes
    - E.g., Q-learning (learn $Q(a, s)$)
    - E.g., REINFORCE (learn policy)
  - No attempt to estimate $P(s'|a,s)$ or $R(s,a)$

- **Pros**
  - Simpler to implement when environment model is unknown or too complex
  - Robust to model inaccuracies since no model is used

- **Cons**
  - Requires more environment interactions (sample inefficient)
  - Harder to incorporate planning or long-term reasoning

* Model-Based vs Model-Free RL: Comparison

\begingroup \scriptsize
| **Aspect** | **Model-Based RL** | **Model-Free RL** |
|----------|----------|----------|
| _Core Distinction_ | Learns environment dynamics $P(s'\|a,s)$ and $R(s,a)$; uses model for planning | Learns value functions $Q(s,a)$ or policies $\pi(a\|s)$ directly from experience |
| _Sample Efficiency_ | Generally more sample efficient due to simulated planning | Typically needs more environment interactions |
| _Computation_ | Higher planning overhead; simulations required | Simpler computations per step; often more scalable |
| _Flexibility and Robustness_ | Sensitive to model inaccuracies | More robust to model errors (since it doesn't learn one) |
| _Typical Use Cases_ | Robotics, planning tasks, known environments | Games, large-scale unknown or stochastic environments |
| _Examples_ | Dyna-Q, PILCO | Q-learning, Deep Q-Networks (DQN), REINFORCE |
\endgroup

* When Correlation Misleads the Model

A learned transition/reward model captures statistical regularities in the data — not necessarily the mechanisms producing them
If two variables are correlated only because of a shared hidden cause, the model can "predict" well in training yet recommend the wrong action
Example: an agent learns that a state feature predicts reward, but a confounder (not an action) is the true driver — the policy exploits a correlation that won't hold once the agent intervenes
Raises the question: what would it mean for a model to capture causal structure rather than just predictive structure?

## Active vs Passive RL

* Active vs Passive Reinforcement Learning

- **Intuition**: it is about who controls the agent's behavior while it's
  learning the value of states or a policy in a MDP

- Core Distinction
  - **Passive RL**: Learns value of a fixed policy; does not choose actions
  - **Active RL**: Learns both the value function and optimal policy through
    exploration

\begingroup \scriptsize
| **Aspect** | **Passive RL** | **Active RL** |
|----------|----------|----------|
| _Policy Handling_ | Follows a given policy $\pi(s)$ and estimates $V^\pi(s)$ or $Q^\pi(s,a)$ | Improves policy over time, aiming for $\pi^*(s)$ that maximizes reward |
| _Exploration_ | No exploration — strictly evaluates the given policy | Explores actions to improve the policy (e.g., $\epsilon$-greedy, softmax) |
| _Learning Goal_ | Accurate value function for a known policy | Optimal policy and value function via interaction |
| _Algorithms_ | Temporal Difference Learning (TD), Adaptive Dynamic Programming for a fixed policy | Q-learning, SARSA, policy iteration methods |
| _Use Cases_ | Evaluation of policies from human demonstrations or expert systems | Autonomous agents discovering optimal strategies from scratch |
| _Sample Efficiency_ | Can converge faster if policy is close to optimal | Slower due to need for exploration and policy improvement |
| _Challenges_ | Limited by the quality of the provided policy | Balancing exploration and exploitation effectively |
\endgroup

* Passive Learning Agent
- **Setup**
  - A fully observable environment with a small number of actions and states
  - The agent:
    - Has a fixed policy $\pi(s)$ to determine its action
    - Needs to learn $U^\pi(s)$, the expected discounted reward if policy $\pi$ is
      executed starting in state $s$
    - Doesn't know the transition model $\Pr(s'|s, a)$ and the reward function
      $R(s',a,s)$

- **Algorithm**
  - The agent executes a set of trials using the policy $\pi$:
  - Starts from an initial state and experiences state transitions until
    reaching terminal states
  - Stores actions and rewards at each state $(s_0, a_0, r_1, s_1, ..., s_n)$
  - Estimates:
    $$
    U^{\pi}(s) = \EE[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R(S_t, \pi(S_t), S_{t+1})]
    $$
    average the returns from all episodes in which $s$ was visited:
    $$
    U^\pi(s) = \frac{1}{N(s)} \sum_{i=1}^{N(s)} G_i(s)
    $$
    where $G_i(s)$ is the return after state $s$ in episode $i$

- **Example**
  - Evaluating a delivery drone's route utility based on completed delivery
    missions without changing its behavior

- **Pros**
  - Unbiased estimator of utility under $\pi$
  - Simple

- **Cons**
  - High variance: requires many samples
  - Inefficient if state visits are rare or episode lengths vary greatly
  - (Important): Ignore the connections between states given that the utility
    values obeys the Bellman equation for a fixed policy

* Adaptive Dynamic Programming

- **Objective**
  - Learn utility estimates $U^\pi(s)$ for a fixed policy $\pi$ using an
    estimated model of the environment

- **Key Components**
  - Model learning: Estimate transition probabilities $\Pr(s'|a,s)$ and reward
    function $R(s,a)$ from experience
  - Utility update: Solve the Bellman equations for the fixed policy:
    $$
    U^\pi(s) = R(s, \pi(s)) + \gamma \sum_{s'} \Pr(s'|s, \pi(s)) U^\pi(s')
    $$

- **Learning Process**
  - Collect transitions $(s, \pi(s), r, s')$ during execution
  - Update model estimates:
    - $\Pr(s'|a,s) \approx$ empirical frequency
    - $R(s,a) \approx$ average observed reward
  - Use dynamic programming to compute $U^\pi(s)$

- **Example**
  - Suitable when environment dynamics are stationary and can be learned from
    interaction
  - A thermostat estimates room temperature dynamics and uses them to predict
    comfort level under a fixed heating schedule

- **Pros**
  - More sample-efficient than direct utility estimation
  - Leverages structure of the MDP to generalize better

- **Cons**
  - Requires accurate model estimation
  - Computational cost of solving Bellman equations repeatedly

* Temporal-Difference Learning

- **Objective**
  - Estimate utility values $U^\pi(s)$ for a fixed policy $\pi$ using experience
    without a model

- **Key Idea**
  - Combine benefits of Monte Carlo methods and Dynamic Programming
  - Update estimates after every transition using bootstrapping

- **TD(0) Update Rule**
  - When a transition occurs from state $s$ to state $s'$ via action $\pi(s)$,
    we apply the update:
    $$
    U^\pi(s) \leftarrow U^\pi(s) + \alpha [ r + \gamma U^\pi(s') - U^\pi(s) ]
    $$
    where:
    - $s$ is the current state
    - $r$ is the immediate reward
    - $s'$ is the next state
    - $\alpha$ is the learning rate
    - $\gamma$ is the discount factor

- **Characteristics**
  - Online and incremental: updates occur after each step
  - Does not require knowledge of model $P(s'|a,s)$ or $R(s,a)$

- **Example**
  - Real-time systems, streaming environments, or when dynamics are too complex
    to model
  - An autonomous cleaner estimates long-term dirtiness reduction for each room
    it passes through, adjusting based on immediate results

- **Pros**
  - More efficient and lower variance than Monte Carlo methods
  - Simpler and more memory-efficient than model-based methods

- **Cons**
  - Introduces bias due to bootstrapping
  - May require careful tuning of learning rate $\alpha$

* Active Reinforcement Learning

- **Definition**: Active RL learns an optimal policy through interaction with the
  environment
  - Agent chooses actions to balance exploration (gather info) and exploitation
    (maximize reward)
  - Does not follow a fixed policy — improves policy over time
  - Exploration strategies: $\epsilon$-greedy, softmax, UCB

- **Active RL vs Passive RL**
  - Passive: follows a fixed policy $\pi$, estimates $U^\pi(s)$
  - Active: chooses actions to improve policy $\pi \to \pi^*$

- **Cost-Sensitive Reward Querying** (extension of active RL)
  - Useful when querying for rewards is expensive or limited (e.g., human
    feedback)
  - Agent decides whether to query for reward $r$ at cost $c$
  - Objective: maximize cumulative reward minus query costs
  - Applications: robotics with costly sensors, human-in-the-loop systems

* Greedy Agent in Reinforcement Learning

- A greedy agent always selects the action with the highest estimated value
  based on current knowledge or Q-values
  $$
  a = \argmax_a Q(s, a)
  $$
  - For state $s$
  - No exploration: purely exploits known information
- An agent must make a tradeoff between
  - Exploitation of current best action to maximize its short-term reward
  - Exploration of unknown states to gain information that can lead to a change
    in policy (and greater rewards in the future)
  - E.g., in life you need to decide continuing a comfortable existence, or try
    something unknown in the hopes of a better life

- Goal: efficient learning with minimal queries to maximize information gain per
  unit cost

- Strategies include:
  - Random follow greedy policy or explore
  - Cost-aware exploration: modify exploration bonus based on query cost
  - Confidence-based querying: only query when uncertain about reward
  - Give weight to actions not tried very often and avoid actions (believed to
    be) of low utility
  - Use an exploration function $f(u, n)$ to trade-off greed (preference for
    high values of utility) vs curiosity (preference for actions not tried
    before)

- Example:
  - An agent exploring a maze may avoid querying in familiar areas
  - Queries are focused on uncertain or novel states

* Safe Exploration in Reinforcement Learning
- In idealized settings, agents can explore freely and learn from negative
  outcomes (e.g., losing in chess or simulations)
  - E.g., a self-driving car in simulation can crash without consequences

- In the real world, exploration has risks:
  - Irreversible actions may lead to states that cannot be recovered from
  - Agents can enter "absorbing states" where no further rewards or actions are
    possible
  - E.g., a crash that destroys a self-driving car permanently limits its future
    learning

- Safer Policy Approaches
  - **Bayesian Reinforcement Learning**: Maintain a probability distribution
    over possible models
    - Compute a policy that maximizes expected utility across all plausible
      models
    - In complex cases, leads to an "exploration POMDP" which is computationally
      intractable but conceptually useful
  - **Robust Control Theory**: Optimize for the worst-case scenario among all
    plausible models
    - Resulting policies are conservative but safe
    - E.g., agent avoids any action that could possibly lead to death
  - Impose constraints to prevent the agent from taking dangerous actions
    - E.g., safety controllers can intervene in risky states for autonomous
      helicopters

* Temporal-Difference Q-Learning
- Q-learning is a model-free reinforcement learning algorithm
  - Learns the value of taking an action in a given state, denoted $Q(s, a)$
  - Does not require a model of the environment

- Temporal-difference (TD) learning updates estimates based on other learned
  estimates
  - Unlike Monte Carlo methods, it updates after every step using bootstrapping

- **Q-learning update rule:**
  $$
  Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha [ r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a) ]
  $$
  - $\alpha$: learning rate
  - $r$: reward received after action $a$
  - $\gamma$: discount factor for future rewards
  - $s'$: next state
  - $a'$: next action

- The update aims to reduce "the TD error"
  $r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)$, i.e., the difference between current
  estimate and observed return

- Converges to the optimal action-value function $Q^*$ under certain conditions
  - Requires exploration of all state-action pairs
  - Learning rate must decay appropriately

- E.g., in a gridworld, Q-learning learns the optimal path to the goal by
  updating $Q$ values as it explores

- Exploration strategy (e.g., $\epsilon$-greedy) is crucial for balancing
  learning and exploitation

## Generalization in Reinforcement Learning

* Generalization in Reinforcement Learning
- Tabular representations become infeasible for large state spaces
  - Real-world problems often have millions or more distinct states
  - Example: Backgammon has ~$10^{20}$ states, but successful agents visit only
    a small fraction

- Function approximation enables scalability and generalization
  - Replace large tables with parameterized functions: $\hat{U}_\theta(s)$ or
    $\hat{Q}_\theta(s, a)$
  - Linear example:
    $\hat{U}_\theta(s) = \theta_1 f_1(s) + \cdots + \theta_n f_n(s)$

- Benefit: Generalizes from visited states to unvisited ones
  - Allows efficient learning with fewer examples

- Temporal-Difference (TD) and Q-learning adapt to function approximation
  - TD update:
    $$
    \theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i + \alpha [r + \gamma \hat{U}_\theta(s') -
    \hat{U}_\theta(s)] \frac{\partial \hat{U}_\theta(s)}{\partial \theta_i}
    $$
  - Q-learning update:
    $$
    \theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i + \alpha [r + \gamma \max_{a'}
    \hat{Q}_\theta(s',a') - \hat{Q}_\theta(s,a)] \frac{\partial \hat{Q}_\theta(s,a)}{\partial \theta_i}
    $$
  - Issues and solutions:
    - **Divergence**: parameters can grow uncontrollably
    - **Catastrophic forgetting**: important knowledge can be lost
    - **Solution**: experience replay reuses old data to stabilize learning

- Deep reinforcement learning:
  - Uses deep neural networks to approximate $U$ or $Q$
  - Learns features automatically from raw inputs (e.g., images)

- Reward shaping:
  - Adds auxiliary (pseudo)rewards to guide learning
  - Must be designed carefully to avoid misleading the agent

- Hierarchical RL:
  - Structures behavior into reusable subprograms and subgoals
  - Decomposes complex tasks to improve learning efficiency

* Why Correlational Policies Fail to Transfer

A policy tuned to correlations in one environment can break when the environment shifts, even if the reward-relevant mechanism hasn't changed
Spurious features (artifacts of how training data was generated) get baked into the policy alongside real causal drivers
Generalization failure is often not "the task changed" — it's "the correlation the agent relied on no longer holds"
Sets up the core promise of causal RL: policies built on causal structure should transfer wherever the underlying mechanism is preserved

## Policy Search

* Policy Search in Reinforcement Learning
- A policy $\pi(s)$ maps states to actions
  - Use a parameterized representation with fewer parameters than states (e.g.,
    linear, deep neural network): $\pi_\theta(s)$
  - Directly optimizes parameters $\theta$ of the policy $\pi_\theta(s)$ rather
    than value functions
  - Pick the value with highest predicted value
    $$
    \pi_\theta(s) = \argmax_a \hat{Q}_\theta(s, a)
    $$
  - Useful in high-dimensional or continuous action spaces

- Even if learning a function replaces the Q-function, it is not an
  approximation of the Q-function (as in Q-learning)
  - Seek a function that gives good performance and might differ from the
    optimal Q-function $Q^*$

- To avoid jittery policy for discrete actions, use stochastic policies for
  smoother optimization:
  - E.g., softmax over Q-values
    $$
    \pi_\theta(s,a) = \frac{e^{\beta \hat{Q}_\theta(s,a)}}{\sum_{a'} e^{\beta \hat{Q}_\theta(s,a')}}
    $$
    where $\beta$ controls exploration vs exploitation

- If everything is continuous and differentiable, use gradient descent to find
  optimal policy
  - If environment (or policy) is not deterministic, compute averages to smooth
    out the gradient
  - High variance in trial outcomes makes gradients noisy
  - Requires many trials to ensure reliable gradient estimation

## Causal Reinforcement Learning

* Causal Reinforcement Learning
- Extends RL by incorporating causal inference — structural causal models,
  do-calculus, counterfactual reasoning — into how agents learn and act
- Goal: distinguish "this predicts reward" from "this causes reward," so policies
  are built on mechanisms rather than coincidental patterns in the data
- Addresses three recurring RL weaknesses: confounding, poor generalization under
  distribution shift, and sample inefficiency
- Bridges the gap left open by POMDPs (hidden state) and model-based RL
  (predictive vs. causal models)

* From Correlation to Causation in RL
- Standard RL agents optimize over correlations between states, actions, and
  rewards observed during interaction
- Causal RL asks the agent to additionally represent why an action leads to an
  outcome, not just that it tends to
- Key formal tool: the do-operator, distinguishing "reward given we observed
  action a" from "reward given we forced action a"
- This distinction is invisible in standard MDP formalism but becomes critical
  once data comes from sources other than the agent's own exploration

* Confounding in Sequential Decision Making
- A confounder is a hidden variable influencing both the chosen action and the
  resulting reward
- In sequential settings, confounding can compound across time steps, distorting
  credit assignment over an entire trajectory, not just one decision
- Common source: logged or human-generated data, where the policy that generated
  the data depended on factors the agent never observes
- Unaddressed confounding can make an agent confidently learn a policy that
  performs worse than random once deployed

* Structural Causal Models for MDPs
- A structural causal model (SCM) augments the MDP with explicit causal
  mechanisms: equations describing how each variable is generated from its causes
- The causal graph makes confounders, mediators, and colliders explicit, rather
  than leaving them implicit in a transition function
- Lets the agent separate "what the data shows" from "what would happen under
  intervention" — the basis for the do-calculus tools introduced above

* Counterfactual Reasoning and Credit Assignment
- Counterfactual reasoning asks "what would have happened had a different action
  been taken in this exact instance," not just on average across similar states
- Enables learning from a single trajectory by reasoning about unrealized
  alternatives, rather than requiring repeated environment interaction
- Improves credit assignment: helps isolate which action in a trajectory actually
  caused a later reward, versus which merely preceded it

* Deconfounding Offline/Off-Policy Data
- Offline RL trains on logged data from a different (often unknown) policy —
  exactly the setting where confounding is most likely
- Causal deconfounding methods identify which correlations in the logged data are
  exploitable for the new policy and which are artifacts of how the data was
  collected
- Without deconfounding, offline RL agents can overfit to the quirks of the
  logging policy rather than learning the true environment dynamics

* Causal Generalization and Transfer Across Environments
- Causal RL provides formal criteria (e.g., transportability) for when a policy
  learned under one set of conditions will hold under another
- Transfer depends on which causal mechanisms are shared between environments
  versus which have changed — not on surface similarity
- Reframes "will this policy generalize?" as a question about graph structure,
  answerable before deployment rather than discovered after failure

* Causal RL: Open Challenges
- Causal discovery from interaction data is hard, especially under partial
  observability or limited samples — most settings don't come with a known causal
  graph
- Integrating causal structure into deep RL architectures without losing
  scalability remains an open design problem
- Active research question: how much causal structure is necessary to gain
  robustness benefits, versus how much can be approximated statistically
