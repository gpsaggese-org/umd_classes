# Logic-Based AI Under Uncertainty

## Problem

* Logic-Based AI Under Uncertainty: Problem

- **Definition**: Logic-based AI systems
  - Based on _propositional logic_
  - Represent _actions_ using _rules_ like:
    - _"If preconditions P hold, then action A causes effect E"_

- **Example**:
  - _"If I turn the car key, the engine starts"_
  - _But_: the battery might be dead, there's no fuel, the starter is broken, ...

- **Problem**: Real-world agents face _uncertainty_ from:
  1. Partial observability
     - Agent can't see the full state of the world
  2. Non-determinism
     - Actions don't always have predictable outcomes
  3. Adversarial conditions
     - Other agents may interfere

* Logic-Based Under Uncertainty: Naive Solution

- **(Naive) Solution**: A possible solution to logic-based AI under uncertainty
  is to use:

  1. _Belief state_, i.e., set of all possible current world states
     - Cons: difficult to enumerate and track
  2. _Causal and exhaustive augmentation_ for rules
     - Cons: Must consider all possible preconditions for acting, even unlikely ones
  3. _Contingent plans_ to handle every possible sensor report and belief
     - Cons: Plans become large and complex
     - No guaranteed plan may exist, yet action is required

* Naive Solution: Example of Starting a Car
- **Goal**: build a system to start a car by turning the car key

- **Obvious preconditions**
  - The car has fuel
  - The car battery is charged
  - The car key is the right one
  - ...

- **Less obvious preconditions**
  - The electrical system is intact (no blown fuses)
  - The fuel pump is working
  - The fuel lines are not clogged
  - The engine oil level is sufficient
  - ...

- **Problem**: Every precondition requires something else in order to do to
  achieve the goal!

## Solution

* Causal and Exhaustive Augmentation

- **Definition**: Causal and exhaustive augmentation of logic-rules
  - To use propositional logic under uncertainty, need to augment the left-side of
    rules $X \implies Y$ to make it:
    1. _Causal_: identify true causal-effect relationships
    2. _Exhaustive_: identify all possible conditions $X$ leading to the outcome
       $Y$

- **Logical qualification problem**
  - Need to enumerate all the preconditions necessary for an action to succeed

- **Difficulties**
  - Every action can have more hidden conditions needed in order to succeed
    - E.g., start the engine $\implies$ turn car key $\implies$ starter motor is
      working $\implies$ battery is charged $\implies$ ...
  - Preconditions can change depending on the situation
    - E.g., ... $\implies$ battery is charged $\implies$ it's not
      too cold $\implies$ ...

* Causal and Exhaustive Augmentation: Limitations
- **Limitations** of logical qualification
  1. **Laziness**
     - Too much effort is required to create all possible rules
  2. **Theoretical ignorance**
     - Science doesn't always have a complete theory of a domain
     - E.g., medical science doesn't know all the "rules"
  3. **Practical ignorance**
     - Even if you knew all the rules, you might not have all the necessary
       information
     - E.g., not all required medical tests can be performed for a particular
       patient

- **AI winter (mid-1980s, 1990s)** due to expert systems failure
  - The real world is complex and open-ended
  - Logical rules can't capture all necessary and sufficient conditions

- **Question**: Is it true that $Rain \implies WetGrass$ ?
  - "$Rain \implies WetGrass$" is not true in general
  - If it rains but there is a cover over the grass, the grass will not be wet
  - If it rains but there is a high temperature, the wet grass might dry quickly

- **Question**: What about the converse "$WetGrass \implies Rain$"?
  - "$WetGrass \implies Rain$" is not true in general:
    - The grass could be wet because of a sprinkler system
    - The grass could be wet because of morning dew

- **Answer**: you also need to consider the propositions:
  - $Cover$ = _"there is a protective cover over the grass"_
  - $Evaporate$ = _"the water evaporates quickly"_
  - $Sprinkler$ = _"the sprinkler system is on"_
  - $Dew$ = _"there is morning dew"_

* Acting Under Uncertainty: Actual Solution
- Can't use propositional logic under uncertainty

- **Solution**: Acting under uncertainty requires combining:
  - _Probability_: to handle uncertainty and partial knowledge
    - E.g., agent's internal estimate of outcome likelihoods
  - _Utility functions_: for evaluating desirability of each outcome
    - E.g., combine goals like punctuality, comfort, legal compliance

- **Key idea:**
  - _Rational choice_ = plan that maximizes expected utility
  - Evaluate plans based on performance on average, given known information
  - Still success is not guaranteed!

* The Paradox of Probability and Knowledge
- **Paradox**
  - _"There is no uncertainty in the actual world!"_
  - E.g., the grass is wet, but either it has rained or not

- **Knowledge is subjective**
  - Probabilities relate to a knowledge state, not to the real world
  - Updating knowledge changes probability statements

* The Paradox of Probability and Knowledge: Example
- Initially, you observe _"wet grass"_
  - From past data you know that $\Pr(Rain | WetGrass) = 0.8$
  - Current belief: _"80% chance it rained if grass is wet"_
  - This is the Bayesian prior

- You further observe that _"sprinkler was on"_
  - Wet grass could be due to the sprinkler, not rain
  - Belief changes: $\Pr(Rain | WetGrass \land Sprinkler) = 0.4$
  - This is a Bayesian update

- You learn from the weather report that there was no rain
  - Belief changes: _"It didn't rain"_
  - Evidence overrides prior: $\Pr(Rain | WetGrass \land WeatherReport) = 0$

- All the statements were true even if contradictory
  - You need non-monotonic logic

## Conditional Independence

// ## 13.1, Representing knowledge in an uncertain domain (p. 425)

* Full Joint Probability Distribution

- **Definition**
  - Consider a set of random variables $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$
  - The _full joint probability distribution_ assigns a probability to every
    possible world:
    $$
    \Pr(X_1 = x_1, X_2 = x_2, \dots, X_n = x_n)
    $$
    where a **possible world** is a particular assignment of values to all
    variables

- **Fact**: full joint probability can answer any probabilistic query about the
  domain

- **Cons**
  - Size grows exponentially $k^n$ with the number of variables $n$ and number of
    values $k$
  - Impractical for real-world problems with many variables
  - Manually specifying each entry is tedious or impossible

- **Remark**: (conditional and absolute) independence simplifies modeling
  - In the real world, many variables are not fully dependent on all others
  - Reduces the number of variables needed in the model
  - Makes compact and structured representations possible
    - E.g., factorized probabilistic models, Bayesian networks

* Independence of Random Variables: Definition

- **Definition**: two random variables $X$ and $Y$ are **independent** iff:
  $$
  \Pr(X, Y) = \Pr(X) \cdot \Pr(Y)
  $$

- **Equivalently** knowing $Y$ tells nothing about $X$
  $$
  \Pr(X | Y) = \Pr(X)
  $$
  - E.g., the events "coin flip" and "weather" are independent
      $$
      \Pr(Coin=Heads | Weather=Rainy) = \Pr(Coin=Heads)
      $$

- **Consequence**: Independence of random variables
  - Reduces the number of parameters needed to model a system, e.g.,
    $$
    \Pr(X_1 | X_2, X_3) = \Pr(X_1)
    $$
  - Allows factorization of joint distribution, e.g.,
    $$
    \Pr(X_1, X_2, X_3) = \Pr(X_1) \cdot \Pr(X_2) \cdot \Pr(X_3)
    $$

* Conditional Independence: Definition

- **Definition**: two random variables $X$ and $Y$ are _conditionally
  independent_ given a random variable $Z$ iff knowing $Z$ makes $X$ and $Y$
  independent:
  $$
  X \perp Y | Z \iff \Pr(X, Y | Z) = \Pr(X | Z) \cdot \Pr(Y | Z)
  $$

- **Example**
  - $X$ = _"it is raining today"_
  - $Y$ = _"a person is carrying an umbrella"_
  - $Z$ = _"the weather forecast"_
  - _Without knowing $Z$_: there is a relationship between rain $X$ and umbrellas
    $Y$
  - _Given / knowing $Z$_: once the forecast is known $Z$, knowing whether
    someone carries an umbrella $Y$ gives no information about rain $X$
  - Thus, $X$ and $Y$ are conditionally independent given $Z$

- **Pros**
  - Conditional independence more common than absolute independence
  - Simplify probabilistic models by factorizing the joint conditional
    distribution into product of individual conditional distributions

* Conditional Independence: Example

* CI: Garden World Example

- Garden world with $Rain$, $Sprinkler$, and $WetGrass$
- Is $\Pr(Rain | Sprinkler) = \Pr(Rain)$ ?
  - _No_: if the sprinkler is on, it's less likely it rained
  - $Rain$ and $Sprinkler$ are not independent
- Is $\Pr(Rain | Sprinkler, WetGrass) = \Pr(Rain | WetGrass)$ ?
  - _Yes_: knowing the grass is wet, whether the sprinkler was on tells you

  - $Rain$ and $Sprinkler$ are conditionally independent given $WetGrass$

- **Definition**: _"Explaining away"_ (collider) occurs when:

  - Evidence for one explains the effect and reduces the need to believe in the
    other

## Bayesian Networks

* Bayesian Networks: Definition
- Aka:
  - "Bayes nets",
  - "Belief networks"
  - "Probabilistic networks"
  - "Graphical models" (somehow a broader class of statistical models)
  - "Causal networks" (arrows have special meaning)

- **Definition**: A _Bayesian network_ is a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)
  1. _Nodes_ $X_i$ correspond to random variables (discrete or continuous)
  2. _Edges_ $X \to Y$ connect nodes and represent dependencies among variables
     - E.g., $X = Parent(Y)$, $Y = Child(X)$, ancestors, spouses, ...
  3. Each node $X_i$ is associated with a _conditional probability table_ (CPT or
     CPD):
     $$
     \Pr(X_i | Parents(X_i))
     $$
     quantifying the effect of the parents on the node
     - If a node has no parents, it has a prior probability

// TODO: Add example graph

* Bayesian Network: Intuition
- **Remark**: Bayesian networks are the analogous for uncertain knowledge to
  propositional logic for definite knowledge
  - _Propositional logic_ = rigid rules, i.e., `True` or `False`
  - _Bayesian networks_ = flexible, degrees of belief
  - Replace $X \implies Y$ with $\Pr(Y | X)$

- **Example**: garden world example
  - $R$ = "It is raining"
  - $W$ = "The grass is wet"
  - Propositional logic
    - If $R \rightarrow W$ and $R$ is true, then $W$ must be true
  - Bayesian network
    - $\Pr(R = True) = 0.2$
    - $\Pr(W | R) = 0.9$
    - $\Pr(W | \neg R) = 0.1$

- **Example**: medical diagnosis
  - Propositional logic
    - _"Patient has disease $D$" $\implies$ "Patient has symptom $S$"_
  - Bayesian network
    - _"Probability of $S$ given $D$ is high, but not certain"_

* Bayesian Network and Full-joint distribution
- **Theorem**: it can be shown that _topology_ and _conditional probabilities_
  are sufficient to specify any full joint distribution, very concisely (often)

- The topology of the network (nodes and edges) specifies conditional
  independence relationships
  - E.g., $X \to Y$ means _"$X$ has a direct influence on $Y$"_, i.e., _"$X$
    relates to $Y$"_ (not necessarily _$X$ causes $Y$_)

- Nodes are:
  - Directly influenced by their parents
  - Indirectly influenced by all their ancestors

- **How to build a Bayesian Network**:
  - Domain experts can decide what relationships exist among domain variables,
    determining the topology
  - Conditional probabilities can be specified or estimated

* Conditional Probability Table
- **Definition**: _Conditional Probability Table_ (CPT) encodes the probability
  of one node $X_i$ given its parents $Parents(X_i)$
  $$
  \Pr(X_i | Parents(X_i))
  $$

- Each row of the CPT contains the conditional probability of the node under a
  conditioning case (i.e., a possible combination of the values for the parent
  nodes)

- **Note**:
  - Natural for discrete variables, but can be extended to continuous variables
  - A conditional probability table summarizes an infinite set of circumstances
    in the table

* Conditional Probability Table: Example

- Consider the events:
	- $T$ = "Traffic Jam"
	- $S$ = "Snow"
	- $R$ = "Rush Hour"

- The CPT models $\Pr(\text{Traffic Jam} | \text{Snow}, \text{Rush Hour}) = \Pr(T | S, R)$

\begingroup \scriptsize
| T 		| P(T\|S,R) | P(T\|S,-R) | P(T\|-S,R) | P(T\|-S,-R) |
|-------|----------|-----------|-----------|------------|
| True  | 0.80     | 0.10      | 0.50      | 0.05       |
| False | 0.20     | 0.90      | 0.50      | 0.95       |
\endgroup

- As for any conditional probability
  - The sum of probabilities on the left side of the conditioning is 1
  - Not on the right side

- **Interpretation**
  - Traffic is highly likely when there is both snow and rush hour
  - Traffic is unlikely when neither condition holds
