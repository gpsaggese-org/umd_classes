# Why Causal AI Matters

## What ML Systems Can and Cannot Tell You

* The Illusion of Understanding
- @Comparison@: Prediction vs. Explanation
  - Models find patterns in historical data
  - Cannot explain mechanisms or answer _"what if?"_ scenarios

- @Problem@: High accuracy $\neq$ understanding causation
  - A model with 95% churn prediction accuracy won't tell what _causes_ churn
  - Accurate sales forecasts cannot predict effects of _pricing changes_

- @Implication@: Without causation, you react to past events
  - Cannot proactively _change outcomes_

* Association, Correlation, and Causation
- @Fact@: Association $\to$ Correlation $\to$ Causation
  - Each level adds specificity about variable relationships

  - **Association** means that variables co-occur or are
    statistically dependent
    - E.g., _"People who buy diapers also buy beer"_
    - No information on direction or mechanism

  - **Correlation** is a linear relationship between variables
    - E.g., _"Ice cream sales and drowning deaths correlate"_ (both seasonal)
    - Captures strength and direction, not causation

  - **Causation** understanding that changing one variable _directly_
    changes another
    - E.g., _"Asbestos causes cancer"_
    - Requires causal model or experimental evidence

- @Key idea@: high correlation _feels like_ understanding but isn't
  - Real understanding means knowing what happens when you _intervene_

* What ML Systems Can Tell You

- @Characteristics@: Traditional ML excels at three capabilities

- **Pattern recognition**
  - Find correlations in data
  - Identify similar items (e.g., through clustering, classification)
  - Forecast based on past trends

- **Predictive accuracy** on held-out test data
  - Accurate prediction if conditions remain stable
  - Only works when training and future data match

- **Feature importance**
  - Find variables with strongest statistical associations
  - Cannot distinguish correlation from causation

* What ML Systems Cannot Tell You

- @Limitations@: Traditional ML struggles with five critical problems

- **Understanding causation** from observational data alone
  - Strong correlations can derive from confounding, reverse causation, or
    coincidence

- **Effects of interventions**
  - _"If we change X, what happens to Y?"_
  - E.g., _"If we lower prices by 10%, will revenue increase?"_
  - Observational data cannot answer without causal assumptions

- **Counterfactuals**
  - _"What would have happened with a different decision?"_
  - E.g., _"Would customer churn if we offered them a discount?"_

- **Fairness**
  - Model can be _statistically_ unbiased but _causally_ biased
  - _Statistically unbiased_: $\EE[Prediction] = \EE[True Value]$
  - _Causally biased_: model uses proxies for protected attributes
    - E.g., variables correlated to race and gender in decisions

- **Optimal decisions**
  - ML optimizes for accuracy, not business outcomes
  - E.g., 90% accurate model may lead to worse decisions than 85% accurate
    one, depending on error consequences

* Problem 1: Correlation is Not Causation!

- @Definition@: **Correlation** is statistical relationships between variables
  - Pros
    - Find patterns in past data to predict future

  - Cons
    - Cannot explain cause
    - Variables move together by coincidence or hidden factors

- @Definition@: **Causation** explains how changing one variable influences
  another
  - Cannot be concluded from correlation alone

- @Key idea@: Data **cannot distinguish** causes from effects
  - _Only humans_ identify variables and relationships from context
  - Without causation, cannot make intelligent decisions

* Correlation is Not Causation: Intuitive Examples

- @Examples@: Humans routinely confuse association with causation
  - _"You eat X, get stomachaches"_ $\to$ infer _"X is bad for you"_
  - _"Buy stock before price spikes"_ $\to$ infer _"you can time markets"_

- @Problem@: Bidirectional causality can be plausible
  - _"Does top-notch consulting improve businesses?"_ or
  - _"Do successful businesses hire top consultants?"_

* Correlation is Not Causation: Business Examples

- @Example@: **Hotel pricing paradox**
  - Prices are low when hotel is empty
  - Prices are high when demand is high and hotel full
  - Naive prediction: _"increase prices to sell more rooms"_
  - Causal reality: high prices reflect demand, not cause it

- @Example@: **Online marketplace pricing**
  - Decision: how to set prices and when to have sales
  - Causal question: _"What is impact of lowering prices on units sold?"_
  - Gain from volume must compensate for price reduction
  - Price cuts have different impacts by context
    - Business type (e.g., clothing vs drugs)
    - Timing (e.g., before Christmas)

* Problem 2: Decision Making

- @Problem@: Businesses need **decisions, not just predictions**

- @Example@: Price reduction scenario
  - _"What happens if a product price is reduced by 10%?"_
  - Will more customers buy the product?
  - Will revenue decrease? What to do then?
  - Why are customers leaving? Quality issue? Emerging competitor?

- @Examples@: Policy questions require causal reasoning
  - _"Low-sugar vs low-fat diet: which works?"_
  - _"If we increase credit lines, how does bank margin change?"_
  - _"Build a library or give tablets to boost test scores?"_

* Problem 3: ML Explainability

- @Problem@: Regulators require **defense of ML/AI decisions**
  - E.g., hiring decisions, policy setup
  - Many ML systems are black boxes
    - E.g., neural networks
    - Humans cannot understand input-to-output process
    - Cannot explain decisions to shareholders
    - Features like age, race, sex introduce bias

- @Cons@
  - Fines from regulatory violations
  - Customer backlash
  - Activist pressure

- @Solution@: **Explainable AI** enables comprehension and trust
  - Users can understand, explain, and verify model results

* Problem 4: How ML Systems Make Decisions Today

- @Workflow@: Typical ML deployment pipeline
  - Collect data $\to$ Train model $\to$ Make predictions
  - Deploy: _"If model predicts X, take action Y"_

- @Problem@: Systems optimize for wrong objectives
  - Optimize for prediction accuracy, not decision quality
  - Ignore business outcomes
  - E.g., fraud detector with 95% accuracy that blocks 50% legitimate
    transactions is a poor system

* Data Science for Decisions: Examples

- @Example@: **Recommendation systems** (Netflix, Amazon, TikTok)
  - Model predicts which items users will click based on historical clicks
  - System recommends top-N items based on predicted clicks
  - _Problem_: Cannot run counterfactual experiments offline
    - Recommending novel items has no historical data to learn from
    - Optimizing purely for clicks may harm long-term engagement
      (users get bored, echo chambers, filter bubbles)

- @Example@: **Hiring pipelines**
  - Model scores candidates
  - HR uses scores to rank applicants
  - _Problem_: Training data encodes historical hiring bias

- @Example@: **Fraud detection**
  - Model flags suspicious transactions
  - System blocks transactions automatically
  - _Problem_: High false positive rate without knowing how to improve

* Problem 5: Feedback Loops

- @Problem@: Prediction systems change the world through **feedback loops**

- @Example@: **Recommendation systems**
  - Model recommends sensational content $\to$ users click more
  - Future training data has more clicks on sensational content
  - Model recommends even more sensational content
  - Consequence: Radicalization, echo chambers, mental health issues

- @Example@: **Criminal justice relapse prediction**
  - Minorities policed more $\to$ arrested more $\to$ data biased
  - Model trained on arrest data (biased against minorities)
  - Judges use models for bail/sentencing
  - Consequence: Model becomes increasingly biased over time

- @Example@: **College admissions**
  - Model trained on past acceptance decisions (encode historical racism, sexism)
  - Model used to screen applications
  - Filter for applicants becomes biased
  - Consequence: Historical biases are perpetuated

* Problem 6: Distribution Shift

- @Problem@: Real-world distributions shift over time, breaking trained models

  - **Concept drift**: link between inputs and predictions 
    $\Pr(Y | X)$ changes
    - E.g., customer preferences change $\to$ model from 2020 becomes less accurate
      by 2023

  - **Covariate shift**: input distribution $\Pr(X)$ changes but
    relationship stays same
    - E.g., more young customers join platform $\to$ model trained on older
      customer base performs worse

  - **Label shift**: outcome distribution $\Pr(Y)$ changes
    - E.g., fraud rate increases from new tactics $\to$ model from old rate becomes
      less effective

- @Solution@: Causal models provide stability
  - Encode mechanisms, not just patterns
  - Mechanisms are more stable across distribution shifts
  - Understand _"why"_ to predict in new contexts

## Optimization vs. Inference vs. Decision Theory

* Optimization vs. Inference vs. Decision Theory

- @Comparison@: traditional machine learning conflates three paradigms

  - **Statistical inference** estimates parameters from data
    - Learns from observations given model structure
    - E.g., estimate treatment effects, compute confidence intervals
    - _Tools_: regression, Bayesian estimation, hypothesis testing

  - **Optimization** finds best action given a _known_ objective
    - Assumes world behavior is known
    - E.g., minimize cost, maximize throughput, tune parameters
    - _Tools_: gradient descent, linear programming, evolutionary algorithms

  - **Decision theory** chooses actions under _uncertainty_
    - E.g., _"Launch product with uncertain demand?"_
    - Requires reasoning about interventions and outcomes
    - _Tools_: utility functions, expected value of information, causal models

* Where ML Falls Short

- @Problem@: Traditional ML focuses on **inference and optimization** only

- @Key idea@: Real business problems require **decision theory**
  - Must act under uncertainty with causal knowledge

- @Solution@: **Causal probabilistic AI** inference and decision theory
  - _Inference_: what the world looks like
  - _Causal models_: what happens when you act
  - _Decision theory_: which action to choose

## The Cost of Ignoring Causality

* The Cost of Ignoring Causality (1/2)

- @Problem@: Wrong decisions and wasted resources occur when organizations
  ignore causality

- @Example@: **Simpson's Paradox**
  - Aggregate data shows opposite trend from subgroups
  - E.g., Berkeley admissions show overall bias, but no bias within
    departments
  - _Solution_: Condition on right variables using DAG

- @Example@: **Confounding bias**
  - Hidden variable drives both treatment and outcome
  - E.g., ice cream sales and drowning deaths both rise in summer
  - _Solution_: Identify and control for confounders

- @Example@: **Collider bias**
  - Conditioning on common effect creates false associations
  - E.g., in hospitalized patients, unrelated diseases appear correlated
    (both cause hospitalization)
  - _Solution_: Do not condition on colliders

* The Cost of Ignoring Causality (2/2)

- @Example@: **Feedback loop amplification**
  - Biased predictions reinforce bias in future training data
  - E.g., more police to minorities $\to$ more arrests $\to$ model learns
    neighborhoods dangerous $\to$ send more police
  - _Solution_: Model feedback loops explicitly

- @Example@: **Intervention backfire**
  - Acting on correlation leads to wrong actions
  - E.g., hospitals with more staff have higher mortality rates
    - Confounded by patient severity: sicker patients need more staff
  - Naive intervention (_"reduce staff to lower mortality"_) would be
    catastrophic
  - _Solution_: Distinguish correlation from causal effect before acting

* Causal AI

- @Capabilities@: Causal AI unlocks five key abilities

- **Understand the why**
  - Determines cause-and-effect between variables
  - Example: _"Did marketing campaign increase sales?"_

- **Identify interventions**
  - Identifies variables and interventions to change outcomes
  - Example: _"Which lifestyle changes reduce blood pressure?"_

- **Predict counterfactuals**
  - Hypothesize outcomes under different circumstances
  - Example: student grades if they attended a different school

- **Avoid bias**
  - Ensure fairness beyond training data biases
  - Account for confounding variables explicitly

- **Improve decisions**
  - Understand relationships for better choices
  - Example: improve supply chain by understanding impact of decisions on logistics

* Causal AI vs Traditional AI

- @Comparison@: **Current AI** focuses on correlation

- Uses correlation to:
  - Analyze data
  - Identify patterns
  - Make predictions
  - Model quality depends on data quality (biased or unclean data $\to$ poor model)

- @Comparison@: **Causal AI** infers causation from association

- Science of inferring causation from association
- Understanding when and why causation and association differ
- Understand cause-and-effect relationships to intervene for desired effect

- @Quote@: _"The next revolution of data science is the science of
  interpreting reality, not of summarizing data"_ (Judea Pearl, 2021)

# Causal AI Fundamentals

## The Ladder of Causation

* The Ladder of Causation

- @Definition@: Pearl's 3-level framework for understanding causality
  1. Association
  2. Intervention
  3. Counterfactuals

\begingroup \scriptsize
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("Level", "Symbol", "Activity", "Questions"),
  rows: (
    ("1. Association", "$Pr(Y | X)$", "Observing", "What is?"),
    ("2. Intervention", "$Pr(Y | do(X), Z)$", "Intervening", "What if?"),
    ("3. Counterfactuals", "$Pr(Y_X | x', y')$", "Imagining", "Why?"),
  ),
)
```
\endgroup

* Rung 1: Association

- @Question@: _"How would seeing $X$ change your belief in $Y$?"_

- @Definition@: $\Pr(Y|X)$

- @Activity@: Passive observation to determine if $X, Y$ relate
  - Traditional AI/ML based on this rung only

- @Examples@
  - _"Trees have green leaves in spring"_
  - _"What does a symptom tell you about disease?"_
  - _"What does a survey tell you about elections?"_

* Rung 2: Intervention

- @Question@: _"What happens to $Y$ if you do $X$?"_

- @Definition@: $\Pr(Y | do(X), Z)$

- @Activity@: Understand impact of action $X$ on $Y$ under conditions $Z$
  - Requires causal model, not just observations

- @Examples@
  - _"Spring makes leaves turn green"_
  - _"Did headache disappear from pain reliever or food?"_
  - _"If I take aspirin, will headache cure?"_
  - _"If we ban sodas, what happens?"_

* Rung 3: Counterfactuals

- @Question@: _"Was $X$ that caused $Y$?"_

- @Definition@: $\Pr(Y_X | x', y')$

- @Activity@: Imagine what would happen if facts were different
  - Highest form of reasoning—requires understanding cause-and-effect

- @Examples@
  - _"What if child received adult drug dose?"_
  - _"What would jury conclude?"_
  - _"Why did marketing campaign fail?"_

## Correlation vs Causation Models

* Correlation vs Causation Model

- @Definition@: **Correlation** describes how variables relate
- @Definition@: **Causality** describes whether one variable causes another

- Both accept inputs, transform, compute predictions
- **Correlation-based AI**: works with abundant historical data
- **Causal-based AI**: build business model first, then integrate data

* Correlation-Based Model Process

- @Approach@: **"Data first"** paradigm
  - More data = better

- @Workflow@: Standard ML pipeline
  - Acquire data
  - Integrate and clean data
  - Exploratory data analysis (EDA)
  - Feature engineering
  - Build and test models
  - Deploy models in production

- @Cons@: Common failures of correlation-based AI
  - Organizational issues in team structure
  - Opaque models, lack explainability
  - Spurious correlations mistaken for signals
  - Never articulate business goal

* Causation-based Model Process

- @Approach@: **"Model first"** paradigm
  - Understand business question before collecting data

- @Workflow@: Causal AI pipeline
  - What is the intended outcome?
  - What is the proposed intervention?
  - What are confounding and effecting factors?
  - Create model diagram/graph
  - Data acquisition and validation

## Data Science vs. Decision Science

* From Data Science to Decision Science

- @Comparison@: Data science and Decision science are **two different paradigms**

\begingroup \footnotesize
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("Dimension", "Data Science", "Decision Science"),
  rows: (
    ("Goal", "Predict outcomes", "Choose best actions"),
    ("Core question", "What will happen?", "What should we do?"),
    ("Method", "Statistical models, ML", "Causal models, decision theory"),
    ("Output", "Score, forecast, cluster", "Recommended action + outcome"),
    ("Success metric", "Accuracy, AUC, RMSE", "Business outcome, ROI, impact"),
    ("Data requirement", "Historical observations", "Observations + causal structure"),
  ),
)
```
\endgroup

- @Key idea@: Gap between prediction and decision is _causal reasoning_
  - Organizations have data science teams doing prediction
  - Business stakeholders need decision science

- @Problem@: Machine learning excels at prediction but fails at decisions
  - Excels at prediction
  - Fails at "what-if" questions
  - Fails for decisions involving interventions
  - Association alone cannot support decisions
  - Models can be useless or harmful without causal reasoning

* Causal Questions vs. Predictive Questions

- @Comparison@: Two classes of questions drive different methods

\begingroup \footnotesize
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("Predictive Question", "Causal Question"),
  rows: (
    ("Which patients will be readmitted?", "Which intervention reduces readmission?"),
    ("Which customers will churn?", "What action prevents churn?"),
    ("What will sales be next quarter?", "How would a 10% price cut affect sales?"),
    ("Which students will fail the exam?", "Does tutoring improve exam scores?"),
    ("Which transactions are fraudulent?", "What policy changes reduce fraud?"),
  ),
)
```
\endgroup

- @Approach@: **Predictive questions** use standard ML
  - Observational data only
  - Useful for _monitoring_ and _alerting_

- @Approach@: **Causal questions** require causal models or experiments
  - Answer "what if" and "why"
  - Useful for _decisions_ and _policy design_

* A Roadmap From Prediction to Decision Intelligence

- @Definition@: **Level 0: Descriptive**
  - Question: _"What happened?"_
  - Tools: Dashboards, reports, statistics (backward-looking)

- @Definition@: **Level 1: Predictive**
  - Question: _"What will happen?"_
  - Tools: ML models, forecasting (assumes stable world)

- @Definition@: **Level 2: Causal**
  - Question: _"Why?"_ and _"What if we intervene?"_
  - Tools: DAGs, do-calculus (understand mechanisms)

- @Definition@: **Level 3: Decision**
  - Question: _"What should we do?"_
  - Tools: Causal models + decision theory + business goals

- @Key idea@: Most organizations stuck at Level 1
  - Causal AI enables jump to Levels 2 and 3 for competitive advantage

# Causal AI in Business

## Business Context and Motivation

* Digital Transformation in Business

- @Definition@: **Digital transformation** uses digital technologies to reduce
  costs and improve revenues

- @Techniques@: Five key approaches
  - **Integration**: Embed digital tools (AI, cloud, IoT, automation)
  - **Cultural change**: Encourage innovation, agility, data-driven mindset
  - **Customer-centric**: Use digital solutions for engagement and satisfaction
  - **Process automation**: Streamline operations through AI, robots, analytics
  - **Data-driven decisions**: Leverage big data, ML, real-time analytics

* Why AI Projects Fail?

- @Problem@: AI projects approach problems only from ML perspective
  - Data scientists focus on accuracy and technical metrics
  - Work in isolation from domain experts, business users, data engineers
  - Lack of problem context leads to irrelevant solutions

- @Limitations@: Five common failure modes
  - **Misalignment**: Models solve wrong problem or answer irrelevant questions
  - **Black-box models**: Difficult to interpret or explain to stakeholders
  - **Data issues**: Poor quality, missing, or biased data
  - **Deployment challenges**: Not easily integrated into production systems
  - **Lack of monitoring**: No process for tracking performance post-deployment

* How to Make AI Project Succeed

- @Techniques@: Four success factors

1. **Create a hybrid team**
   - Represents all business aspects
   - Uses collaborative framework
   - Communicates with single language (e.g., DAGs)

2. **Meet regularly** to ensure continuity

3. **Find an executive sponsor**
   - Understands project goals and potential

4. **Initial pilot**
   - Small team for targeted problem
   - Demonstrates AI approach merit

* Step 1: What Are the Intended Outcomes?

- @Problem@: Most AI projects (even 90%) fail from misalignment between
  business goals and project

- @Questions@: Key questions to answer
  - What process are you analyzing?
  - What happens if a course of action is taken or not?
  - Outcomes: positive, negative, unacceptable, optimal?
  - What related data can be combined and leveraged?
  - What are possible and feasible interventions?
  - Confounding factors correlated with outcomes and treatments?

* Step 2: What Are the Proposed Interventions?

- @Definition@: **Levers** are variables to achieve business outcomes

- @Example@: Customer marketing for a product
  - Introduce a new product?
  - Bundle multiple products (improve sales or inhibit long-term)?
  - Focus advertisement on quality vs other options?
  - Add more variations of same product?
  - Discontinue or divest product line?

* Step 3: What Factors Create Changes?

- @Definition@: **Identify confounders** that obscure or distort relationships
  - Confounders mute or inflate relationship strength

- @Definition@: **Direct effect**: Effect through intervention pathway

- @Definition@: **Indirect effect**: Effect by environment or intervention
  byproduct

- @Definition@: **Total causal effect**: All factors modifying outcome

* Step 4: Build Causal DAG

- @Definition@: **Causal models** simplify systems without losing key
  relationships

- **Visual models** abstract complexity into interpretable formats
  - Highlight direction and strength of influence
  - Aid communication between stakeholders
  - Reduce cognitive overload by excluding irrelevant details
  - Guide data collection by identifying impactful variables

- @Key idea@: **Balance** is critical
  - Too much simplicity loses insight
  - Too much complexity loses clarity

  - Increase price by how much?
  - Decrease price by how much?
  - Change price one-time or over time?
  - Develop individual pricing model for each customer?

* Step 5: Data Acquisition and Integration

- @Workflow@: Leverage existing data and collect new data
  - Use data collected for correlation-based ML
  - Collect data specifically for causal AI objectives

- **Prepare data** to match refined causal model
  - Clean, normalize, align data with model assumptions
  - Map observed variables to DAG nodes
  - Encode categorical variables
  - Handle missing data (imputation or exclusion)
  - Normalize or scale values

- **Control for bias and confounding**
  - Goal: Ensure data structure supports causal estimation

* Step 6: Model Modification

- @Workflow@: Refine initial DAG design
  - Reduce bias and improve reliability
  - Clarify variables as confounders, mediators, or outcomes

- @Cons@: Avoid common pitfalls
  - Do not control for mediators or colliders
  - Control for direct and indirect confounders

- **Test models** against technical and business objectives
  - Isolate causal effects with necessary controls
  - Ensure assumptions align with data and domain logic

* Step 7: Preparing for Deployment in Business

- @Workflow@: Transition from experimentation to decision-making
  - Validate model against real-world business outcomes
  - Ensure stakeholders understand and trust causal logic

- **Develop user-friendly interfaces** for business users

## Explainability and Interpretability

* The Importance of Explainability

- @Question@: Can we understand how AI models make decisions?
  - How do ML models make decisions?
  - Can they be trusted?
  - Are they biased?

- @Problem@: AI systems must foster trust, transparency, and user confidence
  - Loss of trust, regulation violations, fines, lawsuits
  - Example: false negative in medical screening for cancer

- @Solution@: Humans in the loop
  - Ensure interpretation and context are considered

* Techniques for Interpretability

- @Definition@: **LIME** (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations)
  - Focus on single prediction
  - Approximate model locally with interpretable model
  - Perturb input data, observe prediction changes

- @Definition@: **PDP** (Partial Dependence Plots)
  - Show marginal effect of features on predicted outcome

- @Definition@: **ICE** (Individual Conditional Expectation)
  - Show feature-prediction relationship for individual instances

- @Definition@: **SHAP** (SHapley Additive exPlanations)
  - Quantify feature contribution to specific prediction

* Causal AI in Interpretable AI

- @Advantages@: Causal AI enables deeper interpretability
  - DAGs present variables, relationships, strengths clearly
  - Counterfactuals predict action outcomes
  - Remove confounders to prevent skewed estimates
  - Humans understand output (experts and non-experts both)
  - Hybrid teams enhance context, reduce blind spots

* The Future of Causal AI

- @Characteristics@: Causal AI evolution
  - Moves from academia to commercial use
  - Departs from purely data-driven systems
  - Reflects human thinking and decision-making

- @Convergence@: Multiple AI paradigms merge
  - Causal AI
  - Traditional AI
  - Deep learning
  - Generative techniques

* Why Probabilistic Machine Learning Matters

- **Question**: When is knowing _how confident_ a model is more important than the prediction itself?
  - Medical diagnosis: A classifier that says "cancer" needs to know how sure it is
  - Autonomous driving: When should the car defer to human control?
  - Financial risk: Which decisions warrant caution despite high confidence?

- **Key advantage**: Probabilistic models output full distributions, not point estimates
  - Standard ML: "The diagnosis is pneumonia"
  - Probabilistic ML: "87% pneumonia, 13% flu, plus credible intervals for each"

- **Why it matters in practice**
  - Uncertainty quantification drives better decisions under risk
  - Limited or noisy data is handled naturally, not forced into deterministic assumptions
  - Priors encode domain expertise formally, crucial when data is expensive or scarce

- **Intuition**: Probability embraces what we _don't_ know, treating it as information rather than failure


* Probabilistic ML: Robustness Through Uncertainty

- **Problem**: Deterministic models fail silently
  - Overfit to small datasets (commit to one "best" explanation)
  - Produce overconfident predictions on out-of-distribution data
  - Struggle with missing values (requires imputation or row deletion)

- **Solution**: Probabilistic frameworks solve these naturally
  - **Uncertainty propagation**: Bayesian model averaging marginalizes over plausible explanations, building in Occam's razor
    - Naturally penalizes unnecessary complexity
    - Confidence grows only when data strongly supports a model
  - **Principled handling of incomplete data**: Integrate over unknown values instead of guessing or dropping rows
  - **Composability**: Chain probabilistic models (graphical models, hierarchical Bayes) and reason about how uncertainty flows through pipelines
    - Deterministic models bolted together hide how errors accumulate

- **Key insight**: Probability isn't just about predictions—it's about decision-making under uncertainty
  - When to act, when to wait for more data, when to defer to humans

* Why Explainability Matters

- **Problem**: Modern AI systems are powerful but opaque
  - Deep neural networks with millions of parameters are hard to interpret
  - Business logic becomes implicit rather than explicit
  - Regulators and stakeholders demand accountability

- **Question**: When do we need explanations?
  - High-stakes decisions: healthcare diagnosis, loan approval, criminal justice
  - Safety-critical systems: autonomous vehicles, aircraft control
  - Building trust: users and decision-makers need to verify model behavior

- **Example**: Medical diagnosis
  - Model predicts patient has 85% likelihood of disease X
  - Question: Which symptoms drove this prediction?
  - Without explanation: doctor cannot assess clinical validity
  - With explanation: doctor can override or accept the prediction

- **Key idea**: Explainability enables accountability and builds trust

* Approaches to Explainability

- **Definition**: Explainability is the degree to which we can understand why a
  model made a particular prediction
  - Global explanation: _"How does the model work overall?"_
  - Local explanation: _"Why did the model predict this specific outcome?"_
  - Feature importance: _"Which input features most influence the prediction?"_

- **Main approaches**:
  1. Intrinsic methods: Design inherently interpretable models
     - Decision trees, linear regression, rule-based systems
     - Transparent structure allows direct inspection
  2. Post-hoc methods: Explain predictions after training
     - Feature attribution (LIME, SHAP, integrated gradients)
     - Attention mechanisms in neural networks
     - Counterfactual explanations: _"What would need to change for a different
       prediction?"_

- **Example**: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)
  - Computes each feature's contribution to moving the prediction from baseline
    to actual
  - Assigns a value to each feature showing how much it pushed the decision
  - Enables ranking features by importance and visualizing their impact

- **Remark**: Trade-off between model performance and explainability
  - Simpler models are easier to explain but may have lower accuracy
  - Complex models achieve better performance but require post-hoc explanation
    techniques
  - Choice depends on the application's requirements for accuracy vs
    interpretability
