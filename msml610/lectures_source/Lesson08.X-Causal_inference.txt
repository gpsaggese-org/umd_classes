* Lending Industry: Problem Setup

- @Example@: banking and lending industry
  - Business goal: offer more credit to customers with lower default risk
  - What is the causal impact of credit limits on default?

- @Intuition@: theory suggests higher credit limits increase default risk
  - More available credit $\to$ higher spending $\to$ higher balances $\to$ more
    interest paid $\to$ harder to repay debt

- @Data@: bank historical data shows unexpected pattern
  - Customers with higher credit limits have _lower_ default rates
  - Customers with lower credit limits have _higher_ default rates
  - Negative correlation between credit limit and default

- @Problem@: this contradicts theory
  - _Naive interpretation_ (association ML)
    - Increase credit limits to reduce defaults
    - Give riskiest customers the most credit
  - This would be disastrous in practice

- @Question@: why does observed data contradict causal intuition?

* Lending Industry: Confounding

- @Reality@: the bank's underwriting process introduces selection bias
  - Bank estimates customer risk (hard to measure) using proxy variable $X$
    (e.g., credit score, income, employment history)

- @Causal mechanism@:
  - Risk $\to$ bank's credit decision
  - Risk $\to$ default rate
  - Bank gives high limits to _low-risk_ customers only

- @Result@: the negative correlation is _spurious_
  - Observed correlation is driven by who _got_ the high limits
  - Not by the causal effect of limits on default
  - Confounded by unobserved or imperfectly measured risk

* Lending Industry: The Causal Graph

- @Components@:
  - _Risk_: true underlying customer risk (latent)
  - _Proxy_: observed proxy (credit score, income)
  - _CreditLimit_: decision made by bank
  - _Default_: outcome we want to predict

* Lending Industry: Key Insight

- @Recap@: **selection bias** occurs when the treatment assignment itself depends
  on the confounder
  - Bank's decision to increase limits depends on risk assessment
  - This creates spurious correlation in the observed data

- @Lesson@: correlation $\neq$ causation when confounders present
  - Negative observed correlation hides positive true causal effect
  - A decision based on observed data would backfire

- @Solution@: recover causal effect using
  1. Randomized experiments (e.g., random limit assignments)
  2. Causal inference adjusting for confounding (e.g., regression)
  3. Causal graphs to identify confounding paths and use adjustment formula

* Lending Industry: Adjustment Formula
- @Assumption@: assume conditional independence $(Y_0, Y_1) \perp T | X$, use
  the adjustment formula
  $$
  ATE = \EE_X [
    \EE[Y | T=1, X=x] - \EE[Y | T=0, X=x]
  ]
  $$

- @Problem@
  - Need partition data into segments defined by feature $X$
  - If $X$ has many values (e.g., a large vector of variables)
    - Need data points with same characteristics and different treatments
    - Face curse of dimensionality (sparsity issues)

- @Solution@
  - Model potential outcome $Y$ as linear regression on $X$
    - Require treatment and control to be comparable "on average"
    - Functions as dimensionality reduction algorithm

* Using Regression to Estimate Causal Effects

- @Thesis@: Under appropriate hypotheses (e.g., randomization, all relevant
  confounders are known), **linear regression** solves many problems of
  causal inference:
  - Estimates treatment effects (e.g., ATE)
  - Matches adjustment formula from causal theory
  - Has causal interpretation (not only association)
  - Computes statistics (e.g., confidence intervals, p-values)

- @Hypothesis@: **Confounders are known and observed**:
  - Include them as additional regressors (predictors) in your model
  - The model "adjusts for" their influence, isolating the causal effect of
    treatment from their distorting effects
  - E.g., estimating wage effect of job training, adjusted for education level (a
    confounder)

- @Hypothesis@: **Randomized Experiments**
  - Random assignment guarantees treatment isn't correlated with confounders
  - No adjustment needed: a simple regression of outcome on treatment alone gives
    unbiased causal estimate
  - Confounders balance themselves across treatment groups by chance

* Regression Theory
- @Definition@: in a **linear regression problem**, the optimal vector $\beta^*$
  minimizes mean squared error:
  $$
  \beta^* = \argmin_{\beta} \EE[(Y - \beta^T X)^2]
  $$
  - Optimal vector is estimated as $\hat{\beta} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T Y$ (normal
  equations)

- @Comparison@:
  - **Single variable regression** estimates how treatment and outcome move together,
    scaling by variance of treatment

  - **Multivariate regression** control for ("regress against") confounders and
    extract residual
    - Each coefficient is the regressor's coefficient accounting for other
      variables

- @Limitations@: **extrapolating** model outside data region is risky since we
  don't know what happens without data points

* FWL Theorem
- @Theorem@: **Frisch–Waugh–Lovell** (FWL) theorem explains how to interpret
  coefficients in a multiple regression by "partialling out" other variables
  (i.e., removing the effect of other variables on both outcome and predictors)

- @Example@
  - Consider $y = X_1 \beta_1 + X_2 \beta_2 + \varepsilon$
  - The coefficient $\hat{\beta}_1$ from the full multivariate regression is
    equal to the coefficient obtained by:
    1. Regressing $y$ on $X_2$, getting residuals $r_y$ (i.e., remove part of $y$
       explained by $X_2$)
    2. Regressing each column of $X_1$ on $X_2$, getting residuals $r_{x_1}$
       (i.e., remove part of $X_1$ explained by $X_2$)
    3. Regressing $r_y$ on $r_{x_1}$

- @Intuition@
  - $\beta_1$ measures the effect of $X_1$ on $y$, holding $X_2$ constant

* FWL Theorem for Causal Inference
- @Fact@: FWL theorem makes non-experimental data $T \to Y$ look as if the
  treatment was randomized

- @Algorithm@: Estimating a multi-variate regression with FWL theorem is
  equivalent to the 3 steps:
  1. **Denoising**: Regress outcome $Y$ on the confounder variables to obtain the
     outcome residuals $\tilde{Y}$
  2. **Debiasing**: Regress the treatment $T$ on confounders $X$ to obtain the
     treatment residuals $\tilde{T}$
     - Version of the treatment that is uncorrelated with the variables used in
       debiasing the model
  3. **Regresssion**: Regress outcome residuals $\tilde{Y}$ on treatment
     residuals $\tilde{T}$ to obtain estimate of causal effect of $T$ on $Y$

- @Example@: We want to estimate the causal effect of _credit limit_ on _credit card
  spend_, using _wage_ as (only) confounder

- @Extension@: FWL Theorem and regression applies also to cases where the
  relationship is not linear
  - E.g., the effect of credit lines on spend diminishes as you increase credit
    lines
  - You can apply some transform to $X$ (e.g., square root, log)
  - Then you can apply the usual flow (adding confounders, FWL theorem)

* Conditionally Random Experiments

- @Definition@: **Conditionally Random Experiments** (aka stratified random
  experiments) create "local experiments" from different groups based on
  covariates (not across everyone)

- @Example@: Sort customers by credit risk (covariate)
  - _High-risk_ group: randomize credit lines from low average distribution
  - _Neutral_ group: randomize credit lines from medium average distribution
  - _Low-risk_ group: randomize credit lines from high average distribution

- @Characteristics@
  - best alternative to a randomized controlled trial
  - Cheaper than completely random experiments
  - More complex than completely random experiments
  - Useful when treatment effects might differ by group (heterogeneous effects)

- @Techniques@:
  - Linear regression with **one-hot encoding** (dummy variables)
    - Model estimates _one intercept per group_
    - Shared slope across all gropus is is _variance-weighted average_ of
      regression slopes within each group

  - **Saturated regression model**
    - Includes interaction terms between treatment and dummy variables
    - Enables estimation of _separate effects for each group_

* Which Controls to Include in the Regression?
- @Definition@: **controls** are variables we include in the model to account for
  confounding or reduce noise in the estimate

- @Problem@: _What controls to include_ in the regression model?
  - Some variables are confounders and must include to eliminate bias
  - Some variables should not be included (e.g., the ones that open backdoor
    paths)
  - Some variables don't cause either treatment or outcome

- @Key insight@: Regression coefficient $\hat{\beta}$ stays same regardless of
  controls
  - Some variables can induce or reduce noise $\to$ reduce or increase precision
    / standard error

* Noise-Reducing vs Noise-Inducing Controls

- @Definition@: **Noise-Reducing Controls** are variables that _predict the
  outcome well_ but aren't confounders
  - _Example_: `default ~ credit_limit + credit_score + account_age`
    - `credit_score`: confounder (must include)
    - `account_age`: predicts `default` but not `credit_limit` (the treatment)
  - _Effect_: Reduces variance in $\varepsilon_i$ through FWL denoising step
  - _Result_: Same $\hat{\beta}$, but:
    - Standard error shrinks (tighter confidence intervals)
    - More statistical power
  - _Strategy_: Always include strong outcome predictors

- @Definition@: **Noise-Inducing Controls** are variables that _predict the
  treatment well_ but not the outcome
  - _Example_: Conditionally random credit-limit experiment (`* Conditionally
    Random Experiments` above)
    - `lottery_draw`: random number used to set the limit within each risk
      stratum — drives `credit_limit` by design, but $\perp$ `default`
  - _Effect_: Reduces treatment variance by explaining away $\text{Var}(T)$
  - _Result_: Same $\hat{\beta}$, but:
    - Standard error increases (wider confidence intervals)
    - Less statistical power
  - _Strategy_: Avoid including strong treatment predictors

- @Rule of thumb@: does candidate control predict $Y$ or $T$?
  - Predicts $Y$, not $T$ $\to$ include (tighter estimate)
  - Predicts $T$, not $Y$ $\to$ exclude (unless needed for identification)
  - Most real variables mix both roles to some degree (see next section)

* Feature Selection Dilemma

- @Problem@: Most variables are imperfect confounders
  - Strong cause of $T$, weak cause of $Y$ (like noise-inducing)
  - Strong cause of $Y$, weak cause of $T$ (like noise-reducing)

- @Trade-off@: Including all confounders decreases bias but increases variance
  $$\text{MSE} = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance}$$

- @Question@: Is a small amount of bias worth a large reduction in variance?
  - No universal answer, it depends on context, sample size, and precision needs
  - Research shows it's often worth accepting small bias for large variance
    reduction

# Propensity score

* Example: The problem of non-compliance
- @Problem@: In tech companies often talented individual contributors (ICs)
  transition to management tracks (e.g., to increase salary)
  - This transition often requires very different skill sets
  - High negative cost for the new managers and their teams

- @Solution@: Tech companies
  - Invest in manager _training programs for new managers_ to ease this
    transition
  - Select managers randomly for the training program to measure its
    effectiveness
  - Compare employee engagement scores between teams whose managers received
    training vs those who didn't
  - Estimate the average treatment effect (ATE)

- @Example@
  - Some assigned managers don't show up for training
  - Other managers obtained the training without being assigned to it
  - The study is likely biased upward because managers with already-engaged teams
    are more likely to self-select into training

* Non-compliance

- @Definition@: **non compliance** is when people do not receives the treatment
  as assigned

- @Consequences@:
  - Non-compliance introduces bias into the causal estimates
  - Requires adjustment for confounders

* Propensity Score
- @Definition@: **propensity score** is the conditional probability of a unit $x$
  to receive treatment given covariates:
  $$
  e(x) = \Pr(T=1 | X=x)
  $$

- @Intuition@
  - Propensity score can be used as dimensionality reduction technique
  - Instead of conditioning on high-dimensional $X$, condition on single
    propensity score

- @Property@
  - If two managers have same propensity score but different treatment status,
    difference is due to chance
  - Treatment should become "as good as random" after controlling for propensity
    score

* Propensity Score Estimation
- Can use ML models to estimate propensity score
  - Need out-of-fold predictions to avoid overfitting bias

- **Example**: binary treatment
  - Use logistic regression to estimate $e(x)$ from covariates $X$
  - Replace true (unknown) propensity score with estimated version
  - Apply in observational data where assignment mechanism unknown

* FWL Orthogonalization and Propensity Score
- Both FWL approach and propensity score orthogonalize treatment but use
  different models for $T$
  - FWL theorem: linear regression models $$\EE[T | X]$$ in debiasing step
    - Residualize both $T$ and $X$
    - Low-dim $X$, linear T-X relationship
  - Propensity score estimation achieves similar orthogonalization
    - Include propensity score $\hat{e}(X)$ as control in regression
    - E.g., `engagement_score ~ intervention + propensity_score`
    - High-dim $X$, flexible T-X relationship

* Propensity score matching
- Matches treated and control units based on propensity score similarity
- Essentially 1-Nearest Neighbors (i.e., KNN with K=1) on propensity score
  - Fit KNN on treated units to impute $Y_1$ for controls
  - Fit KNN on control units to impute $Y_0$ for treated
- Matched value is outcome of nearest unit
  $$
  \hat{ATE}
  = \frac{1}{N}\sum (
    ( {Y_{i} - Y_{jm}(i)} )T_{i} + 
    ( {Y_{jm}(i) - Y_{i}} )( 1 - T_{i} )
  )
  $$

- **Cons**
  - KNN inefficient in high dimensions
  - Difficult to derive variance

* Inverse Propensity Weighting (IPW)
- **Core Principle**
  - Re-weight data by inverse probability of observed treatment
  - Create pseudo-population where treatment appears randomly assigned

- For treated units:
  - Up-weight those unlikely to be treated (low $e(x)$)
  - They're informative, they look like untreated units but got treatment anyway,
    so they reveal what would happen to
- For control units:
  - Up-weight those likely to be treated (high $e(x)$)
  - Down-weights common treatment cases: Treated units with high propensity
    (likely to be treated) get lower weight, since they're less informative.

  $$E[Y_t] = \EE[ \frac{\mathbf{1}(T=t)Y}{P(T=t|X)}]$$

- The same scaling can be applied to recover the ATE:
  $$
  ATE
  = \EE\left[
      \frac{\mathbb{1}(T=1)Y}{\Pr(T=1|X)}
    \right]
    - \EE\left[
      \frac{\mathbb{1}(T=0)Y}{\Pr(T=0|X)}
      \right]
  $$

- IPW is similar to importance weighting

- To estimate the variance use bootstrap method (resample with replacement
  multiple times)
  - Fit IPW estimator on each sample
  - Calculate 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles for 95% CI

* Stabilized Propensity Weights

- **Problems**
  - Raw inverse propensity weights can be very large
  - Treatment with low probability $\Pr(T | X)$ creates extreme weights
  - Computational stability issue

- **Solution**: Stabilized propensity weights
  $$
  w = \frac{\Pr(T = t)}{\Pr(T = t | X)}
  $$
  - Balances small denominator with also small numerator
  - Produces more stable weights without changing balancing properties
  - Sum of weights in each group matches original group sizes

- Trim propensity scores to avoid extreme weights
  - E.g., keep between 1% and 99%
  - Trade-off: slight bias increase for substantial variance reduction

- **Visual intuition**
  - Plot propensity score distributions by treatment status
  - Before weighting: distributions shifted (confounding)
  - After weighting: distributions overlap (treatment appears random)
  - Overlapping distributions essential for IPW validity

* Propensity Score for Selection Bias
- IPW originally developed (Horvitz-Thompson 1952) for selection bias
  - Can adjust for non-response in surveys
  - Can handle both confounding and selection together

- **Example**
  - You send a survey asking people to rate your product on a 1-to-5 scale
  - Survey respondents may differ from non-respondents
    - E.g., non-respondents are often unsatisfied customers and vice versa
  - Estimate probability of responding given customer's covariates (e.g., 
    age, income, app usage) $\Pr(R = 1 | X)$
  - Re-weight respondents by $1/\hat{\Pr}(R = 1)$ to account for who didn't
    respond
  - Creates pseudo-population representing full population

- You can also compute the weights for both selection and confounding by
  multiplying the re-weighting and can stability using the marginal probability
  $$
  W = \frac{
    \hat{\Pr}(T = t)
  }{
    \hat{\Pr}(R = 1 | X)\hat{\Pr}(T = t | X)
  }
  $$

* Positivity
- **Positivity** means that every unit has non-zero probability of receiving each
  treatment level
  $$
  \Pr(T = t | X) > 0 \text{ for all } t, X
  $$
- Essential for IPW validity

- **Problems**
  - Some subgroups have almost no treated or control units
  - Cannot estimate counterfactual outcomes in those regions
  - IPW fails when positivity violated: cannot reweight units that don't exist

### IPW Sensitivity
- Propensity score distribution easy to visualize
- Can directly check if treatment groups overlap

### Comparison with Regression
- Regression can extrapolate beyond region with data
- Assumes linear (or specified) functional form for $E[Y | T, X]$
- Can recover ATE even with positivity violation if model specification correct
- IPW makes no functional form assumptions → fails without positivity

### Positivity-Bias Trade-Off
- More covariates in propensity model $\to$
  - Stronger unconfoundedness / conditional independence assumption
  - Weaker positivity
- Fewer covariates
  - Better positivity
  - More potential confounding bias
- Balance is contextual and requires judgment

# Effect heterogeneity

* From ATE to CATE
- Average Treatment Effect (ATE) $\EE[Y_1 - Y_0]$ answers:
  - _"Should we roll out a treatment to entire population?"_
- Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE) $\EE[Y_1 - Y_0 | X]$ answers
  - _"Which units should we treat?"_
  - _"What's the optimal treatment for each unit?"_
  - Enables personalization, since each unit is different in terms of its
    covariate $X$

* CATE
- You have customers and a treatment
  - $Y$ = outcome (purchase amount, satisfaction, churn probability, etc.)
  - $T$ = treatment (discount, advertisement exposure, recommendation, pricing
    change, etc.)
- You want to personalize the treatment (e.g., different discounts to different
  customers)

- It's like customer segmentation: create groups of customers based on how they
  respond to your treatment
  - E.g., some customers reacts well to discounts, others poorly

- We want to measure "customer’s response to a treatment" (aka treatment effect or
  marginal effect):
  $$\frac{\delta Y}{\delta T}\Big|_{X=x}$$
  how the outcome Y changes when the treatment T changes slightly,
  holding other factors fixed

- High / medium / low treatment effect (group with high slope)

* Why Prediction Is Not the Answer
- Predictive models estimate outcome $Y = f(X, T)$, not treatment sensitivity
  $$\frac{\delta Y}{\delta T}\Big|_{X=x}$$
  - Partitioning units by $Y$ does not partition by treatment effect
  - Example: high sales prediction does not guarantee high discount sensitivity
- Treatment slopes are non-observable at unit level
  - Fundamental problem: cannot observe same unit under multiple treatment
    conditions
  - Therefore cannot directly compute treatment sensitivity and ML can't predict
    it

* Restaurant Discount: Example
- A chain of restaurants that operate across the country. A key component of this
  business is 
- Understanding when it should give discounts to its customers
- The company ran a nationwide experiment that lasted three years, where it
  randomized discounts in six different restaurants in the chain
  - The unit of treatment is (day, restaurant)

- Linear regression with treatment-covariate interactions estimates CATE
  - Model:
    $$
    y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t_i + \beta_2 X_i + \beta_3 t_i X_i + e_i
    $$
  - Slope prediction:
    $$
    \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial t_i} = \beta_1 + \beta_3 X_i
    $$

- The interaction term $t_i \cdot X_i$ (treatment × covariate) is the simplest
  way to make the slope depend on the covariate
  - Each unit with different $X_i$ gets a different slope prediction despite
    unobservable slopes.

- You can estimate the fit and then use the coefficients to compute the
  CATE
  - A shortcut is to use 
    CATE = prediction(t+1) - prediction(t)

- If the treatment was not randomized, then you will need to do identification
  removing confounding

* Evaluating CATE Predictions
- This is similar to a ML problem, but you don't have the actual outcomes (since
  they are not observable)

- Fundamental problem: $Y_{1i}$ and $Y_{0i}$ are never observed for the same
  unit
- Cannot compute standard ML metrics (e.g., MSE on $\tau_i$)
  $$
  \tau(x) \defeq \EE[Y_1 - Y_0 | X = x]
  $$

1) Ground truth is non-observable at unit level, so evaluation must use
   group-level metrics
   - Instead: partition data by model predictions and estimate effects in each
     partition
2) Use train/test split for unbiased evaluation of CATE model on held-out data

* Process:
1. Fit the CATE model on training data
   ```
   regr_model = smf.ols("sales ~ discounts*(month + weekday + ...)",
                        data=train).fit()
   ```
2. Predict CATE values on test data
   ```
   test_pred = regr_model.predict(test.assign(discounts=test["discounts"]+1)) \
              - regr_model.predict(test)
   ```
3. Partition test data (by quantiles of those predictions)
   ```
   # Divide test data into 10 groups based on predicted CATE values
   quantiles = pd.qcut(test_pred, q=10)
   # Low quantile: days with low predicted CATE
   # High quantile: days with high predicted CATE
   ```
4. Estimate group effects (regression within each group)
   ```
   effect = regress(sales ~ discounts)
   ```
5. Evaluate (do the effects match the predictions?)
   - If your CATE predictions are good:
     - Quantile 1 (low prediction) → low actual effect
     - Quantile 10 (high prediction) → high actual effect
     - Monotonic staircase pattern = good model
   - Random model produces flat curve (same effect in all quantiles = ATE)

* Cumulative Effect
- Sort data by CATE score, accumulate groups sequentially
  - Estimate effect on top 1%, then top 2%, then top 3%, etc
  - Result: curve showing effect by cumulative sample percentage

- Well-ordered model curve shape: starts high, gradually converges to ATE
  - Bad model: quickly converges to ATE or fluctuates around it

* Cumulative Gain
- **Definition**: cumulative gain measures how much value a CATE model captures
  by sequentially treating units with highest predicted effects

- Multiply cumulative effect by cumulative sample proportion:
  $$
  \text{Gain} = \frac{N_{\text{cumulative}}}{N_{\text{total}}} \times (\text{effect} - ATE)
  $$
  - Downscales early portion where sample size (and confidence) is small
  - Centers around zero, with gains relative to population average effect

- **Interpretation**
  - Well-ordered model: steep initial climb, then plateaus near ATE
  - Random model: flat line (no gain above ATE anywhere)
  - Enables visual model comparison on a single plot

* Area Under Curve (AUC)

- **Definition**: area under the cumulative gain curve aggregates CATE model quality
  into a single summary metric

- **Intuition**
  - Larger AUC = better CATE ordering
  - AUC = 0 if model predicts constant effect (equivalent to random assignment)
  - AUC > 0 indicates true heterogeneity in treatment response

- **Application**
  - Automates model selection among competing CATE approaches
  - Enables hyperparameter tuning via grid search or Bayesian optimization

# Metalearners

* Metalearners
- **Goal**: Estimate conditional average treatment effect (CATE) to identify how
  unit $i$ responds differently to treatment
  $$\tau_i(x) \triangleq \EE[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) | X]$$

- **Definition**: metalearners leverage / recycle off-the-shelf machine learning
  algorithms for treatment effect estimation
  - Can estimate ATE but mainly used for CATE estimation
  - Work well with high-dimensional data

- Often this is a _ranking problem_: correctly order units by treatment
  sensitivity
  - Discrete treatments: binary or categorical
  - Continuous treatments: dose, price, discount level

* Marketing Email: Example
- Marketing team at an online retailer

- **Goal**: Determine which customers are receptive to a marketing email
  - The email has potential to increase customer spending

- **Problem**:
  - Your budget of discount is limited 
  - Some customers don't like receiving marketing emails
    - Avoid sending email to unreceptive customers to maintain relationship
      quality

- **Solution**: 
- Estimate CATE of email on customers' future purchase volume

- Data
  - Historical data about sending emails to fit CATE model
  - Data from experiments where emails were randomized (use for evaluation)

- The treatment variable is `mkt_email`
- The outcome variable is `next_mnth_pv` (purchase volume one month after sending
  the email)
- Covariates (e.g., age, time since first purchase, how much bought on each
  category)

* T-Learner
- **Approach**: Fit separate outcome models for each treatment level
  - For binary treatment: estimate
    $\mu_0(x) = \EE[Y | T=0, X]$ and 
    $\mu_1(x) = \EE[Y | T=1, X]$
  - Predict both potential outcomes for each unit and then estimate CATE as:
    $$\hat{\tau}(x)_i = \hat{\mu}_1(X_i) - \hat{\mu}_0(X_i)$$

- **Advantages**: Simple and straightforward implementation

- **Disadvantage**: Prone to regularization bias

* Regularization Bias
- **Problem**: In T-learner you need to fit two models at the same time
  - If treated data is sparse, $mu_1$ becomes overly regularized and simple
    (e.g., a constant)
  - If untreated data is abundant, $mu_0$ can fit a non-linearity

- **Result**:
  - Bias in CATE estimation (nonlinear difference minus linear difference)
  - E.g., model with nonlinear outcome but constant treatment effect produces
    wrong nonlinear CATE
  - In imbalanced data, regularization creates artificial heterogeneity

* X-Learner
- **Approach**
  - Address regularization bias of T-learner
  - Two-stage approach with propensity score weighting
  - Performs better when one treatment group is much larger than the other

- **Stage 1**: Fit initial outcome models (same as T-learner)
  - $\mu_0(X)$ and $\mu_1(X)$ for control and treated groups

- **Stage 2**: Estimate imputed treatment effects
  - For control units: estimate missing treatment outcome:
    $$\hat{\tau}(X, T=0) = \hat{\mu}_1(X, T=0) - Y_{T=0}$$
  - For treated units: estimate missing control outcome:
    $$\hat{\tau}(X, T=1) = Y_{T=1} - \hat{\mu}_0(X, T=1)$$

- **Stage 3**: Fit effect models
  - Fit $\hat{\mu}_{\tau 0}(x)$ to predict $\hat{\tau}(X, T=0)$
  - Fit $\hat{\mu}_{\tau 1}(x)$ to predict $\hat{\tau}(X, T=1)$

- **Pros**
  - X-learner shines when large imbalance between treatment and control groups

* X-Learner With Weighting Scheme
- Combine second-stage models using propensity score $\hat{e}(x) = \Pr(T=1|X)$
  $$\hat{\tau}(x) 
  = \hat{\mu}_{\tau 0}(x) \cdot \hat{e}(x) + 
    \hat{\mu}_{\tau 1}(x) \cdot (1 - \hat{e}(x))$$

- **Intuition**
  - When treatment is rare: $\hat{e}(x)$ is small, down-weights flawed
    $\hat{\mu}_{\tau 0}$
  - When treatment is common: $1 - \hat{e}(x)$ is large, up-weights correct
    $\hat{\mu}_{\tau 1}$
  - Automatically emphasizes the model trained on larger sub-sample

* Metalearners for Continuous Treatments
- **Problem**: Randomized discount experiment at restaurant chain
  - Goal: which days should discounts be highest?
  - Treatment: discount amount (continuous, 0-40%)
  - Outcome: sales volume one day later

- **Insight**
  - Customers more sensitive to prices on some days (e.g., holidays)
  - CATE tells us how much demand increases per unit discount
  - High CATE days $\Rightarrow$ use more discounts
  - Low CATE days $\Rightarrow$ use fewer

- **Problem**
- Discrete approaches fail because they
  - Can't fit separate models for each discount level (infinite treatments)
  - Need methods that handle continuous treatment as a feature or component

- **Solution**: S-learner and Double-ML

* S-Learner
- **Approach**: Single machine learning model that includes treatment $T$ and
  covariates $X$ to predict outcome $Y$
  - Goal is to estimate $\mu_s(x, t) = E[Y | T, X]$
  - "S-Learner" since it's "single"
  - This model doesn't output treatment effect, rather it outputs counterfactual
    predictions (i.e., predictions under different treatment regimes)

- **For binary treatment**: CATE = $\mu_s(X_i, T=1) - \mu_s(X_i, T=0)$

- **For continuous treatment**: Extended procedure needed
  - Create grid of treatment values (e.g., [0, 10, 20, 30, 40] for discounts)
  - Compute predictions for all treatment level
  - Summarize as slope via simple linear regression to extract CATE
  - Formula: $\beta = \text{Cov}(t, y) / \text{Var}(t)$

- **Pros**:
  - Simple
  - Supports both binary and continuous treatments
  - Performs reasonably well

- **Cons**:
  - Biases treatment effect toward zero
  - Treatment variable can be completely discarded if weak relative to other
    covariates

- **Solution**:
  - Use Double/Debiased Machine Learning (R-learner) to address bias

* S-Learner: Implementation Steps

- **Step 1**: Train single model on all data
  - `s_learner.fit(X + [T], y)` where T is treatment feature

- **Step 2**: Create counterfactual grid
  - Define treatment grid: e.g., $[0, 10, 20, 30, 40]$
  - Cross-join with test set: each test unit at each treatment level
  - Makes predictions on expanded grid

- **Step 3**: Extract slopes
  - Group by unit, apply linear regression
  - Extract slope coefficient as unit-level CATE
  - Can also use slope formula directly:
    $\hat{\beta} = \text{Cov}(t,y) / \text{Var}(t)$

- **Result**: CATE prediction for each unit

* S-Learner: The Bias Problem

- **Problem**
  - ML models are regularized by default
  - Regularization can suppress treatment effect unless treatment signal is very
    strong
  - ML model may discard treatment feature entirely
  - Distribution of estimated ATE is shifted toward zero compared to true effect

- **Solution**
  - Use orthogonalization techniques

* R-learner (Double/Debiased Machine Learning)

- **The principle**:
  - Use ML to model confounding relationships
  - Orthogonalize treatment and outcome from confounders
  - Extract treatment effect from residuals

- **The equation**
  - $\widetilde{Y}_i = Y_i - \hat{\mu}_y(X_i)$ (outcome residual)
  - $\widetilde{T}_i = T_i - \hat{\mu}_t(X_i)$ (treatment residual)
  - Regress residuals: $\widetilde{Y}_i = \tau \widetilde{T}_i + \varepsilon_i$

- **Why it works**
  - $\hat{\mu}_y$ captures confounding effects on outcome
  - $\hat{\mu}_t$ captures confounding effects on treatment
  - Residuals are "as if" treatment was randomized

- **Key requirement**: no unobserved confounders

* Double-ML: the Overfitting Problem

- **Stage 1**: Estimate nuisance parameters
  - Focus on treatment effect $\tau$ (or CATE $\tau(X)$)
  - Estimate nuisance parameters (what we are not interested in) to obtain
    parameter of interest
  - _Step 1_: Estimate outcome model $\hat{\mu}_y(X)$
    - Fit ML regression model to predict outcome $Y$ from features $X$
    - E.g., capture nonlinear confounding relationships between $X$ and $Y$
    - Use cross-validation predictions to prevent overfitting
  - _Step 2_: Estimate treatment model $\hat{\mu}_t(X)$
    - Fit ML regression model to predict treatment $T$ from features $X$

- **Stage 2**: Compute residuals
  - _Step 3_: Residualize outcome $\widetilde{Y} = Y - \hat{\mu}_y(X)$
  - _Step 4_: Residualize treatment $\widetilde{T} = T - \hat{\mu}_t(X)$
    - Residuals represent part of $Y$ and $T$ independent of $X$
    - Confounding is removed and can estimate treatment effect without bias

- **Stage 3**: Construct the causal target
  - _Step 5_: Create transformed outcome
    $Y^* = \frac{\widetilde{Y}}{\widetilde{T}}$
    - Dividing gives "slope" of relationship between treatment and outcome
      residuals, i.e., estimate of $\tau(X_i)$
  - _Step 6_: Create weights $w_i = \widetilde{T}_i^2$
  - Dividing by $\widetilde{T}$ introduces noise
  - Squared weights down-weight noisy estimates, up-weight stable estimates

- **Stage 4**: Estimate the CATE Model
  - _Step 7_: Fit final ML model to predict $Y^*$ from $X$, using weights $w_i$
    - Train ML regression model on $(Y^*, X)$
    - Use weights $w_i$ for stable, low-noise predictions
    - Learn how treatment effect varies with features $X$
    - Model predictions give heterogeneous treatment effects

* Double-ML for CATE Estimation

- Allow causal parameter to vary with covariates $\tau(X_i)$
  - Outcome model:
    $$Y_i = \hat{\mu}_y(X_i) + \tau(X_i) \cdot \widetilde{T}_i + \varepsilon_i$$

- You can isolate the error, which is called "R-loss" and can be used as a causal
  objective function
  $$\hat{L}_n(\tau(x)) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\widetilde{Y}_i - \tau(X_i)
  \widetilde{T}_i)^2$$
  - Minimizing this loss directly estimates CATE

# Difference-in-differences

## Canonical Difference-in-Differences

* Data Structures: Definition

- Data can have different temporal and cross-sectional patterns
  - Causal relationships can be estimated depending on the data structure

- **Definition**:
  - _Cross-sectional data_: multiple units observed at a single point in time
    - E.g., snapshot of sales across restaurants with/without discounts
    - E.g., student test scores across schools in a single academic year
  - _Panel data_ (aka _longitudinal data_): same units observed repeatedly over
    time
    - E.g., same restaurants before/after discount implementation
    - E.g., same students' test scores tracked across multiple year
  - _Repeated cross-sectional data_: different units observed at multiple time
    points
    - E.g., different restaurant samples in year 1 and year 2
    - E.g., different student samples from schools surveyed in 2020 and 2025
  - _Pooled cross-section_: multiple independent cross-sections combined and
    analyzed as a single dataset
    - E.g., multiple cross-sections treated as one large cross-section
    - E.g., combining student test score data from 2020, 2022, and 2024 into one
      regression

* Panel
- **Motivation**:
  - Most real-world interventions (policy, marketing, etc.) are difficult to
    randomize
    - E.g., in marketing it's difficult to control who receives the treatment
      (i.e., see your advertisement)
  - Panel data offers two sources of variation
    - _Across units_: compare treated and control cities (spatial variation)
    - _Across time_: compare pre- and post-treatment periods (temporal variation)

- **Key insight**: By tracking units over time, we can remove confounders that
  are fixed at the unit level
  - Even with unmeasured confounders, we can identify causal effects
  - This makes panel data _as good as it gets_ for observational causal
    inference

* Canonical Difference-in-Differences: The Setup

- **Notation**:
  - $D_i = 1$ if unit $i$ is treated, $0$ otherwise
  - $t \leq T_{pre}$ is pre-intervention period
  - $t > T_{pre}$ is post-intervention
  - $\text{Post}_t = 1$ if $t > T_{pre}$, otherwise $0$

- **Definition**:
  - The treatment indicator is $W_{it} = D_i \cdot \text{Post}_t$
    - Equals 1 only for treated units in post-treatment period

  - The outcome variable is $Y_{it}$ (e.g., number of downloads)
    - Observable for all unit-time combinations

  - The target is the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT), i.e., the
    effect of treatment on units that received it, after treatment takes place:
    $$
    \text{ATT} = \EE[Y_{it}(1) - Y_{it}(0) | D_i = 1, t > T_{pre}]
    $$

* DiD: Imputing the Counterfactual

- The missing piece is the treated counterfactual
  $$\EE[Y(0) | D = 1, \text{Post} = 1]$$
  which we estimate with:
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
    \EE[Y(0) | D = 1, Post = 1]
    &= \EE[Y | D = 1, Post = 0] \\
    &\quad + \left(
      \EE[Y | D = 0, Post = 1]
      - \EE[Y | D = 0, Post = 0]
    \right)
  \end{aligned}
  $$

- **Intuition**: Start with the treated baseline, add the control growth
  - _Treated baseline_: average outcome of treated units before treatment
  - _Control growth_: how much control units changed from pre to post
  - Assumption: This growth rate applies to treated units under the counterfactual

* Why Difference-in-Difference?

- Replacing the estimate in ATT:
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
    \text{ATT}
    &= \big[
      \EE[Y | D = 1, \text{Post} = 1]
      - \EE[Y | D = 1, \text{Post} = 0]
      \big] \\
    &\quad - \big[
      \EE[Y | D = 0, \text{Post} = 1]
      - \EE[Y | D = 0, \text{Post} = 0]
      \big]
  \end{aligned}
  $$
  - _First bracket_: treated group's change (pre to post)
  - _Second bracket_: control group's change (pre to post)
  - Difference of these two changes is the treatment effect
