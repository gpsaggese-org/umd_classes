# AI and Machine Learning

## What is AI?

* ML, AI, and Intelligence

- @Definition@: **Machine Learning** is a subset of AI
  - Often confused with deep learning, large-language models, predictive
    analytics, and other related fields

- **Questions to explore**
  - What is artificial intelligence?
  - What is intelligence?

* Human Intelligence
- We call ourselves "homo sapiens" because intelligence sets us apart from animals

- For thousands of years, we tried to understand how we think

- One of the biggest mysteries
  - Brain is a small mass of matter
  - Our brain can understand nature secrets, e.g., theory of relativity, quantum
    mechanics, black holes in the universe
  - How can brain understand, predict, and manipulate a world more complicated
    than itself?

* Artificial Intelligence

- @Definition@: The term _"Artificial Intelligence"_ was coined in 1956

- @Goal@
  - Understand human intelligence
  - Create intelligent entities
  - _"What I cannot create, I do not understand"_ (Feynman, 1988)

- @Characteristics@
  - Universal and applicable to any human activity and task
  - Its impact is greater than any previous historical event
  - Currently generates trillions of dollars annually in revenue
  - Presents many unresolved problems
    - E.g., major concepts in physics might be established

* AI Formal Definition

- @Definition@: AI is characterized along **two key axes**
  - Thinking vs. Acting
  - Human vs. Rational (ideal performance)

- This yields four possible definitions of AI as a machine that can:
  1. Think humanly
  2. Think rationally
  3. Act humanly
  4. Act rationally

- **Question**: Which definition best captures what AI should aim for?

- **Key insight**: Building machines that **act rationally** should be the
  ultimate goal of AI

* 1. AI as Thinking Humanly

- @Problem@: To build machines that think like humans, we need to first
  **determine how humans think**

- **Pros**
  - Express precise theory of the human mind as a computer program

- **Cons**
  - Unknown workings of the human mind
  - Anthropocentric definition

* 2. AI as Thinking Rationally

- @Question@: What are the rules of **correct thinking**?
  - Given correct premises, yield correct conclusions

- @Approach@: **Logic** studies the "laws of thought"
  - Formalize statements about objects and their relations

- @Technique@: **Automatic theorem proving**
  - Programs solve problems in logical notation
  - Run indefinitely if no solution exists (related to the halting problem)

* Thinking Rationally: Cons

1. **Formalizing informal knowledge is difficult**
   - @Example@: _"A handshake occurs when two people extend, grip, shake
     hands, then release."_
   - Formal logic representation:
     $$
     \exists x, y \, (
       \text{Person}(x) \land \text{Person}(y) \land x \neq y \land
       \text{Hand}(x, h_x) \land \text{Hand}(y, h_y) \land
       \text{MoveToward}(h_x, h_y) \land \text{Contact}(h_x, h_y) \land
       \text{Shake}(h_x, h_y) \land
       \text{Release}(h_x, h_y)
     )
     $$

2. **Probabilistic nature of knowledge**
   - @Example@: In medicine, _"Fever, cough, and fatigue could indicate
     flu, COVID-19, or another illness."_

3. **Scalability challenges**
   - Large problems may need heuristics for practical solutions

4. **Intelligence requires more than rational thinking**
   - Importance of agent interaction with the world
   - Problem of the "body"

* 3. AI as Acting Humanly

- @Definition@: An **agent** is something that perceives and acts to reach a
  goal

- @Goal@: AI designs **agents that can act like humans**

- @Approach@: **Turing test**
  - _"A computer passes the Turing test if a human cannot tell whether the
    answers to questions came from a person or a computer"_

::: columns
:::: {.column width=65%}
- @Requirements@: Passing the (embodied) Turing test requires
  1. Natural language processing to communicate
  2. Knowledge representation to store information
  3. Automated reasoning to use stored knowledge and answer questions
  4. Machine learning to detect patterns
  5. Computer vision and speech recognition to perceive objects and
     understand speech
  6. Robotics to manipulate objects and move

* Turing Test: Pros and Cons

- **Pros**
  - Operational definition of intelligence
  - Sidestep philosophical vagueness
    - What is consciousness?
    - Can a machine think?

- **Cons**
  - Intelligence defined by **anthropomorphic** criteria in human terms
    - Multiple forms of non-human intelligence exist
  - Intelligence by Turing test means **fooling humans** into thinking it is
    human
  - @Example@: Aeronautical engineering is about
    - Correct: Focus on wind tunnels and aerodynamics
    - Incorrect: Designing machines that imitate birds

* 4. AI as Acting Rationally

- @Definition@: **Rational agents** are agents that do the "right thing"
  given what they know

- @Characteristics@: Agents that **act rationally** should
  1. Operate autonomously
  2. Perceive environment
  3. Persist over a prolonged time period
  4. Adapt to change
  5. Create and pursue goals

* Acting Rationally as Ultimate Goal of AI

- @Question@: Which definition of AI best captures what we should build?
  - Acting vs. Thinking
  - Rational vs. Human

- **Acting is more fundamental than Thinking**
  - Acting rationally is broader than just thinking rationally

- **Rational is more objective than Human**
  - Rationality can be mathematically defined
  - Human behavior is shaped by evolutionary conditions

- @Conclusion@: AI should focus on **agents acting rationally**

* Rationally is Not Absolute

- @Problem@: AI aims to build agents that **do the right thing**, but what
  is the right thing?

- @Example@: You leave the house and a branch strikes you
  - Did you act rationally?
  - Probably

- @Example@: You cross the street and a car knocks you over
  - Did you act rationally?
  - It depends, but probably not

- @Challenge@: Moral issues with self-driving cars
  - Should a car swerve and hit a pedestrian to avoid a frontal crash that
    would kill two people?

* Problems of a Rational Agent

- @Challenge@: **Probabilistic environment**
  - A rational agent aims for
    - The best outcome in a deterministic setup
    - The best expected outcome under uncertainty

- @Problem@: What does **"best"** mean? It is determined by the objective
  function
  - E.g., cost function, sum of rewards, loss function, utility

- @Consideration@: **Omniscience vs no-regrets**
  - Best is based on available information, not perfect knowledge

- @Constraint@: Sometimes **no provably correct action** exists
  - Yet, an action must be taken

- @Limitation@: Even **with perfect information** rationality may not be
  feasible due to
  - Cost of acquiring all data (e.g., in medicine)
  - Computational demands

- @Solution@: **"Satisficing" (perfect good enough vs perfect)**
  - Acting appropriately given computational constraints

## What is Machine Learning?
## #############################################################################

* Machine Learning: Definitions

- @Question@: How should we define machine learning?

- @Definition@: _"Machine learning is the field of study that gives
  computers the ability to learn without being explicitly programmed"_
  (Samuel, 1959)

- @Alternative@: **Machine learning** builds machines to do **useful things**
  without being **explicitly programmed**
  - @Example@: A computer learns to play checkers by playing against itself,
    memorizing positions that lead to winning

- @Formal@: _"A computer program is said to learn from experience $E$ with
  respect to some task $T$ and some performance measure $P$, if $P(T)$
  improves with experience $E$"_ (Mitchell, 1998)

- @Applications@: Common examples include
  - Computer vision
  - Speech recognition
  - Natural language processing

* Limits of ML Compared to Human Intelligence

- @Problem@: **AI differs from human intelligence**
  - Machines do not learn like humans (e.g., LLMs)

- @Limitation@: **Fragility to input variations**
  - ML models fail with slight input distortions
    - Adversarial attacks cause misclassification by altering one pixel
    - A model trained on a video game fails if the screen rotates slightly;
      humans continue effortlessly

- @Limitation@: **Lack of transfer learning**
  - ML systems cannot apply knowledge across domains without retraining

- @Challenge@: **Massive data and compute requirements**
  - ML requires enormous datasets and computational resources
    - A teenager learns to drive in hours
    - Self-driving systems need billions of compute hours and extensive
      data

- @Limitation@: **Poor common sense and reasoning**
  - ML lacks built-in world knowledge and intuitive logic

* Limits of ML Compared to Human Intelligence (cont.)

- @Limitation@: **Opaque decision-making**
  - Many ML models offer little transparency into decision processes
    - Limits trust, interpretability, and accountability in critical
      applications

- @Limitation@: **Dependence on narrow objectives**
  - ML systems excel at optimizing narrow tasks but fail with ambiguous
    goals
    - @Example@: An algorithm maximizing user engagement may promote harmful
      content

- @Limitation@: **Susceptibility to bias and data quality**
  - Models inherit and amplify biases in training data

- @Limitation@: **Lack of embodiment and physical interaction**
  - Human cognition is grounded in physical and sensory experience

* The 3 Machine Learning Assumptions

- @Context@: In practice, ML involves solving a practical problem by
  - Gathering a dataset
  - Building a statistical model from the dataset algorithmically

- @Definition@: The **three core assumptions** of machine learning
  - A **pattern exists**
  - Pattern cannot be **precisely defined mathematically**
  - **Data is available**

- @Question@: Which assumption is **essential**?
  - A pattern exists: If no pattern exists, learning fails
  - Pattern cannot be precisely defined: Even direct solutions can use ML
  - Data is available: **Without data, no progress is possible**
    - Data is the most crucial assumption

* AI vs ML vs Deep-Learning

::: columns
:::: {.column width=65%}
- @Definition@: **AI (Artificial Intelligence)**
  - Machines programmed to reason, learn, and act in a rational way

- @Definition@: **ML (Machine Learning)**
  - Machines capable of performing tasks without being explicitly programmed

- @Example@: **AI without ML**
  - Rule-based systems (e.g., IBM Deep Blue playing chess)

- @Definition@: **DL (Deep Learning)**
  - ML using neural networks with many layers

- @Definition@: **LLM (Large Language Models)**
  - Neural networks trained on massive text datasets and reinforcement
    learning from human feedback
