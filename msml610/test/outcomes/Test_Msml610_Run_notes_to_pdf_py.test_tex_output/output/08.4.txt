  for \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Christmas}}

  where:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{store}}: id of the store
  \item
    \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{weeks\_to\_xmas}}: how many weeks before

  \item
    \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{avg\_week\_sales}}: how much they sold in

  \item
    \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{is\_on\_sale}}: if store puts product on

    store has sold in that week
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Causal inference question}}: \emph{``What is
  the impact of \(T\) on \(Y\)?''}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    E.g., what is the impact of \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{is\_on\_sale}}
    on \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{weekly\_amount\_sold}}?
  \item
    E.g., when is the best moment to have sales (i.e.,
    \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{weeks\_to\_xmas}})?

\end{itemize}

  \item
    Matches intuition: \emph{``people buy more when prices are low due
    to sales''}
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Causal Model}
\protect\phantomsection\label{causal-model}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A \textbf{\textcolor{red}{causal model}} is a series of one-way
  assignments \(\leftarrow\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Causality is non-reversible: you can't rearrange the equation
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.6\linewidth}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Example}} \[
  \left\{
  \begin{aligned}
  & T \leftarrow f_t(u_t) \\
  & Y \leftarrow f_y(T, u_y) \\
  \end{aligned}
  \right.
  \] where:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(u_t, u_y\) are \emph{exogenous} variables (outside the model)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Account for everything not covered by model variables
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Treatment \(T\) and \(u_y\) cause outcome \(Y\)
  \item
    \(T\) and \(Y\) are \emph{endogenous} variables (inside the model)
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{tmp.notes_to_pdf.preprocess_notes.txt.figs/tmp.notes_to_pdf.render_image.2.png}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Applying a Treatment to Sales Example}
\protect\phantomsection\label{applying-a-treatment-to-sales-example}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Assumption}}: the causal model is: \[
  \small
  \left\{
  \begin{aligned}
  & T \leftarrow f_t(u_t) \\
  & Y \leftarrow f_y(T, u_y) \\
  \end{aligned}
  \right.
  \]
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Question}}: \emph{``what would happen to the
  outcome \(Y\) if I were to set the treatment to \(t_0\)?''} \[
  \small
  \left\{
  \begin{aligned}
  & T \leftarrow t_0 \\
  & Y \leftarrow f_y(T, u_y) \\
  \end{aligned}
  \right.
  \]
\item
  The intervention is represented by the \(do()\) operator, e.g.,
  \(do(T = t_0)\)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conditioning vs Intervention}
\protect\phantomsection\label{conditioning-vs-intervention}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \emph{Conditioning} represent what happens to businesses that make a
  price cut \[
  \EE[AmountSold | isOnSales = 1]
  \]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Filter samples for business that \emph{decide} to cut prices
  \item
    Some business (e.g., large businesses) are \emph{self-selecting} to
    cut prices
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \emph{Intervention} is what happens if \emph{we force} every business
  to engage in sales \[
  \EE[AmountSold | do(IsOnSales = 1)]
  \]
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Remark}}: Conditioning and intervention are
  different \[
  \small
  \EE[AmountSold | isOnSales = 1] \ne \EE[AmountSold | do(IsOnSales = 1)]
  \]
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Individual Treatment Effect}
\protect\phantomsection\label{individual-treatment-effect}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: the impact of the treatment
  \(T\) on the outcome \(Y\) for an individual unit \(i\) is:
  \[\tau_i \defeq Y_i|do(T=t_1) - Y_i|do(T=t_0)\]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The effect \(\tau_i\) of going from treatment \(t_0\) to \(t_1\) for
    unit \(i\) is the difference in the outcome of that unit under
    \(t_1\) compared to \(t_0\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  E.g., for the sales example
  \[AmountSold_i|do(IsOnSales=1) - AmountSold_i|do(IsOnSales=0)\]
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Problem}}: you can only observe one term due
  to the fundamental problem of causal inference

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Represent it in theory, but can't recover it from data
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Potential Outcomes}
\protect\phantomsection\label{potential-outcomes}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  Aka ``counterfactuals''
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: the \(do()\) operator represents
  \emph{``unit \(i\) outcome \(Y\) if treatment is set to \(t\)''}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(Y_{t,i}\) is Rubin's notation
  \item
    \(Y_i | do(T_i = t)\) is Pearl's notation
  \end{itemize}
\item
  For binary treatment:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(Y_{0i}\) potential outcome without treatment
  \item
    \(Y_{1i}\) potential outcome with treatment
  \item
    One is ``factual'' (i.e., observable)
  \item
    The other is ``counterfactual'' (i.e., theoretical, not observable)
  \item
    Alternative expression: \[
    Y_i = T_i * Y_{1i} + (1 - T_i) * Y_{0i} = Y_{0i} + (Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}) * T_i
    \]
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Remark}}: there is a difference between
  conditioning and intervention

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(Y|do(T=t)\) is \emph{``what a business would sell if it cut
    prices''}
  \item
    \(Y|T=t\) is \emph{``what a business that cut the prices sold''}
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Consistency of the Treatment}
\protect\phantomsection\label{consistency-of-the-treatment}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Assumption}}: consistency of the treatment
  means that there is a single version of the treatment

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    I.e., \(T = T_i\) means only one thing
  \item
    No hidden multiple versions of the treatment
  \end{itemize}
\item
  E.g.,

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    A drug can come in multiple dosages, but only one is used
  \item
    Giving a discount coupon: the value of the coupon is the same, it's
    given only once, in way that is always equivalent
  \item
    In \emph{``help from a financial planner''}: is ``help'' a one-time
    consultation or regular advice?
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{No Interference (SUTVA)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{no-interference-sutva}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  Aka ``Stable Unit of Treatment Value (SUTVA)''
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Assumption}}: the effect of one unit is not
  influenced by the treatment of other units

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    E.g., there are no spillovers or network effects
  \end{itemize}
\item
  E.g., vaccinating one person makes other close people less likely to
  catch the illness even if they don't get the treatment
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Average Treatment Effect}
\protect\phantomsection\label{average-treatment-effect}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  Assume you can see both potential outcomes \(Y_{1i}\) and \(Y_{0i}\)
  at the same time (which is impossible)
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: Average Treatment Effect (ATE)
  is the impact that the treatment \(T\) would have on the average (on
  all units): \[
  ATE \defeq \EE[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}] = \EE[Y | do(T=1)] - \EE[Y | do(T=0)]
  \]
\item
  E.g., average impact of price cuts on amount sold: \[
  ATE = \EE[AmountSold_{1i} - AmountSold_{0i}]
  \]
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Average Treatment effect on the Treated (ATT)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{average-treatment-effect-on-the-treated-att}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: Average Treatment effect on the
  Treated (ATT) is the impact of the treatment on the units that got the
  treatment: \[
  ATT = \EE[(Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}) | T=1]
  \]
\item
  E.g., how much sales increase for the business engaged in price cuts:
  \[
  ATT = \EE[(AmountSold_{1i} - AmountSold_{0i}) | IsOnSales=1]
  \]
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conditional Average Treatment Effects (CATE)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{conditional-average-treatment-effects-cate}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: Conditional Average Treatment
  Effects (CATE) is the impact of the treatment on the units in a group
  defined by \(X=x\) \[
  CATE = \EE[(Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}) | X=x]
  \]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    It's like \(ATE\) but on a subgroup with a certain characteristics
  \item
    Note that there is both intervention and conditioning
  \end{itemize}
\item
  E.g., the impact of having sales during the week of Christmas: \[
  CATE = \EE[AmountSold_{1i} - AmountSold_{0i} | weeksToXmas = 0]
  \]
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Remark}}: We can \emph{define} these
  quantities \(ATE\), \(CATE\), \(ATT\), but not \emph{measure} them
  directly

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Because we can't observe both potential outcomes \(Y_{1i}\) and
    \(Y_{0i}\) at the same time
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Remark}}: We can extend the definition to
  continuous treatments using partial derivatives of \(Y_i\),
  \(\frac{\partial \mathbb{\EE}[Y_i]}{\partial t}\)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bias}
\protect\phantomsection\label{bias}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: \emph{bias} is what makes
  association different from causation

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Treated and untreated groups differ in ways other than treatment
  \end{itemize}
\item
  The claim \emph{``cutting prices increases the amount sold by a
  business''} can be questioned with:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{``Those businesses that cut prices could probably have sold
    more anyway, even with no price cuts''}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Formally bias refers to potential outcomes

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    We are trying to estimate:
    \[\EE[Y_{it}] \triangleq \EE[Y_i | do(T=t)]\] using
    \(\EE[Y_i | T=t]\), which is a ``biased estimator'' (i.e., an
    incorrect one)
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Bias Equation}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-bias-equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Causal inference}} is interested in:
  \begingroup
  \small
  $$
  \EE[Y_1 - Y_0] = \EE[Y | do(T = 1)] - \EE[Y | do(T = 0)]
  $$
  \endgroup
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{What is measured}} is: \begingroup
  \small
  $$
  \EE[Y | T = 1] - \EE[Y | T = 0]
  $$
  \endgroup
\item
  Let's express one in terms of the other
\item
  Replacing the observed outcomes (each unit has two potential outcomes
  but only one can be observed and the other is 0) \begingroup
  \small
  $$
  Y | T=1 = (Y_1 | T=1) + \cancel{(Y_0 | T=1)} = Y_1 | T=1
  $$
  \endgroup
\item
  Adding and subtracting \(\EE[Y_0 | T=1]\), reordering and merging
  expectations // Make entire block smaller \begingroup
  \small
  $$
  \underbrace{\EE[Y | T = 1] - \EE[Y | T = 0]}_{\text{Measured}} =
  \underbrace{\EE[Y_1 - Y_0 | T = 1]}_{\text{ATT}} +
  \underbrace{\EE[Y_0 | T = 1] - \EE[Y_0 | T = 0]}_{\text{Bias}}
  $$
  \endgroup
\item
  Association = causation + bias term from group difference
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Bias Equation: Remarks}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-bias-equation-remarks}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  Bias equation says: \[
  \text{Association = causation + a bias term}
  \] \begingroup
  \small
  $$
  \underbrace{\EE[Y | T = 1] - \EE[Y | T = 0]}_{\text{Measured}} =
  \underbrace{\EE[Y_1 - Y_0 | T = 1]}_{\text{ATT}} +
  \underbrace{\EE[Y_0 | T = 1] - \EE[Y_0 | T = 0]}_{\text{Bias}}
  $$
  \endgroup
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Remark}}: bias arises from differences between
  treated and control groups, besides the treatment

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Unobservable factors change together with treatment (i.e.,
    confounders)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  In sales example, treated and untreated businesses differ beyond price
  cuts

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    E.g., differ in size, location, day of the week on which they have a
    sale, cities they are located in
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Corollary}}: for association to be causation,
  treated and control units must be \emph{exchangeable}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identifying the Treatment Effect}
\protect\phantomsection\label{identifying-the-treatment-effect}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  We can't observe \(\EE[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}]\) since we can only observe
  one of the potential outcomes \(Y_{1i}\) or \(Y_{0i}\), but \emph{not
  both}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Question}}: how can we recover causal
  quantities from observable data?
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Solution}}: the bias equation says that:
  \begingroup
  \small
  $$
  \underbrace{\EE[Y | T = 1] - \EE[Y | T = 0]} =
  \underbrace{\EE[Y_1 - Y_0 | T = 1]}_{\text{ATT}} +
  \underbrace{\EE[Y_0 | T = 1] - \EE[Y_0 | T = 0]}_{\text{Bias}}
  $$
  \endgroup
\item
  If the treatment and the control group are comparable regardless of
  the treatment (exchangeable), i.e., \(\EE[Y_0|T=0] = \EE[Y_0|T=1]\)
  then:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The bias term vanishes: \(bias = 0\)
  \item
    The measured difference in means equals
    \(ATT = \EE[(Y_1 - Y_0) | T = 1]\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  If treatment effect is homogeneous (both groups respond similarly to
  treatment), i.e.,
  \(\EE[(Y_1 - Y_0) | T = 1] = \EE[(Y_1 - Y_0) | T = 0]\) then:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The measured difference of means equals \(ATE = \EE[Y_1 - Y_0]\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Sales Example}}: if businesses that cut and
  don't cut prices are similar to each other (i.e., exchangeable), then
  the difference in amount sold be entirely attributed to the price cut
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Independence Property (Aka Unconfoundedness)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{independence-property-aka-unconfoundedness}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: the independence property means
  that \emph{potential outcomes} are independent of the treatment: \[
  (Y_0, Y_1) \perp T
  \]
\item
  It's usually stated conditional on covariates:
  \((Y_0, Y_1) \perp T | X\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Without \(X\) it implies complete randomization (like a perfect
    experiment)
  \item
    In observational settings, we almost always need conditioning
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Implication}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(\EE[Y_0 | T] = \EE[Y_0]\): the treatment gives no information
    about potential outcomes
  \item
    \(\EE[Y_0 | T=1] = \EE[Y_0 | T=0]\): both groups have the same
    \(Y_0\) in average (exchangeable)
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Independence Property (Aka Unconfoundedness)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{independence-property-aka-unconfoundedness-1}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In \((Y_0, Y_1) \perp T\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    We are not asking for \emph{observed outcome} \(Y\) and treatment
    \(T\) to be independent (i.e., the treatment has no effect)
  \item
    We are asking for each \emph{potential outcome} \(Y_0\) and \(Y_1\)
    to be independent of the treatment
  \item
    \emph{``Knowing whether someone received the treatment tells you
    nothing about what their outcome would have been without it''}
  \item
    Treatment assignment is as good as random, so differences in
    outcomes can be attributed to the treatment
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Sales Example}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    We can't tell apart businesses that chose to engage in sales from
    those that didn't
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Phases of Causal Inference}
\protect\phantomsection\label{phases-of-causal-inference}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A causal inference problem is often broken down into two steps:

  \begin{enumerate}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Identification}}

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Express the causal quantity of interest in terms of observable
      data
    \item
      Remove the bias, making treated and untreated comparable, so that
      all the difference can be attributed to the treatment effect
      \[\EE[Y_0 | T=0] = \EE[Y_0 | T=1]\]
    \item
      You need to know how the treatment was distributed or assigned
    \item
      This is why data alone (observational) cannot answer causal
      questions
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Estimation}}

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Use data to estimate the causal quantity of interest:
      \[\EE[Y | T=1] - \EE[Y | T=0]\]
    \end{itemize}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Randomization Removes Bias}
\protect\phantomsection\label{randomization-removes-bias}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Randomization of treatment forces independence to hold \(\implies\)
  removes bias \[\EE[Y | T = 1] - \EE[Y | T = 0] = \EE[Y_1 - Y_0]\]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The measured difference of means is the true ATE (Average Treatment
    Effect)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Sales Example}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Randomize the treatment, i.e., by forcing a business to cut prices
    \[
    \left\{
    \begin{aligned}
    & IsOnSales \leftarrow rand(t) \\
    & AmountSold \leftarrow f_y(IsOnSales, u_y) \\
    \end{aligned}
    \right.
    \]
  \item
    The dependency from \(u_t\) is replaced by assignment mechanism that
    is:

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Tied to a coin flip (unrelated to other factors in causal
      mechanism)
    \item
      Fully known
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Caveat}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Randomizing eligibility doesn't mean randomizing participation
  \item
    E.g., online retailer randomly offered membership program
  \item
    But only engaged customers opted in, so self-selection remained
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A/B Testing: Example}
\protect\phantomsection\label{ab-testing-example}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Fact}}: a common strategy among companies is
  to:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Have a cheap / free product to attract customers
  \item
    Cross-sell other products that are more profitable

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Use email campaigns to sell premium product
    \item
      When a customer get an email and buys the premium product, they
      are ``converted''
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Non-randomized data show that customers receiving emails convert more
  \[
  \EE[ Conversion | Email = 1] > \EE[ Conversion | Email = 0]
  \]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Problem}}: marketing team tends to
    send emails for customers who they think they will convert (e.g.,
    using a survey, customers who visited the website the most) \[
    \EE[ Conversion_0 | Email = 1] > \EE[ Conversion_0 | Email = 0]
    \]
  \item
    Customers would have converted even if the email was not sent
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Solution}}: make the treated and untreated
  comparable with A/B testing

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    E.g., randomize who gets the email
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Chains}
\protect\phantomsection\label{chains}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: a chain is a causal graph
  \(T \to M \to Y\):

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(T\) is the treatment
  \item
    \(Y\) is the outcome
  \item
    \(M\) is ``mediator'' since it mediates the relationship between
    \(T\) and \(Y\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Causation flows only in the direction of the arrows

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    I.e., changing \(M\) affects \(Y\), but doesn't affect \(T\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Association flows both ways

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    I.e., \(T\) and \(Y\) are dependent, \(T \not\!\perp Y\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Proposition}}: conditioning on mediator
  variable blocks association flow \[(T \perp Y) | M\]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The path is blocked
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Chains: Example}
\protect\phantomsection\label{chains-example}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Example}}: consider
  \(Study \to SolveProblems \to JobPromotion\)
\item
  \(JobPromotion\) is causally dependent on \(Study\)
\item
  The association goes both ways

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The more you study, the more likely to get a promotion
  \item
    The greater the chances of promotion, the greater your chance of
    having studied (otherwise it would be difficult to get a promotion)
  \item
    So \(Study\) and \(JobPromotion\) are:

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Dependent
    \item
      Not independent \(Study \cancel \perp JobPromotion\)
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  If you hold the intermediary variable fixed, i.e., conditioning on
  \(M\), \emph{``looking for only people with the same skills at problem
  solving''}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The dependency is blocked
  \item
    \(T\) and \(Y\) are independent given \(M\), i.e.,
    \((T \perp Y) | M\)
  \item
    \(\EE[Y | T, M] = \EE[Y | M]\)
  \item
    Also the inverse \(\EE[T | Y, M] = \EE[T | M]\)
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Forks}
\protect\phantomsection\label{forks}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: a fork is a causal graph
  \(T \leftarrow X \rightarrow Y\):

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(X\) is a ``confounder'' (common cause of \(T\) and \(Y\))
  \end{itemize}
\item
  E.g.,
  \(AnswerQuestion \leftarrow GoodProgrammer \rightarrow GoodEmployee\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    For this reason in tech interviews you are asked to solve problems
    that might you never find in the job you apply for (e.g., ``invert a
    binary tree'')
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Proposition}}: \(T\) and \(Y\) are
  dependent \(T \not\perp Y\), even though neither causes the other

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Association flows backward through the arrows
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Proposition}}: conditioning on the common
  cause blocks the association: \[T \cancel \perp Y, (T \perp Y) | X\]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{\textcolor{violet}{Example}}: if you know that you are a
    good programmer (e.g., you have an impressive resume), asking you a
    tech question won't give new information about if you are a good
    employee or not
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Collider}
\protect\phantomsection\label{collider}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: a collider is a causal graph
  \(T \rightarrow X \leftarrow Y\):

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(X\) is a ``collider'' (two arrows collide at it)
  \item
    \(T\) and \(Y\) are independent: \(T \perp Y\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Proposition}}: conditioning on \(X\)
  makes \(T\) and \(Y\) dependent: \(T \cancel \perp Y | X\) and opens
  the path

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Aka ``explaining away'': one cause explains the effect, making the
    other cause less likely
  \item
    Opens the path if you condition on any descendant of the collider
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Example}}:
  \(Statistics \rightarrow Promotion \leftarrow FlatteringBoss\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    There are two ways to get a job promotion

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      You can be good at statistics
    \item
      You can flatter your boss
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    If I don't know if you get or not the promotion (i.e., I don't
    condition on \(Promotion\)) your level of statistics and flattering
    are independent
  \item
    If I know you got promoted, and I know you are bad at statistics,
    then you must be good at flattering your boss
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{D-separation}
\protect\phantomsection\label{d-separation}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Theorem}}: a path is \emph{blocked} given a
  conditioning set \(Z\) if and only if either condition holds:

  \begin{enumerate}
  \tightlist
  \item
    It contains a non-collider that has been conditioned on
  \item
    It contains a collider that has not been conditioned on and has no
    descendants that have been conditioned on
  \end{enumerate}
\item
  Otherwise the path is \emph{open} (nodes can be dependent)
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Theorem}}: two nodes \(A\) and \(B\) are
  \emph{d-separated} (conditionally independent given \(Z\)) if and only
  if all the paths between them are blocked
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identification}
\protect\phantomsection\label{identification}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: \emph{identification} is the
  process of isolating causal relationship between treatment \(T\) and
  outcome \(Y\)
\item
  Using causal graphs:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    You can understand where the bias is coming from and thus how to
    neutralize it
  \item
    Identification phase becomes equivalent to ``close all undesirable
    flows of association''
  \end{itemize}
\item
  After all sources of association are closed, the treatment behaves as
  if it was ``randomized''
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backdoor Adjustment}
\protect\phantomsection\label{backdoor-adjustment}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: \emph{backdoor adjustment} means
  closing backdoor paths by conditioning on confounders \(X\) makes the
  causal quantity identifiable \(Y\) from observable data
\item
  You are inducing the ``conditional independence assumption'', i.e.,
  \((Y_0, Y_1) \perp T | X\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    I.e., units with the same level of covariates \(X\) have potential
    outcomes that are the same on average (it's as if they were
    randomized)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  You can then use the adjustment formula \begingroup
  \small
  $$
  ATE
    = \EE_X[ \EE[Y | T=1] - \EE[Y | T=0 ]
    = \sum_x (\EE[Y | T=1, X=x] - \EE[Y | T=0, X=x]) \Pr(X=x)
  $$
  \endgroup

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Stratify by confounders \(X=x\)
  \item
    ATE is the weighted average of in-group \(X=x\) treated-vs-control
    differences, weighted by group size \(\Pr(X=x)\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Assumption}}: (Positivity): conditional
  probability of treatment must be \(0 < \Pr(T | X) < 1\) (all groups of
  X must have both treated and control units)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Confounding Bias}
\protect\phantomsection\label{confounding-bias}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: confounding happens when there
  is an open backdoor path through which association flows non-causally

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    E.g., treatment and outcome share a common cause
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{orange}{Problem}}: it's difficult to adjust for all
  common causes

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Some causes are unknown
  \item
    Known causes might be difficult to measure
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Example}}: does new management training
  program increase employers' engagement?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{\textcolor{teal}{Problem}}: training is optional

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Only managers who are already doing a great job attend the program
    \item
      The managers that need the training the most, don't attend
    \item
      When you measure engagement of the teams, engagement under
      managers who took the training is much higher
    \item
      There is a common cause between treatment and outcome, so they
      move together regardless of a causal effect
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    \textbf{\textcolor{cyan}{Solution}}: need to control for manager's
    quality before taking the training
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

    E.g., you can't measure confounder ``manager quality''
  \end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Selection bias}
\protect\phantomsection\label{selection-bias}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: bias introduced when the
  analysis conditions on a variable that is:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    a collider
  \item
    a mediator
  \item
    post-treatment variable
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Selection bias is not only about ``bad sampling'', e.g.,

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Analyze only survivors (survivorship bias)
  \item
    Analyze only adherent patients
  \item
    Self-selection
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Caveat}}: Can occur even with
  randomization
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Solution}}: condition on variables causing
  selection

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Cannot fully eliminate bias when:

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Things causing selection are unmeasured/unmeasurable
    \item
      Treatment or outcome causes selection directly
    \item
      Unmeasured common cause between selection and outcome
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bank Loan Risk: Adjustment Formula}
\protect\phantomsection\label{bank-loan-risk-adjustment-formula}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Assumption}}: assuming conditional
  independence \((Y_0, Y_1) \perp T | X\), use the adjustment formula \[
  ATE = \EE_X [
    \EE[Y | T=1, X=x] - \EE[Y | T=0, X=x]
  ]
  \]
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Problem}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Need partition data into segments defined by feature \(X\)
  \item
    If \(X\) has many values (e.g., a vector of 10 variables, each with
    3 values), face curse of dimensionality (sparsity issues)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Need data points with same 10 characteristics and different
      treatments
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Solution}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Model potential outcome \(Y\) as linear regression on \(X\)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Require treatment and control to be comparable ``on average''
    \item
      Functions as dimensionality reduction algorithm
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Using Regression to Estimate Causal Effects}
\protect\phantomsection\label{using-regression-to-estimate-causal-effects}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Under appropriate hypotheses (e.g., randomization, known confounders),
  regression:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Estimates treatment effects (ATE)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Intercept: control group mean
    \item
      Treatment coefficient: average difference
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Has causal interpretation
  \item
    Computes statistics (confidence intervals, p-values)
  \item
    Matches manual group average computation
  \end{itemize}
\item
  When confounders are observed, include them as regressors to adjust
\item
  In a randomized experiment, no adjustment is required
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Regression theory}
\protect\phantomsection\label{regression-theory}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  The vector of \(\beta^*\) minimizes mean squared error \[
  \beta^* = \argmin_{\beta} \EE[(Y_i - \beta^T X_i)^2]
  \]
\item
  It is estimated as \(\hat{\beta} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T Y\)
\item
  Single variable regression estimates how treatment and outcome move
  together, scaling by variance of treatment
\item
  Multivariate regression: each coefficient is the regressor's
  coefficient accounting for other variables

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Regress against confounders and extract residual
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Extrapolate model outside data region

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Risky since you don't know what happens without data points
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{FWL Theorem}
\protect\phantomsection\label{fwl-theorem}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  The Frisch--Waugh--Lovell (FWL) theorem explains how to interpret
  coefficients in a multiple regression by ``partialling out'' other
  variables
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Example}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Consider \(y = X_1 \beta_1 + X_2 \beta_2 + \varepsilon\)
  \item
    The coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_1\) from the full regression is equal
    to the coefficient obtained by:

    \begin{enumerate}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Regressing \(y\) on \(X_2\), getting residuals \(r_y\) (remove
      part of \(y\) explained by \(X_2\))
    \item
      Regressing each column of \(X_1\) on \(X_2\), getting residuals
      \(r_{X_1}\) (remove part of \(X_1\) explained by \(X_2\))
    \item
      Regressing \(r_y\) on \(r_{X_1}\)
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Intuition}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(\beta_1\) measures the effect of \(X_1\) on \(y\), holding \(X_2\)
    constant
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{FWL Theorem}
\protect\phantomsection\label{fwl-theorem-1}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  Make non-experimental data look as if the treatment was randomized
  (de-biasing)
\item
  Estimating a multi-variate regression is equivalent to the 3 steps:

  \begin{enumerate}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Denoising: Regress outcome \(Y\) on the confounder variables to
    obtain the outcome residuals \(\tilde{Y}\)
  \item
    Debiasing: Regress the treatment \(T\) on confounders \(X\) to
    obtain the treatment residuals \(\tilde{T}\)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Version of the treatment that is uncorrelated with the variables
      used in debiasing the model
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Regress outcome residuals \(\tilde{Y}\) on treatment residuals
    \(\tilde{T}\) to obtain estimate of causal effect of \(T\) on \(Y\)
  \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Non-linear Estimate}
\protect\phantomsection\label{non-linear-estimate}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  You want to estimate the causal effect of credit limit on credit card
  spend, using wage as only confounder

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    It can be that the relationship is not linear

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      E.g., the effect of credit lines on spend diminishes as you
      increase credit lines
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  You can apply some transform to \(X\) (e.g., square root, log)
\item
  Then you can apply the usual flow (adding confounders, FWL theorem)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conditionally Random Experiments}
\protect\phantomsection\label{conditionally-random-experiments}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Best alternative to a randomized controlled trial is ``stratified
  random experiments''

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Create local experiments from different distributions based on
    covariates
  \item
    E.g., use features like credit score to create customer groups
    (high-risk, neutral, low-risk)
  \item
    Randomize credit lines for high-risk from a low average distribution
  \item
    Randomize credit lines for low-risk from a higher average
    distribution
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Stratified random experiments are:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Cheaper
  \item
    More complex than completely random experiments
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Simplify by:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Lower number of groups
  \item
    Bigger overlap in treatment distributions across groups
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Use dummy variables (One-Hot Encoding):

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Model estimates one intercept per group, shared slope across all
    groups
  \item
    Shared slope is variance-weighted average of regression slopes
    within each group
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Saturated regression model includes interaction terms between
  treatment and dummy variables to estimate separate effects for each
  group
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Noise-Reducing vs Noise-Inducing Controls}
\protect\phantomsection\label{noise-reducing-vs-noise-inducing-controls}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Problem}}: Should we include every available
  variable in our regression model?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Some variables are confounders (must include to eliminate bias)
  \item
    Some variables don't cause either treatment or outcome
  \item
    Question: Which additional variables help or hurt precision?
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.5\linewidth}
\textbf{\textcolor{orange}{Noise-Reducing Controls}}

Variables that \emph{predict the outcome well} but aren't confounders

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Example}: Credit score bucket \(\to\) credit limit \(\to\)
  default

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{credit\_score1\_buckets}}: confounder (must
    include)
  \item
    \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{credit\_score2}}: predicts default but not
    treatment
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \emph{Effect}: Reduces variance in \(\varepsilon_i\) by FWL denoising
  step
\item
  \emph{Result}: Same \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{treatment}}\), much tighter
  confidence intervals

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Standard error shrinks substantially
  \item
    More statistical power
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \emph{Strategy}: Always include strong outcome predictors
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{column}{0.5\linewidth}
\textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Noise-Inducing Controls}}

Variables that \emph{predict the treatment well} but not the outcome

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Example}: Conditionally random spend experiment

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{credit\_score1}}: drives treatment
    assignment but \(\perp\) outcome
  \item
    Incorrectly including it increases variance
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \emph{Effect}: Reduces treatment variance by explaining away
  \(\text{Var}(T)\)
\item
  \emph{Result}: Same \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{treatment}}\), but standard
  error \emph{increases}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Wider confidence intervals
  \item
    Less statistical power
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \emph{Strategy}: Avoid including strong treatment predictors
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Propensity score}
\protect\phantomsection\label{propensity-score}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: \emph{propensity score} is the
  conditional probability of receiving treatment given covariates: \[
  e(x) = \Pr(T=1 | X)
  \]
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Intuition}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Propensity score acts as dimensionality reduction technique
  \item
    Instead of conditioning on high-dimensional \(X\), condition on
    single propensity score
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Claim}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    If two managers have same propensity score but different treatment
    status, difference is due to chance
  \item
    Treatment should become ``as good as random'' after controlling for
    propensity score
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{FWL Orthogonalization and Propensity Score}
\protect\phantomsection\label{fwl-orthogonalization-and-propensity-score}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Both FWL approach and propensity score orthogonalize treatment but use
  different models for \(T\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    FWL theorem: linear regression models \[\EE[T | X]\] in debiasing
    step

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Residualize both \(T\) and \(X\)
    \item
      Low-dim \(X\), linear T-X relationship
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Propensity score estimation achieves similar orthogonalization

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Include propensity score \(\hat{e}(X)\) as control in regression
    \item
      E.g.,
      \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{engagement\_score ~ intervention + propensity\_score}}
    \item
      High-dim \(X\), flexible T-X relationship
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Propensity score matching}
\protect\phantomsection\label{propensity-score-matching}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  Matches treated and control units based on propensity score similarity
\item
  Essentially 1-Nearest Neighbors (i.e., KNN with K=1) on propensity
  score

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Fit KNN on treated units to impute \(Y_1\) for controls
  \item
    Fit KNN on control units to impute \(Y_0\) for treated
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Matched value is outcome of nearest unit \[
  \hat{ATE}
  = \frac{1}{N}\sum (
    ( {Y_{i} - Y_{jm}(i)} )T_{i} + 
    ( {Y_{jm}(i) - Y_{i}} )( 1 - T_{i} )
  )
  \]
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Cons}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    KNN inefficient in high dimensions
  \item
    Difficult to derive variance
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Inverse Propensity Weighting (IPW)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{inverse-propensity-weighting-ipw}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Core Principle}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Re-weight data by inverse probability of observed treatment
  \item
    Create pseudo-population where treatment appears randomly assigned
  \end{itemize}
\item
  For treated units:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Up-weight those unlikely to be treated (low \(e(x)\))
  \item
    They're informative, they look like untreated units but got
    treatment anyway, so they reveal what would happen to
  \end{itemize}
\item
  For control units:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Up-weight those likely to be treated (high \(e(x)\))
  \item
    Down-weights common treatment cases: Treated units with high
    propensity (likely to be treated) get lower weight, since they're
    less informative.
  \end{itemize}

  \[E[Y_t] = \EE[ \frac{\mathbf{1}(T=t)Y}{P(T=t|X)}]\]
\item
  The same scaling can be applied to recover the ATE: \[
  ATE
  = \EE\left[
      \frac{\mathbb{1}(T=1)Y}{\Pr(T=1|X)}
    \right]
    - \EE\left[
      \frac{\mathbb{1}(T=0)Y}{\Pr(T=0|X)}
      \right]
  \]
\item
  IPW is similar to importance weighting
\item
  To estimate the variance use bootstrap method (resample with
  replacement multiple times)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Fit IPW estimator on each sample
  \item
    Calculate 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles for 95\% CI
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stabilized Propensity Weights}
\protect\phantomsection\label{stabilized-propensity-weights}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Problems}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Raw inverse propensity weights can be very large
  \item
    Treatment with low probability \(\Pr(T | X)\) creates extreme
    weights
  \item
    Computational stability issue
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Solution}}: Stabilized propensity
  weights \[
  w = \frac{\Pr(T = t)}{\Pr(T = t | X)}
  \]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Balances small denominator with also small numerator
  \item
    Produces more stable weights without changing balancing properties
  \item
    Sum of weights in each group matches original group sizes
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Trim propensity scores to avoid extreme weights

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    E.g., keep between 1\% and 99\%
  \item
    Trade-off: slight bias increase for substantial variance reduction
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Visual intuition}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Plot propensity score distributions by treatment status
  \item
    Before weighting: distributions shifted (confounding)
  \item
    After weighting: distributions overlap (treatment appears random)
  \item
    Overlapping distributions essential for IPW validity
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Propensity Score for Selection Bias}
\protect\phantomsection\label{propensity-score-for-selection-bias}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  IPW originally developed (Horvitz-Thompson 1952) for selection bias

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Can adjust for non-response in surveys
  \item
    Can handle both confounding and selection together
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Example}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    You send a survey asking people to rate your product on a 1-to-5
    scale
  \item
    Survey respondents may differ from non-respondents

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      E.g., non-respondents are often unsatisfied customers and vice
      versa
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Estimate probability of responding given customer's covariates
    (e.g., age, income, app usage) \(\Pr(R = 1 | X)\)
  \item
    Re-weight respondents by \(1/\hat{\Pr}(R = 1)\) to account for who
    didn't respond
  \item
    Creates pseudo-population representing full population
  \end{itemize}
\item
  You can also compute the weights for both selection and confounding by
  multiplying the re-weighting and can stability using the marginal
  probability \[
  W = \frac{
    \hat{\Pr}(T = t)
  }{
    \hat{\Pr}(R = 1 | X)\hat{\Pr}(T = t | X)
  }
  \]
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Positivity}
\protect\phantomsection\label{positivity}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Positivity}} means that every unit has
  non-zero probability of receiving each treatment level \[
  \Pr(T = t | X) > 0 \text{ for all } t, X
  \]
\item
  Essential for IPW validity
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Problems}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Some subgroups have almost no treated or control units
  \item
    Cannot estimate counterfactual outcomes in those regions
  \item
    IPW fails when positivity violated: cannot reweight units that don't
    exist
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{IPW Sensitivity}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Propensity score distribution easy to visualize
\item
  Can directly check if treatment groups overlap
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Comparison with Regression}
\begin{itemize}

\item

\item
  Can recover ATE even with positivity violation if model specification

\item

\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From ATE to CATE}
\protect\phantomsection\label{from-ate-to-cate}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Average Treatment Effect (ATE) \(\EE[Y_1 - Y_0]\) answers:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{``Should we roll out a treatment to entire population?''}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE) \(\EE[Y_1 - Y_0 | X]\)
  answers

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{``Which units should we treat?''}
  \item
    \emph{``What's the optimal treatment for each unit?''}
  \item
    Enables personalization, since each unit is different in terms of
    its covariate \(X\)
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{CATE}
\protect\phantomsection\label{cate}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  You have customers and a treatment

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(Y\) = outcome (purchase amount, satisfaction, churn probability,
    etc.)
  \item
    \(T\) = treatment (discount, advertisement exposure, recommendation,
    pricing change, etc.)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  You want to personalize the treatment (e.g., different discounts to
  different customers)
\item
  It's like customer segmentation: create groups of customers based on
  how they respond to your treatment

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    E.g., some customers reacts well to discounts, others poorly
  \end{itemize}
\item
  We want to measure ``customer's response to a treatment'' (aka
  treatment effect or marginal effect):
  \[\frac{\delta Y}{\delta T}\Big|_{X=x}\] how the outcome Y changes
  when the treatment T changes slightly, holding other factors fixed
\item
  High / medium / low treatment effect (group with high slope)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Prediction Is Not the Answer}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-prediction-is-not-the-answer}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Predictive models estimate outcome \(Y = f(X, T)\), not treatment
  sensitivity \[\frac{\delta Y}{\delta T}\Big|_{X=x}\]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Partitioning units by \(Y\) does not partition by treatment effect
  \item
    Example: high sales prediction does not guarantee high discount
    sensitivity
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Treatment slopes are non-observable at unit level

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Fundamental problem: cannot observe same unit under multiple
    treatment conditions
  \item
    Therefore cannot directly compute treatment sensitivity and ML can't
    predict it
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Restaurant Discount: Example}
\protect\phantomsection\label{restaurant-discount-example}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A chain of restaurants that operate across the country. A key
  component of this business is
\item
  Understanding when it should give discounts to its customers
\item
  The company ran a nationwide experiment that lasted three years, where
  it randomized discounts in six different restaurants in the chain

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The unit of treatment is (day, restaurant)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Linear regression with treatment-covariate interactions estimates CATE

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Model: \[
    y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t_i + \beta_2 X_i + \beta_3 t_i X_i + e_i
    \]
  \item
    Slope prediction: \[
    \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial t_i} = \beta_1 + \beta_3 X_i
    \]
  \end{itemize}
\item
  The interaction term \(t_i \cdot X_i\) (treatment × covariate) is the
  simplest way to make the slope depend on the covariate

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Each unit with different \(X_i\) gets a different slope prediction
    despite unobservable slopes.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  You can estimate the fit and then use the coefficients to compute the
  CATE

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    A shortcut is to use CATE = prediction(t+1) - prediction(t)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  If the treatment was not randomized, then you will need to do
  identification removing confounding
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Evaluating CATE Predictions}
\protect\phantomsection\label{evaluating-cate-predictions}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  This is similar to a ML problem, but you don't have the actual
  outcomes (since they are not observable)
\item
  Fundamental problem: \(Y_{1i}\) and \(Y_{0i}\) are never observed for
  the same unit
\item
  Cannot compute standard ML metrics (e.g., MSE on \(\tau_i\)) \[
  \tau(x) \defeq \EE[Y_1 - Y_0 | X = x]
  \]
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  Ground truth is non-observable at unit level, so evaluation must use
  group-level metrics

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Instead: partition data by model predictions and estimate effects in
    each partition
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Use train/test split for unbiased evaluation of CATE model on held-out
  data
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Process:}
\protect\phantomsection\label{process}
\begin{enumerate}
\item
  Fit the CATE model on training data

\begin{verbatim}
regr_model = smf.ols("sales ~ discounts*(month + weekday + ...)",
                     data=train).fit()
\end{verbatim}
\item
  Predict CATE values on test data

\begin{verbatim}
test_pred = regr_model.predict(test.assign(discounts=test["discounts"]+1)) \
           - regr_model.predict(test)
\end{verbatim}
\item
  Partition test data (by quantiles of those predictions)

\begin{verbatim}
# Divide test data into 10 groups based on predicted CATE values
quantiles = pd.qcut(test_pred, q=10)
# Low quantile: days with low predicted CATE
# High quantile: days with high predicted CATE
\end{verbatim}
\item
  Estimate group effects (regression within each group)

\begin{verbatim}
effect = regress(sales ~ discounts)
\end{verbatim}
\item
  Evaluate (do the effects match the predictions?)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    If your CATE predictions are good:

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Quantile 1 (low prediction) → low actual effect
    \item
      Quantile 10 (high prediction) → high actual effect
    \item
      Monotonic staircase pattern = good model
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Random model produces flat curve (same effect in all quantiles =
    ATE)
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cumulative Effect}
\protect\phantomsection\label{cumulative-effect}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Sort data by CATE score, accumulate groups sequentially

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Estimate effect on top 1\%, then top 2\%, then top 3\%, etc
  \item
    Result: curve showing effect by cumulative sample percentage
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Well-ordered model curve shape: starts high, gradually converges to
  ATE

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Bad model: quickly converges to ATE or fluctuates around it
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cumulative Gain}
\protect\phantomsection\label{cumulative-gain}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: cumulative gain measures how
  much value a CATE model captures by sequentially treating units with
  highest predicted effects
\item
  Multiply cumulative effect by cumulative sample proportion: \[
  \text{Gain} = \frac{N_{\text{cumulative}}}{N_{\text{total}}} \times (\text{effect} - ATE)
  \]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Downscales early portion where sample size (and confidence) is small
  \item
    Centers around zero, with gains relative to population average
    effect
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Interpretation}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Well-ordered model: steep initial climb, then plateaus near ATE
  \item
    Random model: flat line (no gain above ATE anywhere)
  \item
    Enables visual model comparison on a single plot
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Area Under Curve (AUC)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{area-under-curve-auc}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}: area under the cumulative gain
  curve aggregates CATE model quality into a single summary metric
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Intuition}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Larger AUC = better CATE ordering
  \item
    AUC = 0 if model predicts constant effect (equivalent to random
    assignment)
  \item
    AUC \textgreater{} 0 indicates true heterogeneity in treatment
    response
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Application}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Automates model selection among competing CATE approaches
  \item
    Enables hyperparameter tuning via grid search or Bayesian
    optimization
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Metalearners}
\protect\phantomsection\label{metalearners}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Goal}}: Estimate conditional average treatment
  effect (CATE) to identify how unit \(i\) responds differently to
  treatment \[\tau_i(x) \triangleq \EE[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) | X]\]
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Definition}}: metalearners leverage / recycle
  off-the-shelf machine learning algorithms for treatment effect
  estimation

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Can estimate ATE but mainly used for CATE estimation
  \item
    Work well with high-dimensional data
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Often this is a \emph{ranking problem}: correctly order units by
  treatment sensitivity

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Discrete treatments: binary or categorical
  \item
    Continuous treatments: dose, price, discount level
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{T-Learner}
\protect\phantomsection\label{t-learner}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Approach}}: Fit separate outcome models for
  each treatment level

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    For binary treatment: estimate \(\mu_0(x) = \EE[Y | T=0, X]\) and
    \(\mu_1(x) = \EE[Y | T=1, X]\)
  \item
    Predict both potential outcomes for each unit and then estimate CATE
    as: \[\hat{\tau}(x)_i = \hat{\mu}_1(X_i) - \hat{\mu}_0(X_i)\]
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Advantages}}: Simple and straightforward
  implementation
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Disadvantage}}: Prone to regularization bias
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Regularization Bias}
\protect\phantomsection\label{regularization-bias}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Problem}}: In T-learner you need to fit two
  models at the same time

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    If treated data is sparse, \(mu_1\) becomes overly regularized and
    simple (e.g., a constant)
  \item
    If untreated data is abundant, \(mu_0\) can fit a non-linearity
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Result}}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Bias in CATE estimation (nonlinear difference minus linear
    difference)
  \item
    E.g., model with nonlinear outcome but constant treatment effect
    produces wrong nonlinear CATE
  \item
    In imbalanced data, regularization creates artificial heterogeneity
    // TODO: add picture
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{X-Learner}
\protect\phantomsection\label{x-learner}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Approach}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Address regularization bias of T-learner
  \item
    Two-stage approach with propensity score weighting
  \item
    Performs better when one treatment group is much larger than the
    other
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{orange}{Stage 1}}: Fit initial outcome models (same
  as T-learner)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(\mu_0(X)\) and \(\mu_1(X)\) for control and treated groups
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Stage 2}}: Estimate imputed treatment
  effects

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    For control units: estimate missing treatment outcome:
    \[\hat{\tau}(X, T=0) = \hat{\mu}_1(X, T=0) - Y_{T=0}\]
  \item
    For treated units: estimate missing control outcome:
    \[\hat{\tau}(X, T=1) = Y_{T=1} - \hat{\mu}_0(X, T=1)\]
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{teal}{Stage 3}}: Fit effect models

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Fit \(\hat{\mu}_{\tau 0}(x)\) to predict \(\hat{\tau}(X, T=0)\)
  \item
    Fit \(\hat{\mu}_{\tau 1}(x)\) to predict \(\hat{\tau}(X, T=1)\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{cyan}{Pros}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    X-learner shines when large imbalance between treatment and control
    groups
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{X-Learner With Weighting Scheme}
\protect\phantomsection\label{x-learner-with-weighting-scheme}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  Combine second-stage models using propensity score
  \(\hat{e}(x) = \Pr(T=1|X)\) \[\hat{\tau}(x) 
  = \hat{\mu}_{\tau 0}(x) \cdot \hat{e}(x) + 
    \hat{\mu}_{\tau 1}(x) \cdot (1 - \hat{e}(x))\]
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Intuition}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    When treatment is rare: \(\hat{e}(x)\) is small, down-weights flawed
    \(\hat{\mu}_{\tau 0}\)
  \item
    When treatment is common: \(1 - \hat{e}(x)\) is large, up-weights
    correct \(\hat{\mu}_{\tau 1}\)
  \item
    Automatically emphasizes the model trained on larger sub-sample
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Metalearners for Continuous Treatments}
\protect\phantomsection\label{metalearners-for-continuous-treatments}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Problem}}: Randomized discount experiment at
  restaurant chain

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Goal: which days should discounts be highest?
  \item
    Treatment: discount amount (continuous, 0-40\%)
  \item
    Outcome: sales volume one day later
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Insight}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Customers more sensitive to prices on some days (e.g., holidays)
  \item
    CATE tells us how much demand increases per unit discount
  \item
    High CATE days \(\Rightarrow\) use more discounts
  \item
    Low CATE days \(\Rightarrow\) use fewer
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Problem}}
\item
  Discrete approaches fail because they

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Can't fit separate models for each discount level (infinite
    treatments)
  \item
    Need methods that handle continuous treatment as a feature or
    component
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{violet}{Solution}}: S-learner and Double-ML
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{S-Learner}
\protect\phantomsection\label{s-learner}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Approach}}: Single machine learning model that
  includes treatment \(T\) and covariates \(X\) to predict outcome \(Y\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Goal is to estimate \(\mu_s(x, t) = E[Y | T, X]\)
  \item
    ``S-Learner'' since it's ``single''
  \item
    This model doesn't output treatment effect, rather it outputs
    counterfactual predictions (i.e., predictions under different
    treatment regimes)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{orange}{For binary treatment}}: CATE =
  \(\mu_s(X_i, T=1) - \mu_s(X_i, T=0)\)
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{For continuous treatment}}: Extended
  procedure needed

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Create grid of treatment values (e.g., {[}0, 10, 20, 30, 40{]} for
    discounts)
  \item
    Compute predictions for all treatment level
  \item
    Summarize as slope via simple linear regression to extract CATE
  \item
    Formula: \(\beta = \text{Cov}(t, y) / \text{Var}(t)\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{teal}{Pros}}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Simple
  \item
    Supports both binary and continuous treatments
  \item
    Performs reasonably well
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{cyan}{Cons}}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Biases treatment effect toward zero
  \item
    Treatment variable can be completely discarded if weak relative to
    other covariates
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Solution}}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Use Double/Debiased Machine Learning (R-learner) to address bias
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{S-Learner: Implementation Steps}
\protect\phantomsection\label{s-learner-implementation-steps}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Step 1}}: Train single model on all data

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textcolor{blue}{\texttt{s\_learner.fit(X + [T], y)}} where T is
    treatment feature
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Step 2}}: Create counterfactual grid

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Define treatment grid: e.g., \([0, 10, 20, 30, 40]\)
  \item
    Cross-join with test set: each test unit at each treatment level
  \item
    Makes predictions on expanded grid
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Step 3}}: Extract slopes

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Group by unit, apply linear regression
  \item
    Extract slope coefficient as unit-level CATE
  \item
    Can also use slope formula directly:
    \(\hat{\beta} = \text{Cov}(t,y) / \text{Var}(t)\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{violet}{Result}}: CATE prediction for each unit
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{S-Learner: The Bias Problem}
\protect\phantomsection\label{s-learner-the-bias-problem}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Problem}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    ML models are regularized by default
  \item
    Regularization can suppress treatment effect unless treatment signal
    is very strong
  \item
    ML model may discard treatment feature entirely
  \item
    Distribution of estimated ATE is shifted toward zero compared to
    true effect
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Solution}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Use orthogonalization techniques
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{R-learner (Double/Debiased Machine Learning)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{r-learner-doubledebiased-machine-learning}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{The principle}}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Use ML to model confounding relationships
  \item
    Orthogonalize treatment and outcome from confounders
  \item
    Extract treatment effect from residuals
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{The equation}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(\widetilde{Y}_i = Y_i - \hat{\mu}_y(X_i)\) (outcome residual)
  \item
    \(\widetilde{T}_i = T_i - \hat{\mu}_t(X_i)\) (treatment residual)
  \item
    Regress residuals:
    \(\widetilde{Y}_i = \tau \widetilde{T}_i + \varepsilon_i\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Why it works}}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(\hat{\mu}_y\) captures confounding effects on outcome
  \item
    \(\hat{\mu}_t\) captures confounding effects on treatment
  \item
    Residuals are ``as if'' treatment was randomized
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{violet}{Key requirement}}: no unobserved
  confounders
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Double-ML: the Overfitting Problem}
\protect\phantomsection\label{double-ml-the-overfitting-problem}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Stage 1}}: Estimate nuisance parameters

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Focus on treatment effect \(\tau\) (or CATE \(\tau(X)\))
  \item
    Estimate nuisance parameters (what we are not interested in) to
    obtain parameter of interest
  \item
    \emph{Step 1}: Estimate outcome model \(\hat{\mu}_y(X)\)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Fit ML regression model to predict outcome \(Y\) from features
      \(X\)
    \item
      E.g., capture nonlinear confounding relationships between \(X\)
      and \(Y\)
    \item
      Use cross-validation predictions to prevent overfitting
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    \emph{Step 2}: Estimate treatment model \(\hat{\mu}_t(X)\)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Fit ML regression model to predict treatment \(T\) from features
      \(X\)
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{darkgreen}{Stage 2}}: Compute residuals

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Step 3}: Residualize outcome
    \(\widetilde{Y} = Y - \hat{\mu}_y(X)\)
  \item
    \emph{Step 4}: Residualize treatment
    \(\widetilde{T} = T - \hat{\mu}_t(X)\)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Residuals represent part of \(Y\) and \(T\) independent of \(X\)
    \item
      Confounding is removed and can estimate treatment effect without
      bias
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Stage 3}}: Construct the causal target

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Step 5}: Create transformed outcome
    \(Y^* = \frac{\widetilde{Y}}{\widetilde{T}}\)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Dividing gives ``slope'' of relationship between treatment and
      outcome residuals, i.e., estimate of \(\tau(X_i)\)
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    \emph{Step 6}: Create weights \(w_i = \widetilde{T}_i^2\)
  \item
    Dividing by \(\widetilde{T}\) introduces noise
  \item
    Squared weights down-weight noisy estimates, up-weight stable
    estimates
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{violet}{Stage 4}}: Estimate the CATE Model

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Step 7}: Fit final ML model to predict \(Y^*\) from \(X\),
    using weights \(w_i\)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Train ML regression model on \((Y^*, X)\)
    \item
      Use weights \(w_i\) for stable, low-noise predictions
    \item
      Learn how treatment effect varies with features \(X\)
    \item
      Model predictions give heterogeneous treatment effects
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Double-ML for CATE Estimation}
\protect\phantomsection\label{double-ml-for-cate-estimation}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Allow causal parameter to vary with covariates \(\tau(X_i)\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Outcome model:
    \[Y_i = \hat{\mu}_y(X_i) + \tau(X_i) \cdot \widetilde{T}_i + \varepsilon_i\]
  \end{itemize}
\item
  You can isolate the error, which is called ``R-loss'' and can be used
  as a causal objective function
  \[\hat{L}_n(\tau(x)) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\widetilde{Y}_i - \tau(X_i)
  \widetilde{T}_i)^2\]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Minimizing this loss directly estimates CATE
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Data Structures: Definition}
\protect\phantomsection\label{data-structures-definition}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Data can have different temporal and cross-sectional patterns

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Causal relationships can be estimated depending on the data
    structure
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Definition}}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Cross-sectional data}: multiple units observed at a single
    point in time

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      E.g., snapshot of sales across restaurants with/without discounts
    \item
      E.g., student test scores across schools in a single academic year
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    \emph{Panel data} (aka \emph{longitudinal data}): same units
    observed repeatedly over time

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      E.g., same restaurants before/after discount implementation
    \item
      E.g., same students' test scores tracked across multiple year
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    \emph{Repeated cross-sectional data}: different units observed at
    multiple time points

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      E.g., different restaurant samples in year 1 and year 2
    \item
      E.g., different student samples from schools surveyed in 2020 and
      2025
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    \emph{Pooled cross-section}: multiple independent cross-sections
    combined and analyzed as a single dataset

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      E.g., multiple cross-sections treated as one large cross-section
    \item
      E.g., combining student test score data from 2020, 2022, and 2024
      into one regression
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Panel}
\protect\phantomsection\label{panel}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Motivation}}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Most real-world interventions (policy, marketing, etc.) are
    difficult to randomize

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      E.g., in marketing it's difficult to control who receives the
      treatment (i.e., see your advertisement)
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Panel data offers two sources of variation

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      \emph{Across units}: compare treated and control cities (spatial
      variation)
    \item
      \emph{Across time}: compare pre- and post-treatment periods
      (temporal variation)
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Key insight}}: By tracking units over time,
  we can remove confounders that are fixed at the unit level

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Even with unmeasured confounders, we can identify causal effects
  \item
    This makes panel data \emph{as good as it gets} for observational
    causal inference
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Canonical Difference-in-Differences: The Setup}
\protect\phantomsection\label{canonical-difference-in-differences-the-setup}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Notation}}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(D_i = 1\) if unit \(i\) is treated, \(0\) otherwise
  \item
    \(t \leq T_{pre}\) is pre-intervention period
  \item
    \(t > T_{pre}\) is post-intervention
  \item
    \(\text{Post}_t = 1\) if \(t > T_{pre}\), otherwise \(0\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Definition}}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The treatment indicator is \(W_{it} = D_i \cdot \text{Post}_t\)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Equals 1 only for treated units in post-treatment period
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    The outcome variable is \(Y_{it}\) (e.g., number of downloads)

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Observable for all unit-time combinations
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    The target is the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT),
    i.e., the effect of treatment on units that received it, after
    treatment takes place: \[
    \text{ATT} = \EE[Y_{it}(1) - Y_{it}(0) | D_i = 1, t > T_{pre}]
    \]
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{DiD: Imputing the Counterfactual}
\protect\phantomsection\label{did-imputing-the-counterfactual}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  The missing piece is the treated counterfactual
  \[\EE[Y(0) | D = 1, \text{Post} = 1]\] which we estimate with: \[
  \begin{aligned}
    \EE[Y(0) | D = 1, Post = 1]
    &= \EE[Y | D = 1, Post = 0] \\
    &\quad + \left(
      \EE[Y | D = 0, Post = 1]
      - \EE[Y | D = 0, Post = 0]
    \right)
  \end{aligned}
  \]
\item
  \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Intuition}}: Start with the treated baseline,
  add the control growth

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Treated baseline}: average outcome of treated units before
    treatment
  \item
    \emph{Control growth}: how much control units changed from pre to
    post
  \item
    Assumption: This growth rate applies to treated units under the
    counterfactual
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Difference-in-Difference?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-difference-in-difference}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Replacing the estimate in ATT: \[
  \begin{aligned}
    \text{ATT}
    &= \big[
      \EE[Y | D = 1, \text{Post} = 1]
      - \EE[Y | D = 1, \text{Post} = 0]
      \big] \\
    &\quad - \big[
      \EE[Y | D = 0, \text{Post} = 1]
      - \EE[Y | D = 0, \text{Post} = 0]
      \big]
  \end{aligned}
  \]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{First bracket}: treated group's change (pre to post)
  \item
    \emph{Second bracket}: control group's change (pre to post)
  \item
    Difference of these two changes is the treatment effect
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
