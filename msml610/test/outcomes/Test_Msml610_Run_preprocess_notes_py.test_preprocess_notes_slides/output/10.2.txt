# Time Series vs. Cross-Sectional Causality

## Temporal Causal Structures

#### Why Time Series Causality Is Different

- Time series provide structure to exploit
  - The _arrow of time_ (causes precede effects) eliminates backward arrows
  - Information from cohorts of related data

- **\textcolor{red}{Univariate Time series causal inference}** compares a unit to _itself_ over time
  - E.g., compare a city's crime rate _before_ vs _after_ a new policy
  - Unit is its own control, but observations are _not_ independent

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Cross-sectional causal inference}** compares units at a single point in time
  - E.g., compare outcomes for treated vs untreated patients in one cohort
  - Assumes units are (conditionally) exchangeable

- **\textcolor{blue}{Challenges}**
  - Observations are correlated across time (autocorrelation)
  - Treatments and confounders may drift or trend
  - Often impossible to run experiments

- **\textcolor{violet}{Example}**:
  - Did the Federal Reserve rate cut in 2020 cause the stock market rebound?
  - Cannot rerun 2020 with a different policy $\to$ must use temporal structure
    to reason counterfactually

#### Temporal Causal Structures: Basic Setup

- Observe a sequence of variables over time:
  - Treatment: $T_t$ (continuous, binary)
  - Outcome: $Y_t$
  - Covariates: $\vX_t$

::: columns
:::: {.column width=50%}
- A simple **\textcolor{red}{temporal causal graph}** (first-order)

- **\textcolor{blue}{Key principle}**: _effects cannot precede causes_
  - $T_t$ can affect $Y_{t}, Y_{t+1}, ...$ but never $Y_{t-1}$
  - Eliminates half of the arrows a-priori

#### Temporal Causal Structures: Key Quantities


- **\textcolor{red}{Definition}**: The causal effect of a _sustained intervention_ starting at
  time $t^*$:
  $$
  \tau = \EE[Y_t | do(T_{t^*:t} = 1)]
       - \EE[Y_t | do(T_{t^*:t} = 0)]
  $$

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Intuition}**: average effect when we _force_ the treatment from $t^*$ onward

- **\textcolor{blue}{Definition}** _Dynamic treatment_ is when treatment at time $t$ depends on
  history
  $(\vX_{1:t}, Y_{1:t-1}, T_{1:t-1})$
  - Common in medicine: dosing depends on patient response so far

- Two common special cases:
  1. _Contemporaneous effect: $T_t \to Y_t$
  2. _Lagged effect_: $T_t \to Y_{t+k}$ for some lag $k > 0$

- **\textcolor{violet}{Example}**: monetary policy
  - Rate cut _this quarter_ affects GDP _over several quarters_ (lagged effect)
  - Fed adjusts rates based on past GDP $\to$ feedback loop

## Challenges Specific to Time Series

#### Autocorrelation

- **\textcolor{red}{Autocorrelation}**: $Y_t$ is correlated with $Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2}, ...$
  - Standard errors computed assuming i.i.d. data are _too small_
  - Significance tests reject the null far more than their nominal rate

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Intuition}**: autocorrelated data carries less _independent_ information than
  the number of observations suggests

- **\textcolor{blue}{Solution}**:
  - If quantities are autocorrelated, naive OLS standard errors mislead us
  - Fix: Newey-West or HAC (heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent)
    standard errors

- **\textcolor{violet}{Example}**: regressing daily stock returns on a dummy for "news day"

#### Non-Stationarity and Trends

- **\textcolor{red}{Definition}**: a _stationary_ process has constant mean, variance, and
  autocovariance over time
  - Many real-world series are _not_ stationary (e.g., GDP, prices, populations
    grow)

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Spurious regression}** 
  - Two series trending upward over time look correlated even if unrelated
  - E.g., number of pirates vs global temperature (both trend over
    decades) but correlation $\neq$ causation

- **\textcolor{blue}{Solutions}**:
  - Difference the series: work with $\Delta Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}$
  - Detrend: remove a fitted trend before analysis
  - Use cointegration methods if two series share a common trend

#### Feedback Loops and Simultaneity

- **\textcolor{red}{Fact}**: in time series, cause and effect often influence each other creating
  _feedback loops_

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Problem}**: this creates _simultaneity bias_ in regression
  - $Y_t$ and $T_t$ are jointly determined
  - OLS estimates are inconsistent
  - Cannot interpret coefficients as causal effects

#### Structural Vector Autoregressions (VARs)

- **\textcolor{red}{Definition}**: Model variables together, allowing simultaneous influence

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Key idea}**
  - Treat policy $T_t$ and outcome $Y_t$ symmetrically
  - Estimate response to shocks (i.e., unexpected, exogenous changes)

- **\textcolor{blue}{Mathematical formulation}**: VAR(p) model with $k$ variables
  $$
  \vY_t = \vc + A_1 \vY_{t-1} + A_2 \vY_{t-2} + ... + A_p \vY_{t-p} + \vv{\varepsilon}_t
  $$
  where:
  - $\vY_t$ = vector of $k$ time series
  - $A_i$ = coefficient matrices (dependence on lagged values)
  - $\vv{\varepsilon}_t$ = structural shocks (uncorrelated across equations)

- **\textcolor{violet}{Identification challenge}**: How do you determine causality direction?
  - Impose _structural assumptions_
    - E.g., monetary policy reacts to inflation _within_ quarter; inflation
      reacts _next_ quarter

#### Instrumental Variables (IV)

- **\textcolor{red}{Key idea}**:
  - Find a variable $Z$ that affects treatment $T$ but not outcome $Y$ directly
  - Use $Z$ to predict $T$, then measure how predicted $\hat{T}$ correlates
    with $Y$

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Advantage}**: breaks the feedback loop by providing exogenous variation in
  treatment

- **\textcolor{blue}{Problem}**: must find a credible instrument
  - E.g., studying effect of schooling on earnings, use distance to nearest
    college (affects schooling access, not earnings directly)

- **\textcolor{violet}{Limitation}**: only estimates the effect on "compliers": units whose
  treatment changes with the instrument

#### Natural Experiments

- **\textcolor{red}{Key idea}**: exploit an unexpected, external event that randomly assigns
  treatment to some units but not others
  - E.g., lottery, policy rollout, shock, discontinuity in time

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Advantage}**: randomization eliminates confounding without requiring an
  instrument

- **\textcolor{blue}{Example}**
  - Vietnam War draft lottery randomly assigns military service
  - Compare drafted vs non-drafted on lifetime earnings

- **\textcolor{violet}{Limitation}**
  - Only valid when the shock is truly exogenous and affects treatment assignment
  - Cannot replicate in observational data

#### Unobserved Confounders That Vary Over Time

- **\textcolor{red}{Problem}**
  - Time-varying confounders make identification harder
  - $U_t$ may affect _both_ $T_t$ and $Y_t$ and change slowly

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Example}**: evaluating a diet on weight loss
  - Season (winter vs summer), stress, and motivation all change over time
  - Hard to measure but drive both adherence and outcome

- **\textcolor{blue}{Solution}**
  - If a confounder is _time-invariant_ simple before-after comparisons
    difference it out
    - E.g., a person's genetics
    - This is the core idea behind DiD and fixed-effects models
  - If the confounder is _time-varying_ it contaminates the comparison even after
    differencing
    - E.g., mood, weather

#### Other Common Challenges

- **\textcolor{red}{Anticipation effects}**: units change behavior _before_ treatment if they
  expect it
  - E.g., consumers delay purchases before a known tax cut

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Spillovers / SUTVA violations}**: treatment on one unit affects another
  - E.g., advertising in one region spills to neighboring regions

- **\textcolor{blue}{Structural breaks}**: the data-generating process changes mid-sample
  - E.g., 2008 financial crisis, COVID-19 shock

- **\textcolor{violet}{Missing data and irregular sampling}**: standard time series tools assume
  equispaced observations
  - E.g., sensor dropouts, holidays, weekends

#### When Temporal Structure Helps

- **\textcolor{red}{Time orders causes before effects}**: rules out many causal directions a
  priori
  - Future cannot cause past $\to$ prunes the graph

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Pre-treatment history as a control}**: unit is its own baseline
  - Time-invariant confounders are automatically controlled for
  - E.g., genes, geography, institutional features

- **\textcolor{blue}{Natural experiments are easier to spot}**: policy changes, shocks, and
  discontinuities give quasi-experimental variation
  -  Interrupted Time Series (ITS), DiD, and synthetic control all exploit this

  - **\textcolor{violet}{Example}**: studying the effect of a sugary-drink ban
    - Compare the same city before and after
    - Genes, climate, culture are held fixed

#### When Temporal Structure Misleads

- **\textcolor{red}{Trends fake causation}** (spurious regression)
  - Two series both trending upward look causally linked even if unrelated

- **\textcolor{orange}{Seasonality hides effects}**: a holiday bump can mask a policy effect

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Lagged effects confuse attribution}**: today's outcome may reflect yesterday's
  treatment
  - Wrong lag specification $\to$ wrong effect estimate

- **\textcolor{teal}{Feedback inflates correlations}**: if $Y_t$ drives $T_{t+1}$, regressing
  $Y_t$ on $T_t$ also picks up the reverse effect

- **\textcolor{cyan}{Regime changes invalidate models}**: a model fit before COVID may not hold
  after

- **\textcolor{blue}{Key takeaway}**: temporal data is informative, but blindly applying
  cross-sectional tools to it creates more problems than it solves

# Granger Causality

#### Granger Causality: Intuition

- **\textcolor{red}{Intuition}**: Granger Causality (Nobel 2003)
  _"Does knowing the past of $X$ help me predict $Y$ beyond what $Y$'s own past
  already tells me?"_
  - Causes must come before effects, and effects should carry information about
    their causes

- "Granger-causing" (a bit of a misnomer)
  - Is _not_ causation in Pearl's do-calculus sense
  - Is a _predictive_ notion based on temporal precedence
  - Answers _"does X help predict Y?"_ not _"does changing X change Y?"_
  - $X \to Y$ and $Y \to X$ can both hold (common in economic feedback systems)

- **\textcolor{blue}{Example}**:
  - _Ice cream sales_ does not Granger-cause _drowning_
  - _Temperature_ Granger-causes both _ice cream sales_ and _drowning_

#### Granger Causality: Formal Definition

- Consider two stationary time series $X_t$ and $Y_t$

- Compare two predictors of $Y_t$:
  1. _Restricted model_: uses only past $Y$
     $$
     Y_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i Y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t
     $$
  2. _Unrestricted model_: adds past $X$
     $$
     Y_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i Y_{t-i}
                  + \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X_{t-j} + \eta_t
     $$

- **\textcolor{red}{Definition}**: $X$ _Granger-causes_ $Y$ iff the unrestricted model has
  _strictly smaller_ forecast error than the restricted one
  - Equivalently: F-test on $H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = ... = \beta_p = 0$

#### Granger Causality: Key Assumptions

1. **\textcolor{red}{Stationarity}**: both series have time-invariant statistical properties
   - Non-stationary series can produce spurious Granger causality
   - Pre-test with ADF / KPSS; difference if needed

2. **\textcolor{orange}{Correct lag order $p$}**: too few lags miss the effect; too many overfit
   - Choose via AIC, BIC, or cross-validation

3. **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Linearity}** (in the basic formulation)
   - Non-linear Granger causality extensions exist (e.g., transfer entropy)

4. **\textcolor{teal}{No omitted common causes}** (the big one)
   - If $Z$ drives both $X$ and $Y$ with different lags, $X$ appears to
     Granger-cause $Y$

5. **\textcolor{cyan}{Correct sampling frequency}**
   - If the true effect operates faster than the sampling interval, Granger
     causality may fail to detect or reverse direction

#### Granger Causality: Limitations

- **\textcolor{red}{Granger causality $\neq$ causation}**
  - It tests **\textcolor{orange}{predictive}** relationships, not **\textcolor{darkgreen}{interventional}** ones
  - A Granger-causal relationship can be entirely due to a common driver

- **\textcolor{teal}{Example of spurious Granger causality}**:
  - Barometer reading $X_t$ Granger-causes storms $Y_{t+1}$
  - But raising the barometer manually does _not_ cause a storm
  - Atmospheric pressure is the actual common cause

- **\textcolor{cyan}{Aggregation problems}**:
  - Monthly data may miss daily Granger effects
  - Sampling slower than the process $\to$ spurious bidirectional causality

- **\textcolor{blue}{Cannot handle contemporaneous effects}** in the basic form
  - If $X_t$ affects $Y_t$ instantly, lag-based tests miss it

- **\textcolor{violet}{Requires long time series}** for reliable inference
  - Typically $T \gg p$ (e.g., 100+ observations for 4 lags)

#### Granger Causality: Practical Example 1

- **\textcolor{red}{Question}**: does advertising spend Granger-cause sales?
  - $X_t$: weekly ad spend
  - $Y_t$: weekly sales

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Caveat}**: this does _not_ mean "if we spend more on ads, sales will rise"
  - Both could be driven by seasonal demand $\to$ need an experiment or a causal
    design to get an interventional answer

- **\textcolor{blue}{Question}**: do Twitter sentiment scores Granger-cause stock returns?
  - A widely-studied question; results are mixed
  - Twitter sentiment _does_ Granger-cause short-horizon returns for some stocks
    and horizons, but effect sizes are small

# Interrupted Time Series (ITS)

## Design and Estimation

#### Interrupted Time Series: Motivation

- **\textcolor{red}{Scenario}**: suppose a policy is implemented at a single known date $t^*$
  across all units
  - E.g., new speed limit enforced from January 1st
  - E.g., a website redesign launched on a specific day

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Problem}**
  - No control group is available: the whole population is treated

- **\textcolor{blue}{Solution}**:
  - Use the _pre-intervention_ trajectory of the series to forecast what would
    have happened _without_ the intervention
  - Compare the forecast (_counterfactual_) to what actually happened
  - The gap is the estimated causal effect

- **\textcolor{violet}{Example}**: 
  - Seatbelt law: ~15-20% reduction in fatalities
  - Netflix password ban: 
    - Expected: subscriber growth from monetizing shared accounts
    - Actual: mixed results (growth in certain regions, churn in others)
  - iOS privacy changes:
    - Apps must ask permission to track
    - 60-70% opt-in to tracking $\to$ 15-25% opt-in
    - ad-targeting effectiveness tanked, Meta reported billions in revenue losses


#### ITS: Segmented Regression Formulation

- _Segmented regression_ model:
  $$
  Y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \beta_2 D_t + \beta_3 (t - t^*) D_t + \varepsilon_t
  $$
  where $D_t = I(t \ge t^*)$

- **\textcolor{red}{Interpretation}**
  - $\beta_0$: pre-intervention intercept
  - $\beta_1$: pre-intervention slope (trend)
  - $\beta_2$: _level change_ at the intervention (immediate jump)
  - $\beta_3$: _slope change_ after the intervention (trend shift)

- **\textcolor{blue}{Questions}**:
  1. Is there a jump at $t^*$?
     - Test $\beta_2 = 0$
  2. Does the trajectory change?
     - Test $\beta_3 = 0$

#### ITS: Details

- _Autocorrelation_ is almost always present
  - Fit with autocorrelation robust methods (Newey-West, ARIMA)

- _Robustness checks_:
  - Vary the window size (use 1, 2, 5 years pre-period)
  - Try placebo intervention dates: do you find fake effects?
  - Check for seasonality, holidays, co-occurring events

- _Visual diagnostic_:
  - Always plot the series with the fitted model and the intervention date marked
  - Does ITS result survives?

#### ITS: Applications

- **\textcolor{red}{Public health}**
  - Effect of soda taxes on sugar-sweetened beverage purchases

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Policy evaluation}**
  - Effect of congestion pricing in London (2003) on traffic volumes
  - Effect of a minimum wage increase on employment (comparing pre/post)

- **\textcolor{blue}{Tech and product}**
  - Effect of a website redesign on conversion rate (before/after)
  - Effect of a new recommendation algorithm on engagement

- **\textcolor{violet}{Safety interventions}**
  - Did installing speed cameras reduce accidents?

#### ITS: Strengths and Weaknesses

- **\textcolor{red}{Pros}**
  - Works when _no control group exists_ (universal policy changes)
  - Uses the unit as its own control $\to$ time-invariant confounders cancel

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Cons}**
  - Relies on _extrapolation_ of the pre-period trend
  - Vulnerable to _co-occurring events_ (_"was it the policy or the
    recession?"_)
  - Autocorrelation inflates errors if ignored
  - Needs a long, stable pre-period (rare in practice)

- **\textcolor{blue}{Rule of thumb}**: ITS is most credible when:
  1. The intervention is sharply timed
  2. No other major events occur near $t^*$
  3. The pre-period is long and stable
  4. The outcome has low noise relative to the effect size

# Difference-in-Differences (DiD)

## Parallel Trends Assumption

#### DiD: Motivation and Setup

- **\textcolor{red}{Motivating Example}**
  - Suppose a policy is rolled out in _some_ units but not others
    - E.g., New Jersey raises its minimum wage in 1992; Pennsylvania does not
    - Two groups: treated (NJ) and control (PA); two periods: before and after

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Definition}**: Difference-in-Differences (DiD) estimates a causal effect by
  comparing the change in outcomes over time between a group that received
  treatment and a group that didn't
  - Assume both groups would have followed the same trend in the absence of
    treatment

- **\textcolor{blue}{Procedure}**
  - Subtracts out time-invariant differences between groups
  - Subtracts out common time trends across groups
  - What's left is attributed to the policy

- **\textcolor{violet}{Example}**: Card & Krueger (1994) studied the NJ vs PA minimum wage
  - Standard economic theory predicted that raising the minimum wage would reduce
    employment (since labor becomes more costly)
  - DiD estimate: essentially no employment effect (a surprising result)

#### DiD: The Core Idea

```graphviz
digraph DiD {
    rankdir=LR;
    splines=true;
    nodesep=0.8;
    ranksep=0.8;
    node [shape=box, style="rounded,filled", fontname="Helvetica", fontsize=12, penwidth=1.4];

    TreatPre   [label=<Treated<BR/>Pre>,   fillcolor="#FFD1A6"];
    TreatPost  [label=<Treated<BR/>Post>,  fillcolor="#F4A6A6"];
    CtrlPre    [label=<Control<BR/>Pre>,   fillcolor="#B2E2B2"];
    CtrlPost   [label=<Control<BR/>Post>,  fillcolor="#A0D6D1"];
    Effect     [label=<DiD =<BR/>(T_post - T_pre)<BR/>- (C_post - C_pre)>, fillcolor="#A6C8F4"];

    TreatPre -> TreatPost [label="  change in treated"];
    CtrlPre -> CtrlPost  [label="  change in control"];
    TreatPost -> Effect;
    CtrlPost -> Effect;
}
```

- _DiD estimator_ (with 2 groups, 2 periods):
  $$
  \hat{\tau}_{DiD}
    = (\bar{Y}^{T}_{\text{post}} - \bar{Y}^{T}_{\text{pre}})
      - (\bar{Y}^{C}_{\text{post}} - \bar{Y}^{C}_{\text{pre}})
  $$

#### DiD: Regression Formulation

- Equivalent regression form (with unit $i$ and time $t$):
  $$
  Y_{it} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{Treat}_i
         + \gamma \cdot \text{Post}_t
         + \tau \cdot (\text{Treat}_i \cdot \text{Post}_t) + \varepsilon_{it}
  $$
  - $\beta$: time-invariant difference between groups
  - $\gamma$: common time trend
  - $\tau$: _the DiD causal effect_ (coefficient on the interaction)


#### Parallel Trends Assumption

- **\textcolor{red}{Assumption}**: in the absence of treatment, the treated and control units
  would have followed the _same trend_ over time
  $$
  \EE[Y^{(0)}_{it} - Y^{(0)}_{i,t-1} | \text{Treat}_i = 1]
  = \EE[Y^{(0)}_{it} - Y^{(0)}_{i,t-1} | \text{Treat}_i = 0]
  $$
  where $Y^{(0)}$ denotes the untreated potential outcome

- **\textcolor{orange}{Intuition}**: levels can differ, but _changes_ should match
  - E.g., NJ may always have higher wages than PA, but both move together
    absent the policy

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Question}**: how do we test it?
  - We cannot test it: it is a _counterfactual_ statement
  - But we can check the _pre-treatment_ trends as a proxy
  - If they are parallel _before_ treatment, we gain confidence (but no proof)

- **\textcolor{teal}{Visual check}**: plot the two group means over time; do they move in
  lockstep before treatment?

- **\textcolor{cyan}{Red flags}**:
  - Significant pre-trends with opposite sign of the effect
  - Divergence starting _before_ the policy (anticipation or selection)
  - Using too short a pre-period $\to$ can't check parallel trends


#### DiD: Robustness Checks

- **\textcolor{red}{Placebo treatments in time}**: pretend the treatment happened earlier
  - Should get no effect if the design is sound

- **\textcolor{orange}{Placebo outcomes}**: apply DiD to unrelated outcomes that _shouldn't_ change
  - E.g., if studying minimum wage on employment, check it has no effect on
    commodity prices

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Alternative control groups}**: use different comparison units
  - Results should be stable across reasonable choices

- **\textcolor{teal}{Sensitivity to functional form}**: levels vs. logs, linear vs. trend
  adjustment

- **\textcolor{cyan}{Falsification tests}**: subset the data in ways that should _not_ yield an
  effect
  - If they do, your design picks up something other than the treatment

#### DiD: Summary of Strengths and Limits

- **\textcolor{red}{Pros}**
  - Removes time-invariant confounders with unit fixed effects
  - Removes common shocks with time fixed effects
  - Uses both treated and control units, making it more credible than ITS alone
  - Flexible: extends to multiple periods, staggered adoption, covariates

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Cons}**
  - Parallel trends assumption is _untestable_: can only check pre-trends
  - Time-varying confounders still bias the estimate
  - Staggered adoption with heterogeneous effects breaks naive TWFE
  - Standard errors require clustering — easy to get wrong

- **\textcolor{blue}{Rule of thumb}**: DiD is credible when:
  1. Pre-trends are visually parallel
  2. No co-occurring shocks hit treated and controls differently
  3. Staggered timing is handled with modern estimators

# Synthetic Control Methods

## Constructing a Counterfactual from Donor Series

#### Synthetic Control: Motivation

- **\textcolor{red}{Problem}**: DiD works when a credible control unit exists
  - But what if _no single control unit_ matches the treated one?

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Example}**: studying the effect of German reunification (1990) on West
  Germany's per-capita GDP
  - No other single country matches West Germany's pre-1990 size, growth path,
    and economic structure
  - Synthetic Control instead builds a weighted combination of other OECD
    countries that closely tracks West Germany's pre-reunification GDP, then uses
    that as the counterfactual

- **\textcolor{blue}{Key idea}**
  - Construct a _weighted average_ of control units that best matches the
    treated unit's pre-treatment characteristics
  - This weighted average is the synthetic control
  - Compare treated unit to the synthetic control in the post-period
  - The gap is the estimated causal effect

- **\textcolor{red}{Definition}**: _Donor pool_ are untreated units eligible for inclusion in the
  weighted combination
  - Should exclude units treated by the same or similar interventions

## Weighted Combinations and Optimal Weights

#### Synthetic Control: Formal Setup

- There are $J+1$ units:
  - Unit 1 is treated at time $t^*$
  - Units $2, ..., J+1$ are untreated (donors)

- $\vX_1$ be a vector of pre-treatment predictors for the treated unit
- $\vX_0$ the matrix of the same predictors for donors (size $k \times J$)

- Pick _weights_ $\vw = (w_2, ..., w_{J+1})$ satisfy:
  $$
  w_j \ge 0, \quad \sum_{j=2}^{J+1} w_j = 1
  $$
  - Non-negativity and unit-sum $\to$ weights are a _convex combination_ of
    donors
  - Ensures the synthetic control is in the _convex hull_ of the donor pool
    $\to$ no extrapolation

#### Synthetic Control: Optimization

- Choose $\vw^*$ to minimize pre-treatment mismatch:
  $$
  \vw^* = \arg\min_{\vw} (\vX_1 - \vX_0 \vw)^\top \vV (\vX_1 - \vX_0 \vw)
  $$
  subject to $w_j \ge 0, \sum w_j = 1$

- $\vV$ is a $k \times k$ diagonal matrix of _predictor importance weights_
  - Chosen to minimize mean-squared prediction error on pre-treatment outcomes
  - Nested optimization (inner: find $\vw$; outer: find $\vV$)

- The _estimated causal effect_ at time $t \ge t^*$:
  $$
  \hat{\tau}_t = Y_{1t} - \sum_{j=2}^{J+1} w^*_j Y_{jt}
  $$
  - Gap between treated unit's outcome and the weighted donor outcome

#### Synthetic Control: Inference via Placebos

- **\textcolor{red}{Problem}**: there is only _one_ treated unit, so standard inference doesn't
  apply

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Solution}**: _placebo tests_ by permutation
  1. Pretend each donor was the treated unit
  2. Compute the synthetic control and causal-effect trajectory for each
  3. Compare the _actual_ treated unit's effect against this "null distribution"

- If the treated unit's effect is _larger in magnitude_ than most placebo
  effects $\to$ evidence of a real effect
  - Compute an empirical $p$-value from the placebos

- **\textcolor{blue}{Variant}**: filter placebos to keep only those with good pre-treatment fit
  - A donor with bad pre-fit produces noise, not a legitimate null

#### Synthetic Control: When It Works Well

- **\textcolor{red}{Long pre-treatment period}**: many time points to match on
  - Rule of thumb: at least 10+ pre-treatment observations

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Rich donor pool}**: many untreated units with diverse characteristics
  - Enables good pre-treatment fit via convex combinations

- **\textcolor{blue}{Small number of treated units}** (usually one)
  - Designed for aggregated units: countries, states, cities, firms

- **\textcolor{violet}{Outcome with moderate noise}**: low enough to detect the signal

#### Synthetic Control: When It Fails

- **\textcolor{red}{Poor pre-treatment fit}**: no convex combination matches the treated unit
  - The treated unit is an _outlier_ in the donor space
  - Reported effects are unreliable

- **\textcolor{orange}{Treatment contaminates donors}** (spillovers)
  - E.g., neighboring states also affected by a policy violate the design

- **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Short pre-period or noisy outcome}** $\to$ fit is unreliable or overfits
  noise

- **\textcolor{teal}{Confounding unobserved events}**: a co-occurring shock to the treated unit
  near $t^*$ masquerades as the treatment effect

- **\textcolor{cyan}{Diagnostic}**: always plot the pre-treatment fit
  - A poor pre-fit $\to$ don't trust the post-period gap

# Summary

#### Synthetic Control vs. Other Methods: Summary

\begingroup \scriptsize
| **Method**        | **Control Group**       | **Strength**                         | **Key Assumption**                  |
| ----------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| Granger           | Own history             | Predictive screen                    | Stationarity, no common causes      |
| Interrupted Time Series (ITS) | Pre-period of same unit | Works without controls               | Stable pre-trend, no co-events      |
| DiD               | Untreated comparison    | Removes time-invariant confounders   | Parallel trends                     |
| Synthetic Control | Weighted donor pool     | Data-driven counterfactual           | Good pre-fit, no spillovers         |
\endgroup

- Use **\textcolor{red}{Granger causality}** when:
  - You want a fast predictive screen on two long time series
  - Intervention / experiment is not available

- Use **\textcolor{darkgreen}{Interrupted Time Series (ITS)}** when:
  - A single intervention has a sharp date and no comparison units exist
  - Pre-period is long and stable

- Use **\textcolor{blue}{Difference-in-Differences (DiD)}** when:
  - Some units are treated and others are not
  - Parallel pre-trends are plausible
  - You have panel data with units and time

- Use **\textcolor{violet}{Synthetic Control}** when:
  - One (or few) treated units with a rich donor pool
  t- No single donor is a credible match, but a weighted combination is
