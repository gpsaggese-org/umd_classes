==== Logistic Regression Is a Probabilistic Classifier
<logistic-regression-is-a-probabilistic-classifier>
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Logistic regression] learns:

  - The probability of each class $Pr \( y \| bold(underline(x)) \)$
    given input $bold(underline(x))$
  - Instead of predicting class $y$ directly

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Parametric approach];: assume
  $Pr \( y \| bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(w)) \)$ has a known
  functional form
  $ Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(w)) \) = upright("logit") \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x)) \) $

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Optimize parameters] $bold(underline(w))$ using maximum
  likelihood
  $ bold(underline(w))^(\*) = sans("argmax")_(bold(underline(w))) Pr \( cal(D) ; bold(underline(w)) \) $

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Predict] by outputting class with highest probability
  $ h_(bold(underline(w))) \( bold(underline(x)) \) = cases(delim: "{", + 1 & Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(w)) \) gt.eq 0.5, - 1 & Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(w)) \) < 0.5, ) $

==== Logistic Regression: Example
<logistic-regression-example>
- Assume $y$ is:
  - The event $y_i$ #emph["patient with characteristics
    $bold(underline(x))$ had heart attack"]
  - Function of parameters $bold(underline(x))$ (e.g., age, gender,
    diet)
- In data set $cal(D)$:
  - No samples of $Pr \( y \| bold(underline(x)) \)$
  - Have realizations:
    - #emph["Patient with $bold(underline(x))_1$ had a heart attack"]
    - #emph["Patient with $bold(underline(x))_2$ didn't"]
    - …
- Learn $Pr \( y \| bold(underline(x)) \)$
  - Find best parameters $bold(underline(w))$ for logistic regression
    model to explain data $cal(D)$

==== Logistic Function
<logistic-function>
- Aka "sigmoid"
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Logistic function] $upright("logit") \( s \)$ is defined as
  $ upright("logit") \( s \) eq.delta frac(e^s, 1 + e^s) = frac(1, 1 + e^(- s)) $
  - Varies in \[0, 1\]
  - Crosses the origin at 0.5
  - Asymptotes at 0 and 1
  - It is a soft version of $sans("sign") \( \)$

==== Logistic Regression vs Linear Classifier
<logistic-regression-vs-linear-classifier>
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Functional form is similar]
  - #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Logistic regression];:
    $h \( bold(underline(w)) \) = upright("logit") \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))$)
  - #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Linear classifier] (perceptron):
    $h \( bold(underline(w)) \) = sans("sign") \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x)) \)$
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Difference] in probabilistic interpretation and fitting method
  - Logistic regression\*\* lacks samples of probability function to
    interpolate
    - Uses realizations of random variable
    - Seeks model parameters maximizing data likelihood
  - #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Linear classification] assumes class value is linear
    function of inputs

==== Error for Probabilistic Binary Classifiers
<error-for-probabilistic-binary-classifiers>
- For probabilistic binary classification, use #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[log-probability
  error] as point-wise error
  $ e \( h \( bold(underline(x)) \) \, y \) eq.delta - log \( Pr \( y = h \( bold(underline(x)) \) \| bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(w)) \) \) $
  - Negate for positive errors:
    $log \( \[ 0 \, 1 \] \) in \[ - oo \, 0 \)$
- Log probability generalizes 0-1 error
  - Case $y = 1$
    - Output $h \( bold(underline(x)) \)$ close to 1
      $arrow.r.double.long$ probability 1
      $arrow.r.double.long log \( 1 \) = 0$
      $arrow.r.double.long e \( dot.op \) = 0$
    - Output close to 0
      $arrow.r.double.long e \( \) = - log \( 0 \) arrow.r + oo$
  - Similar behavior for $y = 0$

==== One-Liner Error for Probabilistic Binary Classifiers
<one-liner-error-for-probabilistic-binary-classifiers>
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Point-wise error] for example $\( bold(underline(x)) \, y \)$
  for probabilistic binary classifiers is defined as:

- Any function of a binary variable:
  $ y & = cases(delim: "{", a & x = 1, b & x = 0, )\
   $ can be written as one-liner:
  $y = x dot.op a + \( 1 - x \) dot.op b$

- Point-wise error can be written independently of
  $Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x)) \)$:
  $ e \( h \( bold(underline(x)) \) \, y \) = - y log \( Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x)) \) - \( 1 - y \) log \( 1 - Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x)) \) \) $

==== One-Liner Error for Logistic Regression
<one-liner-error-for-logistic-regression>
- The point-wise error for a binary classifier is:
  $ e \( h \( bold(underline(x)) \) \, y \) = - y log \( Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x)) \) - \( 1 - y \) log \( 1 - Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x)) \) \) $

- Simplify further #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[with logit function];:

- Point-wise error for logistic regression equals #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[cross-entropy
  error]

==== Cross-Entropy Error
<cross-entropy-error>
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Point-wise error for logistic regression] has expression:
  $ e \( h \( bold(underline(x)) \) \, y \) = log \( 1 + exp \( - y dot.op bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x)) \) \) $
  - It is called #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[cross-entropy error]
  - Note: no $-$ before $log \( dot.op \)$ but before
    $y dot.op bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))$
- Cross-entropy error #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[generalizes 0-1 error]
  - If $bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))$ agrees with $y$ in sign
    and $\| bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x)) \|$ is large
    $arrow.r.double.long$ error goes to 0
  - If they disagree in sign $arrow.r.double.long$ error goes towards
    $oo$
- Define #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[in-sample error on training set] as average of
  point-wise errors:
  $ E_(i n) eq.delta 1 / N sum_n e \( h \( bold(underline(x))_n \) \, y_n \) $

==== Fitting Logistic Regression
<fitting-logistic-regression>
- A plausible error measure of a hypothesis is based on
  #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[likelihood of data] $ Pr \( cal(D) \| h = f \) $

  - #emph["How likely is the data $cal(D)$ under the assumption that
    $h = f$?"]
  - #emph["How likely is that the data $cal(D)$ was generated by $h$?"]

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Maximize likelihood] $cal(D)$ generated from logistic
  regression
  $ Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(w)) \) $

- It can be proved that this is equivalent to #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[minimizing
  in-sample error] on training set #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[using cross-entropy error]

==== Fitting Logistic Regression (Optional)
<fitting-logistic-regression-optional>
- Find $bold(underline(w))$ that maximizes likelihood for data set
  $cal(D) = { \( bold(underline(x))_1 \, y_1 \) \, . . . \, \( bold(underline(x))_N \, y_N \) }$
  generated by model $h \( bold(underline(x)) \)$:
  $ Pr \( D \| bold(underline(w)) \) = Pr \( y_1 = h \( bold(underline(x))_1 \) and . . . and y_N = h \( bold(underline(x))_N \) \) = Pr \( y_1 = y_(1') and . . . and y_N = y_(N') \) $

- Model form:
  $ y' = h \( bold(underline(x)) \) = cases(delim: "{", + 1 & upright(" if logit") \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x)) \) > 0.5, - 1 & upright(" otherwise")) $

- Assuming independence among training examples:
  $ Pr \( D \| bold(underline(w)) \) = product_(i = 1)^N Pr \( y_i = y_(i') \| bold(underline(x))_i \) $

- Fold $y_n$ in expression:

  - When $y_n = 1$,
    $Pr \( y_n = y_(n') \) = upright("logit") \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_n \)$
  - When $y_n = - 1$,
    $Pr \( y_n = y_(n') \) = upright("logit") \( - bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_n \)$

- Thus,
  $Pr \( y_n = y_(n') \) = upright("logit") \( y_n bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_n \)$

<fitting-logistic-regression-optional-1>

  $E_(i n) = sum e \( h \( bold(underline(x))_n \) \, y_n \)$

  - Maximize $log \( . . . \)$ with respect to $bold(underline(w))$
    since log argument $> 0$ and $log \( \)$ is monotone

==== Gradient Descent for Logistic Regression
<gradient-descent-for-logistic-regression>
- Gradient descent requires two inputs:

  - Gradient of the cost function
    $frac(partial E, w_j) upright(" for all ") j$
  - Cost function $E_(i n) \( bold(underline(w)) \)$

- The cost function is:
  $ E_(i n) \( bold(underline(w)) \) = 1 / N sum_i e \( h \( bold(underline(x))_i ; bold(underline(w)) \) \, y_i \) $

- The cost function for logistic regression:
  $ E_(i n) \( bold(underline(w)) \) = 1 / N sum_i log \( 1 + exp \( - y_i bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i \) \) $

- Thus gradient descent converges to global minimum

  - It can be shown that $E_(i n) \( bold(underline(w)) \)$ is convex in
    $bold(underline(w))$
  - In fact sum of exponentials and flipped exponentials is convex and
    log is monotone

==== One-Vs-All Multi-Class Classification
<one-vs-all-multi-class-classification>
- Aka "one-vs-rest" classifier

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Problem];: you have $n$ classes $c_1 \, . . . \, c_n$ to
  distinguish given $bold(underline(x))$

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Learn]

  - Create $n$ binary classification problems where we classify $c_i$ vs
    $c_(- i)$ (everything but $i$)
  - Learn $n$ classifiers with optimal $bold(underline(w))_i$, each
    estimating
    $Pr \( y = i \| bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(w))_i \)$

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Predict]

  - Evaluate the $n$ classifiers
  - Pick the class $y = i$ with the maximum
    $Pr \( y = i \| bold(underline(x)) \)$

==== Cost Function for Multi-Class Classification (Opt)
<cost-function-for-multi-class-classification-opt>
- The cost function for logistic regression is:
  $ E_(i n) \( bold(underline(w)) \) = - 1 / N sum_(i = 1)^N \( y_i log Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x))_i \) + \( 1 - y_i \) log \( 1 - Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x))_i \) \) \) $

- Encode expected outputs $bold(underline(y))_i$ one-hot

  - $j$-th element $bold(underline(y))_i \|_j$ is 1 if correct class is
    $j$-th
  - E.g., for $k = 4$ #text(fill: blue)[1000]

- Using $bold(underline(h)) \( bold(underline(x)) \)$ as model outputs
  and
  $Pr \( y = 1 \| bold(underline(x)) \) = p \( bold(underline(x)) \)$:
  $ E_(i n) \( bold(underline(w)) \) = - 1 / N sum_(i = 1)^N sum_(k = 1)^K (bold(underline(y))_i log \( bold(underline(p)) \( bold(underline(x))_i \) \) + \( 1 - bold(underline(y))_i \) log \( 1 - bold(underline(p)) \( bold(underline(x))_i \) \)) #scale(x: 120%, y: 120%)[\|]_k $

- Innermost summation considers error on each class/digit

  - E.g., for $k = 4$ #text(fill: blue)[1000] vs #text(fill: blue)[0100]
  - Equal digits don't contribute to error
  - Different digits give positive contribution

==== Basic Idea of Parametric Models
<basic-idea-of-parametric-models>
- Assume a model generates data
  - Known functional form
  - Parametrized with unknown parameters to estimate
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Pros]
  - Utilize data structure
  - Easy to fit: few parameters
  - Accurate predictions if assumptions correct
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Cons]
  - Strong data assumptions
  - Low accuracy if assumptions incorrect

==== Linear and Quadratic Discriminant Analysis
<linear-and-quadratic-discriminant-analysis>
- Aka LDA and QDA

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Parametric models]

  - Assume each class generating process is multivariate Gaussian
  - Classifiers with linear and quadratic decision surface

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Pros]

  - Closed-form solutions easy to compute (sample mean and covariance)
  - Inherently multiclass
  - No hyperparams to tune

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Cons]

  - Strong assumptions about the data

==== LDA / QDA: Model Form
<lda-qda-model-form>
- Both LDA and QDA assume class generating process
  $f_k \( bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(mu_k)) \, bold(underline(underline(Sigma_k))) \)$
  is #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[multivariate Gaussian]

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Linear discriminant analysis (LDA)] model:
  $ f_k \( bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(mu_k)) \, bold(underline(underline(Sigma_k))) \) tilde.op cal(N) \( bold(underline(mu_k)) \, bold(underline(underline(Sigma))) \) \) $

  - Means $bold(underline(mu_k))$ differ for all $k$ classes
  - Covariance matrix $bold(underline(underline(Sigma)))$ same for all
    $k$ classes
  - Classes separated by linear decision boundaries

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA)] model:

  - Classes $k$ have different covariance matrix
    $bold(underline(underline(Sigma_k)))$
  - Classes separated by quadratic boundaries

==== Bayes Theorem for LDA / QDA
<bayes-theorem-for-lda-qda>
- Consider a classification setup with multi-class output
  $Y in { 1 \, . . . \, K }$

- Build a #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[parametric model for the conditional distribution];:
  $ Pr \( Y = k \| X = bold(underline(x)) \) $

- Use #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Bayes theorem];:
  $ Pr \( Y = k \| X = bold(underline(x)) \) = frac(Pr \( X = bold(underline(x)) \| Y = k \) dot.op Pr \( Y = k \), Pr \( X = bold(underline(x)) \)) $
  where:

  - $Pr \( X = bold(underline(x)) \| Y = k \)$: given a class, estimate
    probability of $bold(underline(x))$
  - $Pr \( Y = k \) = pi_k$: probability of each class (prior)
  - $Pr \( X = bold(underline(x)) \)$: probability of each input

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Estimate probabilities] from data

==== LDA / QDA: Boundary Decision (Optional)
<lda-qda-boundary-decision-optional>
- Consider the ratio between the probabilities of $Y = k$ vs $Y = j$:
  $ r = frac(Pr \( Y = k \| X = bold(underline(x)) \), Pr \( Y = j \| X = bold(underline(x)) \)) $

- Using the model assumption and Bayes theorem: where:
  $ f_k \( bold(underline(x)) \) = c dot.op exp (- 1 / 2 \( bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(mu_k)) \)^T bold(underline(underline(Sigma)))^(- 1) \( bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(mu_k)) \)) $

- Apply $log \( dot.op \)$ as a monotone transformation
  $ r = log frac(f_k \( bold(underline(x)) \), f_j \( bold(underline(x)) \)) + log pi_k / pi_j $

==== LDA / QDA: Boundary Decision (Optional)
<lda-qda-boundary-decision-optional-1>
- Apply $log \( dot.op \)$ as a monotone transformation
  $ r = log frac(f_k \( bold(underline(x)) \), f_j \( bold(underline(x)) \)) + log pi_k / pi_j $

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Second term] independent of $bold(underline(x))$ so you can
  ignore it

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[First term] proportional to:
  $ \( bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(mu_k)) \)^T bold(underline(underline(Sigma)))^(- 1) \( bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(mu_k)) \) - \( bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(mu_j)) \)^T bold(underline(underline(Sigma)))^(- 1) \( bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(mu_j)) \) $

- Expand, simplify
  $bold(underline(x))^T bold(underline(underline(Sigma)))^(- 1) bold(underline(x))$,
  and note:
  $ 2 bold(underline(x))^T bold(underline(underline(Sigma)))^(- 1) \( bold(underline(mu_k)) - bold(underline(mu_j)) \) $
  Resulting in a linear relationship in $bold(underline(x))$
  $ - 1 / 2 \( bold(underline(mu_k)) + bold(underline(mu_j)) \)^T bold(underline(underline(Sigma)))^(- 1) \( bold(underline(mu_k)) - bold(underline(mu_j)) \) + bold(underline(x))^T bold(underline(underline(Sigma)))^(- 1) \( bold(underline(mu_k)) - bold(underline(mu_j)) \) $

==== LDA / QDA: Learn Model (Optional)
<lda-qda-learn-model-optional>
- In practice:

  - Ignore $Pr \( X = bold(underline(x)) \)$; it's common for all
    classes
  - Know or estimate prior $Pr \( Y = k \) = pi_k$ from data
  - Estimate conditional probability
    $Pr \( X = bold(underline(x)) \| Y = k \)$

- Model assumes Gaussian distribution for conditional probability:
  $ Pr \( X = bold(underline(x)) \| Y = k \) = f_k \( bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(mu_k)) \, bold(underline(underline(Sigma_k))) \) $
  where:
  $ f_k \( bold(underline(x)) \) = frac(1, \( 2 pi \)^n \| bold(underline(underline(Sigma_k))) \|^(1 \/ 2)) exp (- 1 / 2 \( bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(mu_k)) \)^T bold(underline(underline(Sigma_k)))^(- 1) \( bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(mu_k)) \)) $

- Estimate parameters $bold(underline(mu_k))$,
  $bold(underline(underline(Sigma_k)))$ using sample mean and covariance

==== Evaluating LDA / QDA
<evaluating-lda-qda>
- For new $bold(underline(x)) = bold(underline(x))'$, compute for each
  class $Y = k$
  $ Pr \( Y = k \| X = bold(underline(x)) \) prop f_k \( bold(underline(x)) ; bold(underline(mu_k)) \, bold(underline(underline(Sigma_k))) \) dot.op pi_k $

- Choose $k$ that maximizes posterior probability

- If $f_i \( bold(underline(x)) \)$ is from a multivariate Gaussian
  distribution with diagonal $bold(underline(underline(Sigma)))$
  (feature independence), you can simplify expressions further

==== Kernel: Definition
<kernel-definition>
- Consider a transformation $Phi : cal(X) arrow.r cal(Z)$
  - E.g., transform features in space $cal(X)$ non-linearly into
    higher-dimensional space $cal(Z)$
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Kernel of transformation] $Phi$ yields inner product of two
  points $bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' in cal(X)$ in
  transformed space $cal(Z)$

==== Kernel: Expression From the Transform
<kernel-expression-from-the-transform>
- If you have an expression for $Phi$, compute a closed formula for the
  kernel

- E.g., if transformation is $Phi : bb(R)^2 arrow.r bb(R)^6$, it
  introduces interaction terms:
  $ bold(underline(z)) = Phi \( bold(underline(x)) \) = Phi \( x_1 \, x_2 \) = \( 1 \, x_1 \, x_2 \, x_1^2 \, x_2^2 \, x_1 x_2 \) $

- Kernel of $Phi$ is:

==== Gaussian Kernel
<gaussian-kernel>
- Aka "exponential kernel" or "Radial Basis Function" (RBF) kernel
- A #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Gaussian kernel] has the form:
  $ K \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \) = exp \( - gamma parallel bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(x))' parallel^2 \) = exp \( - frac(parallel bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(x))' parallel^2, sigma^2) \) $
- It can be shown to be an inner product in an infinite dimension
  $cal(Z)$

==== Kernel as Way to Measure Similarity
<kernel-as-way-to-measure-similarity>
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Intuition];: The Gaussian kernel
  $ K \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \) = exp \( - gamma parallel bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(x))' parallel^2 \) $
  measures "similarity" of point $bold(underline(x))$ to point
  $bold(underline(x))_i$:
  - $K \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \)$ is 1 when points
    are the same
  - Value is 0 when points are distant
  - Effect strength depends on $gamma$
- Using kernels to compute features:
  - Kernels often rely on distance between vectors
    - E.g., euclidean norm
      $parallel bold(underline(x)) - bold(underline(x))' parallel^2$
  - Need to scale features for similar effects among coordinates

==== Linear Kernel
<linear-kernel>
- Consider the transformation $Phi$ as the identity function
  $Phi \( bold(underline(x)) \) = bold(underline(x))$
- The kernel function is:
  $ K_Phi \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \) = bold(underline(x))^T bold(underline(x))' $
  - A #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[linear kernel] means using no kernel
  - It is just a "pass-through"

==== Polynomial Kernel
<polynomial-kernel>
- Given a point $bold(underline(x)) in bb(R)^n$, consider the function
  with two parameters $k$ and $d$
  $ K_Phi \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \) = \( k + bold(underline(x))^T bold(underline(x))' \)^d $
- It is called #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[polynomial] since if you expand the dot product
  you get a polynomial
- It can be proved that this is always a kernel

==== Kernel: Identifying a Function as a Kernel
<kernel-identifying-a-function-as-a-kernel>
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Problem];:
  - You have a certain function
    $K \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \)$ and you want to
    show that $K \( dot.op \)$ is an inner product in the form for some
    function $Phi \( dot.op \)$
    $ K \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \) = Phi \( bold(underline(x)) \)^T Phi \( bold(underline(x))' \) quad forall bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' $
    for a certain $Phi$ and $cal(Z)$
- In theory, a given function
  $K \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \)$ is a valid kernel
  iff:
  - It is a symmetric, and
  - Satisfies the Mercer's condition: the matrix
    $K \( bold(underline(x))_i \, bold(underline(x))_j \)$ is definite
    semi-positive

==== Kernel: Example of Identifying a Kernel
<kernel-example-of-identifying-a-kernel>
- Let's show that:
  $ K \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \) = \( k + bold(underline(x))^T bold(underline(x))' \)^d $
  is a #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[kernel] for any $n \, k \, d$

- According to the definition you need to show that there is always a
  transform $Phi$: $ Phi : cal(X) = bb(R)^n arrow.r cal(Z) = bb(R)^q $
  with $q gt.double d$, such that
  $K_Phi = \( k + bold(underline(x))^T bold(underline(x))' \)^d$

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Example]

  - $cal(X) = bb(R)^2$,
    $K \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \) = \( 1 + bold(underline(x))^T bold(underline(x))' \)^2 = \( 1 + x_1 x'_1 + x_2 x'_2 \)^2$
  - Compute the full expression in terms of the coordinates:
    $ K \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \) = \( 1 + x_1^2 x'_1^2 + x_2^2 x'_2^2 + 2 x_1 x'_1 + 2 x_2 x'_2 + 2 x_1 x'_1 x_2 x'_2 \) $
  - Choose:
    - $cal(Z) = bb(R)^6$
    - $Phi \( x_1 \, x_2 \) = \( 1 \, x_1^2 \, x_2^2 \, sqrt(2) x_1 \, sqrt(2) x_2 \, sqrt(2) x_1 x_2 \)$
  - This is a particular case of the polynomial kernel

==== A Kernel Is a Computational Shortcut
<a-kernel-is-a-computational-shortcut>
- In literature, the #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[kernel trick] is a #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[computational
  shortcut] for the dot product of transformed vectors

- Compare 2 ways to compute the inner product of transformed vectors for
  a polynomial kernel

  + #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Using definition];: compute images of vectors, then inner
    product in transformed space:
    $ \( 1 \, x_1 \, x_2 \, sqrt(2) x_1 x_2 \, x_1^2 \, x_2^2 \, . . . \)^T dot.op \( 1 \, x'_1 \, . . . \) $
    - Requires combinatorial powers and a large dot product
  + #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Kernel trick];: use kernel function for dot product in
    transformed space
    $ \( k + bold(underline(x))^T bold(underline(x))' \)^d $
    - Requires inner product of small vectors, then power of a number

- Kernel trick is more computationally efficient for inner product
  computation

==== Support Vector Machines (SVM)
<support-vector-machines-svm>
- Arguably one of the most successful classification algorithm, together
  with neural networks and random forests

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Idea];: find a separating hyperplane that maximizes the
  distance from the class points (aka "margin")

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[All the rage in 2005-2015]

  - Robust classifier handling outliers automatically
  - Strong theoretical justification of out-of-bound error
  - Strong link with VC dimension
  - Cool geometric interpretation
  - Solve a very complex optimization problem with some neat tricks
  - Works for both regression and classification

- SVM for classification:

  - Does not output probabilities (like logistic regression), but
    predicts directly the class
  - Has a notion of confidence, as distance from the margin

==== SVM: Notation and Conventions
<svm-notation-and-conventions>
- Assume that:
  + Outputs are encoded as $y_i in { - 1 \, 1 }$
  + Pull out $w_0$ from $bold(underline(w))$
    - The bias $w_0 = b$ plays a different role
    - $bold(underline(w)) = \( w_1 \, . . . \, w_d \)$ and there is no
      $x_0 = 1$
    - $bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x)) + b = 0$ is the equation
      of the separating hyperplane
  + $bold(underline(x))_n$ is the closest point to the hyperplane
    - It can be multiple points from different classes
- Normalize $bold(underline(w))$ and $b$ to get a canonical
  representation of the hyperplane imposing
  $\| bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_n + b \| = 1$

==== Primal Form of SVM Problem
<primal-form-of-svm-problem>
- You can rewrite it as:

  $ upright("find ") bold(underline(w)) \, b #h(2em) & \
  upright("minimize ") #h(2em) & 1 / 2 bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(w))\
  upright("subject to ") #h(2em) & y_i \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i + b \) gt.eq 1 #h(0em) forall i = 1 \, . . . \, n\
   $

- Note that under $bold(underline(w))$ minimal and linear separable
  classes, it is guaranteed that for at least one $bold(underline(x))_i$
  in the second equation will be equal to $1$ (as in the original
  problem)

  - In fact otherwise we could scale down $bold(underline(w))$ and $b$
    (which does not change the plane) to use the slack, against the
    hypothesis of minimality of $bold(underline(w))$

==== Dual (Lagrangian) Form of SVM Problem
<dual-lagrangian-form-of-svm-problem>
$ upright("minimize with respect to ") bold(underline(alpha)) #h(2em) & cal(L) \( bold(underline(alpha)) \) = sum_(i = 1)^N alpha_i - 1 / 2 sum_(i = 1)^N sum_(j = 1)^N y_i y_j alpha_i alpha_j bold(underline(x))_i^T bold(underline(x))_j\
upright("subject to ") #h(2em) & bold(underline(alpha)) gt.eq bold(underline(0)) \, sum_(i = 1)^N alpha_i y_i = 0\
 & bold(underline(w)) = sum_(i = 1)^N alpha_i y_i bold(underline(x))_i\
 $

- The equation for $bold(underline(w))$ is not a constraint, but it
  computes $bold(underline(w))$ (the plane) given
  $bold(underline(alpha))$, while $b$ is given by
  $sans("min") \| bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i + b \| = 1$

==== Dual Form of SVM as QP Problem
<dual-form-of-svm-as-qp-problem>
- The dual form of SVM problem is a convex quadratic programming
  problem, in the form:

  $ upright("minimize with respect to ") bold(underline(alpha)) #h(2em) & bold(underline(1))^T bold(underline(alpha)) - 1 / 2 bold(underline(alpha))^T bold(underline(underline(Q))) bold(underline(alpha))\
  upright("subject to ") #h(2em) & bold(underline(alpha)) gt.eq 0 \, bold(underline(y))^T bold(underline(alpha)) = 0\
   $

  where:

  - the matrix is
    $bold(underline(underline(Q))) = { y_i y_j bold(underline(x))_i^T bold(underline(x))_j }_(i j)$
  - $bold(underline(alpha))$ is the column vector
    $\( alpha_1 \, dots.h \, alpha_N \)$

==== Solving Dual Formulation of SVM Problem (1/2)
<solving-dual-formulation-of-svm-problem-12>
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Solving convex problem] for $alpha$

  - Feeding this problem to a QP solver, you get the optimal vector
    $bold(underline(alpha))$

- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Compute hyperplane] $bold(underline(w))$

  - From $bold(underline(alpha))$ recover the plane $bold(underline(w))$
    from the equation:
    $bold(underline(w)) = sum_(i = 1)^N alpha_i y_i bold(underline(x))_i$
  - Looking at the optimal $alpha_i$, you can observe that many of them
    are $0$
  - This is because when you applied the Lagrange multipliers to the
    inequalities:
    $y_i \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i + b \) gt.eq 1$,
    you got the KKT condition:
    $ alpha_i \( y_i \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i + b \) - 1 \) = 0 $
  - From these equations, either
    - $alpha_i = 0$ and $bold(underline(x))_i$ is an #emph[interior
      point] since it has non-null distance from the plane (i.e., slack)
      from the plane; or
    - $alpha_i eq.not 0$ and the slack is $0$, which implies that the
      $bold(underline(x))_i$ point touches the margin, i.e., it is a
      #emph[support vector]

==== Support Vectors and Degrees of Freedom for SVM
<support-vectors-and-degrees-of-freedom-for-svm>

- Because of the VC dimension, you have an in-sample quantity to bound

==== Non-Linear Transform for SVM
<non-linear-transform-for-svm>
- $Phi : cal(X) arrow.r cal(Z)$ transforms $bold(underline(x))_i$ into
  $bold(underline(z))_i = Phi \( bold(underline(x))_i \) in bb(R)^(tilde(d))$
  with $tilde(d) > d$

- Transform vectors through $Phi$ and apply SVM machinery

- Dual SVM formulation in $cal(Z)$ space:
  $ cal(L) \( bold(underline(alpha)) \) = sum_(i = 1)^N alpha_i - 1 / 2 sum_(i = 1)^N sum_(j = 1)^N alpha_i alpha_j y_i y_j bold(underline(z))_i^T bold(underline(z))_j $

- Note:

  - Optimization problem has same number of unknowns as original space
    (number of points $N$)
  - Support vectors live in $cal(Z)$: they have $alpha = 0$. In
    $cal(X)$, they are pre-images of support vectors
  - Decision boundary and margin can be represented in original space
    (not linear)

==== SVM in Higher Dimensional Space
<svm-in-higher-dimensional-space>
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Pros]
  - You don't pay the price in terms of complexity of optimization
    problem
    - Number of unknowns is still $N$ (different than a linear problem)
  - You don't pay the price in terms of increased generalization bounds
    - Number of support vectors is $lt.eq N$
    - This is because each hypothesis $h$ can be complex but the
      cardinality of the hypothesis set $cal(H)$ is the same
- #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Cons]
  - You pay a price to compute
    $Phi \( bold(underline(x))_i \)^T Phi \( bold(underline(x))_j \)$,
    since $Phi$ could be very complex
    - The kernel trick will remove this extra complexity by doing
      $Phi \( bold(underline(x))_i \)^T Phi \( bold(underline(x))_j \) = K_Phi \( bold(underline(x))_i \, bold(underline(x))_j \)$

==== Non-Linear Transform in SVM vs Kernel Trick
<non-linear-transform-in-svm-vs-kernel-trick>
- The trivial approach is:
  - Transform vectors with $Phi \( dot.op \)$
  - Apply all SVM machinery to the transformed vectors
  - #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Cons];: $Phi$ might be very complex, e.g., potentially
    exponential number of terms
- You can express the SVM problem formulation and the prediction in
  terms of a kernel
  $ K_Phi \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \) = Phi \( bold(underline(x)) \)^T Phi \( bold(underline(x))' \) = bold(underline(z))^T bold(underline(z))' $
  - You only need the kernel
    $K_Phi \( bold(underline(x)) \, bold(underline(x))' \)$ of the
    transformation $Phi \( dot.op \)$ and not $Phi \( dot.op \)$ itself

==== SVM in Terms of Kernel: Optimization Step
<svm-in-terms-of-kernel-optimization-step>
- When you build the QP formulation for the Lagrangian to compute the
  $alpha$ we can use
  $K_Phi \( bold(underline(x))_i \, bold(underline(x))_j \)$ instead of
  $bold(underline(z))_i^T bold(underline(z))_j$
  $ cal(L) \( bold(underline(alpha)) \) = sum_(n = 1)^N alpha_n - 1 / 2 sum_(n = 1)^N sum_(m = 1)^N y_n y_m alpha_n alpha_m K_Phi \( bold(underline(x))_n \, bold(underline(x))_m \) $

- $bold(underline(z))_n$ does not appear in the constraints

  $ bold(underline(alpha)) gt.eq bold(underline(0)) \, bold(underline(alpha))^T bold(underline(y)) = 0 $

==== SVM in Terms of Kernel: Prediction Step
<svm-in-terms-of-kernel-prediction-step>
- You need only inner products to compute a prediction for a given
  $bold(underline(z))$

- In fact to make predictions, you replace the expression of
  $tilde(bold(underline(w))) = sum_(i : alpha_i > 0) alpha_i y_i bold(underline(z))_i$
  in
  $h \( bold(underline(x)) \) = sans("sign") \( bold(underline(w))^T Phi \( bold(underline(x)) \) + b \)$,
  yielding:

  $ h \( bold(underline(x)) \) = sans("sign") \( sum_(i : alpha_i > 0) alpha_i y_i K_Phi \( bold(underline(x))_i \, bold(underline(x)) \) + b \) $

  where $b$ is given by
  $y_i \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(z))_i + b \) = 1$ for any
  support vector $bold(underline(x))_m$ and thus

  $ b = 1 / y_m - sum_(i : alpha_i > 0) alpha_i y_i K_Phi \( bold(underline(x))_i \, bold(underline(x))_m \) $

==== Implications of Kernel Trick in SVM
<implications-of-kernel-trick-in-svm>
- The "kernel trick" is a computational shortcut:

  - Use the kernel of the transformation instead of the transformation
    itself

- To use SVMs, compute inner products between transformed vectors
  $bold(underline(z))$

- The kernel trick implies:

  - No need to compute $Phi \( \)$
    - Use the kernel $K_Phi$, not the transformation $Phi$
  - No need to know $Phi$
    - With function $K_Phi$ as an inner product, use SVM machinery
      without knowing $cal(Z)$ space or transformation $Phi$
  - $Phi$ can be impossible to compute
    - $K_Phi$ can correspond to a transformation $Phi$ to an infinite
      dimensional space (e.g., Gaussian kernel)

==== Primal Formulation for Soft Margin SVM
<primal-formulation-for-soft-margin-svm>
- You want to introduce an error measure based on the margin violation
  for each point, so instead of the constraint:
  $ y_i \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i + b \) gt.eq 1 upright(" (hard margin)") $

- You can use:
  $ y_i \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i + b \) gt.eq 1 - xi_i \, upright(" where ") xi_i gt.eq 0 upright(" (soft margin)") $

- The cumulative margin violation is $C sum_(i = 1)^N xi_i$

- The soft margin SVM optimization (primal form) is:

  $ upright("find ") bold(underline(w)) \, b \, bold(underline(xi)) & \
  upright("minimize ") #h(2em) & 1 / 2 bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(w)) + C sum_(i = 1)^N xi_i\
  upright("subject to ") #h(2em) & y_i \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i + b \) gt.eq 1 - xi_i #h(0em) forall i\
   & xi_i gt.eq 0 $

==== Classes of Support Vectors for Soft Margin SVM
<classes-of-support-vectors-for-soft-margin-svm>
- There are 3 classes of points:
  - #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Margin support vectors];: they are exactly on the margin and
    define it
    - In primal form:
      $y_i \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i + b \) = 1 arrow.l.r.double xi_i = 0$
    - In dual form: $0 < alpha_i < C$
  - #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Non-margin support vectors];: they are inside the margin and
    classified correctly or not
    - In primal form:
      $y_i \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i + b \) < 1 arrow.l.r.double xi_i > 0$
    - In dual form: $alpha_i = C$
  - #text(fill: black, weight: "bold")[Non-support vectors];, i.e., interior points:
    - In primal form:
      $y_i \( bold(underline(w))^T bold(underline(x))_i + b \) > 1$
    - In dual form: $alpha_i = 0$

==== Intuition for C in SVM
<intuition-for-c-in-svm>
- $C$ represents how much penalty you incur for passing the margin
  - If $C$ is large, then SVM will try to fit all the points to avoid
    being penalized
    - Lower bias / higher variance
    - $C arrow.r oo$ which yields hard-margin SVM
  - If $C$ is small, then you allow margin violations
    - Higher bias / lower variance
- From another point of view $C prop 1 / lambda$, so large $C$ means
  small $lambda$ and thus small regularization
  - $C$ is chosen through cross validation, like any regularization
    parameter
