# Why Decisions, Not Predictions

## Course Roadmap

## From Data Science to Decision Science

* Why Traditional ML Falls Short
// From Lesson 08.1

- Traditional machine learning optimizes prediction accuracy on a fixed
  distribution, which ignores four things a decision requires:
  1. Causality: modeling correlation, not mechanisms
  2. Uncertainty: no quantification of what the model does not know
  3. The business objective: a prediction is not a decision
  4. Dynamics: the world reacts to the actions informed by the prediction
- Each gap turns a technically accurate model into a poor decision-maker

- **Key idea**: the rest of this lesson works through the cost of each gap in
  turn

* From Data Science to Decision Science
// From Lesson 08.1

- Data science and decision science optimize for different things
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("Dimension", "Data Science", "Decision Science"),
  rows: (
    ("Goal", "Predict outcomes", "Choose best actions"),
    ("Question", "What will happen?", "What should we do?"),
    ("Method", "Statistical models, ML", "Causal models, decision theory"),
    ("Output", "Score, forecast", "Action + expected outcome"),
    ("Metric", "Accuracy, AUC, RMSE", "Business outcome, ROI"),
    ("Data", "Observations", "Observations + causal structure"),
  ),
  bold-first-col: true,
)
```
- **Key idea**: teams that ship predictions while the business needs decisions
  leave the causal step, and the value, on the table

* Causal vs Predictive Questions
// From Lesson 08.1

- Every business question has a predictive form and a causal form, and only
  one of them says what to do
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("Predictive Question", "Causal Question"),
  rows: (
    ("Which customers will churn?", "What action prevents churn?"),
    ("What will sales be next quarter?", "How would a 10% price cut affect sales?"),
    ("Which students will fail the exam?", "Does tutoring improve exam scores?"),
    ("Which transactions are fraudulent?", "What policy change reduces fraud?"),
  ),
  bold-first-col: true,
)
```
- Predictive questions need only observational data and support monitoring and
  alerting
- **Key idea**: causal questions need causal models or experiments, and support
  decisions and policy design

* The Analytics Maturity Ladder
// From Lesson 08.1

- Organizations climb four levels of analytical sophistication
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("Level", "Question", "Tools"),
  rows: (
    ("0. Descriptive", "What happened?", "Dashboards, reports"),
    ("1. Predictive", "What will happen?", "ML, forecasting"),
    ("2. Causal", "Why? What if we act?", "DAGs, do-calculus"),
    ("3. Decision", "What should we do?", "Causal models + utility"),
  ),
  bold-first-col: true,
)
```
- **Problem**: most organizations are stuck at Level 1, predicting well but
  unable to say what to do
- **Key idea**: climbing to Levels 2 and 3 is the jump this book makes, from
  prediction pipelines to decision pipelines

# The Cost of Ignoring Causality

## When Correlation Misleads

* Correlation Encodes Confounding as Causation
// From Lesson 08.1, 11.1
// From paper [P007] A Survey on Causal Inference

- **Problem**: a purely predictive model learns $\Pr(Y | X)$ from historical
  data and absorbs any association in the data, spurious or not
  - It answers _"what will happen?"_, not _"what should we do?"_
  - It assumes the world stays fixed, even though the actions informed by the
    prediction change it
- **Example**: ice cream sales and drowning deaths are highly correlated
  - Predictor: _"more ice cream $\to$ predict more drownings"_ (accurate)
  - Decision built on it: _"ban ice cream to cut drownings"_ (disastrous)
  - Confounder: hot weather drives both
- **Key idea**: the observational and interventional quantities disagree
  $$\Pr(Y | X) \neq \Pr(Y | do(X))$$
  - Acting on $\Pr(Y | X)$ is what makes correlation-based decisions backfire

* Missing Interventions and Counterfactuals
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: a correlation model answers _"what is?"_, never _"what if?"_ or
  _"why?"_
- Two classes of business question stay permanently out of reach:
  - **Intervention**: _"If we cut price 10%, how do units sold and revenue
    change?"_
  - **Counterfactual**: _"Would this customer have churned had we offered a
    discount?"_
- **Example**: a tutoring program correlates with higher exam scores
  - Does tutoring cause the improvement, or do strong students self-select
    into it?
  - No amount of additional observational data resolves the question
- **Key idea**: answering these questions requires a causal model, not a more
  accurate predictor

* Selection Bias and the Missing Counterfactual
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: historical data records only the decisions actually made, never
  the alternative
  - Loan data shows outcomes for approved applicants, never for rejected ones
  - A missing-counterfactual problem present _before_ modeling starts,
    distinct from confounding
- **Example**: a credit model trained only on approved loans learns what
  predicts repayment among people the bank already trusted
  - It cannot say who among the rejected applicants would have repaid
- **Key idea**: a model can be accurate on the population it observed and
  still be blind to the population the business needed to reason about

## Structural Failure Modes

* Interference and Spillovers Across Units
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: standard ML assumes each unit's outcome depends only on its own
  treatment
  - Marketplaces, pricing equilibria, and social networks routinely violate
    this assumption
- **Example**: a discount shown only to group A can cannibalize purchases from
  group B
  - The two groups share the same limited supply or the same social network
  - Group B is no longer a clean counterfactual for group A
- **Key idea**: when one unit's action changes another unit's outcome, the
  usual estimate of a treatment effect is biased, however accurate the
  underlying predictor is

* Simpson's Paradox: When Aggregates Lie
// From Lesson 08.1, 11.1
// From paper [P005] Simpson's Paradox, Confounding, and Collapsibility

- **Definition**: a trend within every subgroup _reverses_ once the subgroups
  are pooled
- **Example**: a drug's recovery rate
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("Group", "Recovery w/ drug", "Recovery w/o drug"),
  rows: (
    ("Male", "81 / 87 = 93%", "234 / 270 = 87%"),
    ("Female", "192 / 263 = 73%", "55 / 80 = 69%"),
    ("Combined", "273 / 350 = 78%", "289 / 350 = 83%"),
  ),
  bold-first-col: true,
)
```
- The drug helps _each_ subgroup, yet appears to hurt when the groups are
  pooled
  - Gender correlates with both treatment choice and baseline recovery
- **Key idea**: a model can look correct in aggregate and be wrong, or
  harmful, for every segment it is applied to

* Collider Bias: Conditioning Creates Illusions
// From Lesson 08.1, 08.2

- **Definition**: a _collider_ is a variable influenced by two or more
  independent causes
  - Conditioning on a collider opens a spurious association between its
    causes, a mechanism known as Berkson's paradox
- **Example**: statistics skill and flattery both influence who gets promoted
  - Among everyone, statistics skill and flattery are independent
  - Among the _promoted_ only, they become negatively associated: bad at
    statistics implies must be a good flatterer
- **Key idea**: conditioning on the wrong variable, not just omitting the
  right one, is enough to manufacture a correlation with no causal meaning

* Discarding Domain Knowledge and Mechanism
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: a purely correlation-based pipeline discards known structure,
  physics, economics, or the causal graph, in favor of whatever pattern the
  data happens to contain
  - No principled way to inject constraints or priors
- **Example**: a wolf-vs-husky classifier with high test accuracy keys on
  _snow_ in the background, not on the animal
  - Accurate for the wrong reason, and worthless once conditions change
- **Key idea**: a model that ignores mechanism inherits every artifact of how
  the training data was collected

# The Cost of Ignoring Uncertainty

## Point Estimates in a Small-Data World

* Point Estimates Without Error Bars
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: most business problems are "small data" problems, yet standard
  ML pipelines emit a single number as if it were certain
  - No error bars, no posterior, no sense of how wrong the estimate could be
- **Example**: a demand forecast of "1,200 units next month" hides whether the
  true range is 1,100-1,300 or 400-3,000
  - The two ranges call for different inventory decisions
- **Key idea**: a decision-maker needs the spread of an estimate at least as
  much as its center

* Epistemic vs Aleatoric Uncertainty Conflated
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: standard models collapse two different kinds of uncertainty
  into one number
  - _Aleatoric_ uncertainty is irreducible randomness in the outcome itself
  - _Epistemic_ uncertainty comes from not having seen enough data, or from
    the wrong model
- Without separating them, a model cannot flag what it has never seen: it is
  confidently wrong exactly off-distribution, where decisions bite hardest
- **Example**: a fraud model's low predicted risk for a novel transaction
  pattern may reflect genuine low risk (aleatoric) or simply that the model
  has never seen this pattern before (epistemic)
- **Key idea**: only epistemic uncertainty shrinks with more data; treating
  both as the same number misleads the decision of when to collect more

* No Abstention: Systems That Never Say "I Don't Know"
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: a standard classifier or regressor always returns an answer,
  even when the input falls far outside its training distribution
  - No abstention or deferral mechanism routes uncertain cases to a human
- **Example**: a loan-approval model forced to score an applicant profile
  unlike anything in its training data still returns a confident approval or
  denial
- **Key idea**: a decision system that cannot say _"I don't know"_ cannot be
  trusted with the cases that matter most

* Overfitting and the Statistical Significance Traps
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: without a prior or assumptions to constrain the fit, nothing
  bounds overfitting
- Statistical significance traps compound the problem:
  - _Peeking_: checking a test repeatedly and stopping the moment $p < 0.05$
    inflates the true false-positive rate well past the nominal $5\%$
  - _Multiple comparisons_: testing many metrics at once all but guarantees a
    spurious win somewhere
  - _Burning the test set_: reusing the same held-out data across many rounds
    of tuning turns it into training data in disguise
- **Key idea**: overfitting is not a single mistake in one run, it accumulates
  from every repeated look at the same data

# The Cost of Ignoring the Business Objective

## Proxies, Costs, and Trade-offs

* Optimizing the Wrong Objective
// From Lesson 08.1
// From paper [P096] Problems of Monetary Management

- **Problem**: models chase a proxy metric such as clicks or accuracy instead
  of the business goal it stands in for, such as revenue, retention, or
  wellbeing
- **Definition**: _Goodhart's law_ states that once a proxy becomes a target,
  it stops measuring what you care about
- **Example**: a recommender optimized purely for click-through rate learns to
  surface sensational content, growing clicks while eroding long-term
  engagement
- **Key idea**: the objective a model is trained on becomes the objective the
  business actually gets, whether or not that was the intent

* Symmetric Losses for Asymmetric Errors
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: standard losses such as log-loss and mean squared error treat
  every error the same size
  - Business errors are not symmetric: a fraud false negative and a fraud
    false positive cost differently
- **Example**: a fraud model with 95% accuracy that blocks half of legitimate
  transactions is a costly decision system, however accurate it is by the
  standard metric
- **Key idea**: cost asymmetry has to be built into the objective, not bolted
  on after deployment as an afterthought

* Multi-Objective Decisions Under Constraints
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: real decisions trade off competing goals under budget,
  capacity, and legal limits
  - A single scalar loss cannot express a constrained, multi-stakeholder
    trade-off
- **Example**: a hospital choosing a cancer therapy weighs five-year survival,
  quality of life, and cost simultaneously
  - No single "best" therapy exists independent of how those three are
    weighed against each other
- **Key idea**: collapsing a multi-objective decision into one loss function
  hides the trade-off instead of making it explicit

## From Scores to Actionable Decisions

* Black-Box Scores Are Not Recommendations
// From Lesson 08.1, 13.1
// From paper [P001] Why Should I Trust You?

- **Problem**: a black-box score ranks units but names no lever and gives no
  _why_
  - A churn score does not say which action prevents churn
- Credit, hiring, and healthcare increasingly require explanations, not just
  scores, to justify decisions to regulators and stakeholders
- **Example**: instead of _"this customer has churn score 0.8"_, a useful
  recommendation says _"offering a discount lowers this customer's churn
  probability by 12 points"_
  - It names the lever, the effect, and the unit it acts on
- **Key idea**: a recommendation needs an actionable lever and an explanation,
  neither of which a bare prediction provides

* The Cost of Solving Sub-Problems Separately
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: a decision pipeline is often split into smaller, individually
  optimized pieces, a prediction model followed by a separate rule that turns
  predictions into actions
- Learning end-to-end has been shown to outperform stitching together
  separately-optimized parts, an approach known as decision-focused learning
- **Example**: an inventory system that forecasts demand accurately, then
  applies a fixed reorder rule, can order worse than a system trained to
  minimize inventory cost directly
- **Key idea**: optimizing each piece in isolation does not add up to
  optimizing the decision

# The Cost of Ignoring Dynamics and Feedback

## A World That Reacts

* The Static-World Assumption Breaks
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: any decision changes the world, breaking the distribution the
  model was trained on
- Three ways the world moves out from under a static model:
  - _Concept shift_: the link $\Pr(Y | X)$ between inputs and outcomes changes
  - _Covariate shift_: the input distribution $\Pr(X)$ changes while the
    relationship stays the same
  - _Label shift_: the outcome distribution $\Pr(Y)$ itself changes
- **Example**: a fraud model trained on last year's fraud rate degrades as
  attackers adopt new tactics, a label shift the model has no way to detect on
  its own
- **Key idea**: a model that encodes mechanism, not just pattern, degrades
  more gracefully because mechanisms are more stable across distribution
  shift than surface correlations

* Feedback Loops and Bias Amplification
// From Lesson 08.1
// From paper [P003] Data Feedback Loops

- **Problem**: predictions act on the world and reshape the future data the
  model is retrained on
- **Example**: a recommender that surfaces sensational content earns more
  clicks on that content, so future training data contains even more clicks
  on sensational content, and the next model recommends more of it still
- **Example**: over-policing a neighborhood produces more arrests there, the
  arrest data trains a model that flags the neighborhood as higher risk,
  which justifies more policing
- **Key idea**: a feedback loop turns a small initial bias into a growing one,
  purely from the model training on the outcomes of its own past decisions

* Performativity: When the Prediction Changes the Outcome
// From Lesson 08.1
// From paper [P099] Performative Prediction

- **Definition**: _performativity_ is a prediction that changes the very
  quantity it predicts, a sharper, named case of a feedback loop
- **Example**: a published credit-risk score moves borrower behavior, which
  moves the true default rate the score was trying to predict
- **Key idea**: once a prediction is performative, $\Pr(Y | X)$ is no longer a
  fixed target to fit, it is a function of which model gets deployed

## Missing Exploration and Long Horizons

* No Exploration: Trapped in the Logged Policy
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: an observational-only model never experiments, so it stays
  trapped inside the support of whatever policy logged the training data
  - It cannot learn the effect of an action nobody has ever taken, an
    overlap, or positivity, violation
- **Example**: a pricing model trained on historical prices that never
  dropped below \$20 cannot say what happens at \$15, no matter how much
  historical data it has
- **Key idea**: some business questions cannot be answered by more historical
  data, only by a deliberate intervention

* Delayed and Long-Horizon Effects
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: a model optimized for an immediate proxy ignores downstream
  outcomes and has no way to assign credit over time
- **Example**: a support team optimized for fast ticket closure closes
  tickets quickly, whether or not the underlying issue is resolved
  - The true cost, repeat complaints and lost trust, shows up months later,
    in a different metric
- **Key idea**: short-term proxies and long-term business outcomes can point
  in opposite directions, and only one of them is what the business actually
  cares about

* Strategic and Adversarial Response
// From Lesson 08.1

- **Problem**: once a model is deployed, the people it scores learn to
  optimize against it
  - Standard ML assumes passive agents; in business, agents are not passive
- **Example**: publishing the features of a credit-scoring model invites
  credit gaming; publishing a search-ranking algorithm invites
  keyword-stuffing
- **Key idea**: a model trained on how agents behaved before deployment can
  become wrong the moment agents learn how it scores them

# Why This Matters

## Roadmap

* Why AI Projects Fail
// From Lesson 08.1

- Most AI project failures are not modeling failures, they are framing
  failures:
  - **Misalignment**: the model optimizes a proxy, not the business goal
  - **Black boxes**: outputs cannot be explained, so they are not trusted or
    adopted
  - **No lever**: a score is delivered where an action was needed
  - **No feedback**: performance is never monitored after deployment
- **Key idea**: an accurate model that answers the wrong question is a failed
  project, however good its metrics look on a dashboard

* Four Costs, One Root Cause

- The four costs surveyed in this lesson, ignoring causality, uncertainty,
  the business objective, and dynamics, look unrelated but share a single
  root cause
- Each one comes from optimizing prediction accuracy on a fixed distribution
  instead of decision quality on a distribution the decision itself changes
- **Key idea**: fixing all four costs at once is not four separate projects,
  it is one shift in what the pipeline is built to optimize

* Roadmap: Where the Book Goes From Here

- This lesson catalogued what a purely predictive pipeline misses
- The rest of the book builds the pipeline that does not miss it:
  - **Part II** supplies the probabilistic and causal tools: Gaussian
    processes, variational inference, normalizing flows, and causal discovery
  - **Part III** turns those tools into single-step decisions: decision
    theory, policy learning, and partial identification
  - **Part IV** extends to multi-step and dynamic decisions: causal world
    models, causal reinforcement learning, and feedback loops
  - **Part V** covers deploying, monitoring, and governing the resulting
    decision systems in production
- **Key idea**: every later technique plugs into one loop
  $$data \to model \to policy \to action \to feedback$$
