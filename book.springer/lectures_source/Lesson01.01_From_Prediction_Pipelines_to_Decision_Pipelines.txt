# Why Business Decisions Require Causal Thinking

## The Problem with Predictions in Business Decisions

* Limitations of Traditional Machine Learning in Business Setups

- **Traditional ML relies on correlation**: it learns $\Pr(Y | X)$ from
  historical data
  - Answers _"what will happen?"_, not _"what should we do?"_
  - Optimized for predictive accuracy, not decision quality

- **Confounding is encoded as causation**
  - A hidden common cause makes $X$ and $Y$ correlate spuriously
  - $\Pr(Y | X) \neq \Pr(Y | do(X))$; acting on correlations backfires

- **No interventions or counterfactuals**
  - Cannot answer _"if we change $X$, what happens to $Y$?"_
  - Cannot reason about _"what would have happened otherwise?"_

- **Assumes a static world**
  - Any decision changes the world, breaking the training distribution
  - Concept, covariate, and label shift degrade accuracy over time

- **Ignores feedback loops**
  - Predictions act on the world and reshape future data
  - Leads to bias amplification (echo chambers, over-policing)

- **Lack of assessing uncertainty**
  - Most problems are "small data" problems

* What Prediction Tells You

- **Definition**: _Prediction_ answers the question _"What will happen?"_
  - Input: features $X$
  - Output: estimate $\hat{y} = \EE[Y | X]$
  - Optimized for accuracy on historical data

- **Strengths of predictions**
  - Find patterns in past data
  - Forecast future outcomes when conditions remain stable
  - Enable monitoring and alerting systems

- **Key limitation**: Accuracy $\neq$ understanding
  - A 95% churn predictor tells you _who leaves_, not _why_ or _what changes it_
  - Correlation alone cannot answer intervention questions

* What Prediction Cannot Tell You

- **Causal effects**: _"If we change X, what happens to Y?"_
  - E.g., _"If we lower prices by 10%, will revenue increase?"_
  - Observational data shows who bought at what price, not the effect of your
    intervention

- **Counterfactuals**: _"What would have happened with a different choice?"_
  - E.g., _"Would this customer churn if we offered a discount?"_
  - Requires reasoning about alternatives, not just patterns

- **Policy-relevant questions**: _"What should we do?"_
  - Predictions optimize for accuracy, not business outcomes
  - A 90% accurate model might guide worse decisions than an 85% one

- **Key idea**:
  - Predictions assume the world stays the same, but any decision change the
    world

* The Real Problem: Correlation vs. Causation

- **Ice cream sales and drowning deaths correlate strongly**
  - Both increase in summer
  - Accurate predictor: _"hot days predict more drownings"_

- **Naive decision**: ban ice cream to prevent drownings
  - Disastrous, because hot weather (_not ice cream_) causes both

- **Confounder**: a hidden variable that drives both the observed variables
  - Correlation through confounding looks like causation to correlation-based models

- **Pattern in business**: correlation-based decisions often backfire
  - Hotel pricing: high prices when demand is high; naive model says _"raise prices to sell more"_
  - In reality: demand drives prices and occupancy, not the reverse

## Prediction vs. Decision: Side-by-Side

* The Two Paradigms

- **Prediction pipeline** (correlation-based)
  - Collect data $\to$ fit model $\to$ score new units
  - Assumes joint distribution $\Pr(X, Y)$ at deployment matches training
  - No feedback: the system doesn't act, so the world stays unchanged

- **Decision pipeline** (causal)
  - Model the intervention $do(a)$ explicitly
  - Use causal model to compute $\Pr(Y | do(a))$
  - Loop: decide $\to$ observe outcome $\to$ update model

- **Core difference**
  - $\Pr(Y | X)$ is observational: _"What does X tell me about Y?"_
  - $\Pr(Y | do(X))$ is interventional: _"What happens if I set X?"_
  - These are _radically different_ when confounding is present

* Example: Hospital Staffing Paradox

- **Observation**: hospitals with more staff have higher mortality
  - Accurate predictor: fewer staff $\to$ lower mortality

- **Policy reversal**: naive decision to reduce staff
  - Would be catastrophic
  - Confounding: sicker patients need more staff; staff doesn't cause mortality

- **Causal resolution**: adjust for patient severity in a causal model
  - Within severity strata, more staff reduces mortality
  - Simpson's paradox in action

- **Key idea**: the causal graph tells you which confounders to adjust for

# Simpson's Paradox and Policy Reversals

* Simpson's Paradox: Definition and Intuition

- **Simpson's paradox**: a trend in subgroups reverses when aggregated
  - Within males: treatment helps more
  - Within females: treatment helps more
  - Pooled: treatment appears to hurt

- **Root cause**: a confounder is unevenly distributed across groups
  - E.g., gender affects both who gets treated and baseline recovery

- **Why it matters**: _same data, opposite decisions_ depending on adjustment
  - Ignoring the confounder $\to$ wrong policy
  - Causal knowledge $\to$ right policy

- **Remark**: most real datasets contain Simpson's paradoxes lurking in unseen subgroups

* Simpson's Paradox: Drug Recovery Example

- **Scenario**: evaluate whether a drug improves recovery

  - Aggregating masks this asymmetry

- **Decision rule**: condition on gender; the drug _does_ help

## The Costs of Ignoring Causality

* Confounding Bias

- **Definition**: a confounder $C$ is a common cause of treatment $X$ and outcome $Y$
  - Induces spurious association $X \leftrightarrow Y$ through $C$
  - Observational: $\Pr(Y | X) \neq \Pr(Y | do(X))$

- **Problem**: correlation-based models encode confounding as causal
  - Leads to wrong policies when acted upon

- **Example**: cold weather and ice cream sales
  - Predictor: more ice cream $\to$ predict more drowning
  - Policy: ban ice cream (catastrophic)
  - Mechanism: warm weather causes both

- **How to fix**: identify confounders in a causal graph, adjust for them via stratification or regression

* Feedback Loops and Amplification

- **Recommendation systems**
  - Model recommends sensational content $\to$ users click more
  - Future training data has more sensational content
  - Model recommends even more sensational content
  - Result: radicalization, echo chambers

- **Criminal justice**
  - Minorities policed more $\to$ arrested more $\to$ data biased against minorities
  - Model trained on biased arrests learns to flag minorities
  - Judges use model for bail/sentencing
  - Feedback loop reinforces and amplifies bias

- **Key insight**: predictions change the world through feedback loops
  - Must model the feedback explicitly
  - Ignoring it leads to spiral of bias and harm

* Distribution Shift Under Intervention

- **The world is always changing**
  - _Concept drift_: $\Pr(Y | X)$ changes over time; rules the model learned become stale
  - _Covariate shift_: $\Pr(X)$ changes; model trained on old distribution fails on new
  - _Label shift_: $\Pr(Y)$ changes; outcome distribution evolves (e.g., fraud tactics)

- **Problem**: prediction models assume stable world
  - Your decision changes that world
  - Distribution shifts, model becomes inaccurate

- **Solution**: causal models encode mechanisms, not just patterns
  - Mechanisms are more stable across shifts
  - Reason about _why_ to predict in new contexts

- **Example**: customer churn model trained in 2020
  - In 2023, customer preferences change; model accuracy drops
  - Causal model explaining _why_ customers churn adapts better

# The Ladder of Causation

* The Three Rungs of Causality

- **Judea Pearl's framework** for understanding what questions we can answer

\begingroup \scriptsize
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("Rung", "Symbol", "Question", "Activity"),
  rows: (
    ("1. Association", "$\\Pr(Y | X)$", "_What is?_", "Observing"),
    ("2. Intervention", "$\\Pr(Y | do(X))$", "_What if?_", "Intervening"),
    ("3. Counterfactual", "$\\Pr(Y_X | x', y')$", "_Why?_", "Imagining"),
  ),
)
```
\endgroup

- **Each rung builds on the previous**
  - Rung 1 (association) is pure data
  - Rung 2 (intervention) requires a causal model
  - Rung 3 (counterfactual) requires rich causal structure

- **Ladder property**: you cannot move down the ladder
  - Data alone (rung 1) cannot answer intervention questions (rung 2)

* Rung 1: Association

- **Question**: _"How would seeing $X$ change your belief in $Y$?"_

- **Math**: $\Pr(Y | X)$

- **Activity**: passive observation
  - Traditional ML/statistics based on this rung
  - What correlations exist in data?

- **Examples**
  - _"Do patients with high fever have infections?"_ (conditional probability)
  - _"What does a positive test signal about disease?"_
  - _"What do survey responses predict about voting?"_

- **Key limitation**: association tells you correlations, not mechanisms

* Rung 2: Intervention

- **Question**: _"What happens to $Y$ if you do $X$?"_

- **Math**: $\Pr(Y | do(X))$

- **Activity**: understand impact of action $X$ on $Y$
  - Requires causal model, not just observations
  - What if I intervene?

- **Examples**
  - _"If I take aspirin, will my headache cure?"_
  - _"If we ban sodas, what happens to obesity rates?"_
  - _"If I lower prices by 10%, will sales increase?"_

- **How to compute**: use back-door or front-door adjustment formulas
  - Cut edges into $X$ in the causal graph
  - Propagate through structural equations

* Rung 3: Counterfactuals

- **Question**: _"Was $X$ that caused $Y$?"_

- **Symbol**: $\Pr(Y_X | x', y')$; read as _"$Y$ if $X$ had been different, given we observed $X = x'$ and $Y = y'$"_

- **Activity**: imagine what would have happened if facts were different
  - Highest form of reasoning
  - Requires understanding cause-and-effect deeply

- **Examples**
  - _"Would this patient have survived with a different treatment?"_
  - _"What would jury conclude about negligence?"_
  - _"Why did marketing campaign fail?"_ (what was the key difference?)

- **Remark**: most causal inference focuses on rung 2; counterfactuals (rung 3) are harder and require richer models

# From Causal Models to Decisions

* Causal Models: Definition and Role

- **Definition**: a causal model specifies, for each variable $X_i$
  - Its direct causes (parents): $\text{Pa}(X_i)$
  - A structural mechanism: $X_i = f_i(\text{Pa}(X_i), U_i)$ with noise $U_i$

- **Enables reasoning about all three rungs**
  - _Observation_: $\Pr(Y | X)$ (condition on observations)
  - _Intervention_: $\Pr(Y | do(X))$ (mutate the graph)
  - _Counterfactual_: $\Pr(Y_{X=x} | X=x')$ (reason about alternative histories)

- **Example**: marketing team
  - Observe: users who open emails purchase more
  - Causal model enables: does _sending_ the email cause purchases?

- **Key idea**: the causal model sits between data and decisions

* Decision Loop with Causal Models

::: columns
:::: {.column width=45%}
- **Step 1**: Learn causal model from data
- **Step 2**: Compute expected outcome under candidate actions
  - $\EE[Y | do(a)]$ for each $a \in \mathcal{A}$
- **Step 3**: Apply utility function $U(y, a)$ to rank actions
  - Choose $a^* = \arg\max_a \EE[U(Y, a)]$
- **Step 4**: Execute action, observe outcome, update model
  - Loop: new data refines causal understanding

::::
:::: {.column width=50%}
```graphviz

    Action -> World [label="  intervene"];
}
```
::::
:::

* Bayesian Decision-Making

- **Two-step process**
  1. _Inference_: compute posterior $\Pr(\theta | \mathcal{D})$ from data
  2. _Decision_: choose action maximizing posterior expected utility

- **Formula**
  $$
  a^* = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}}
    \EE_{\theta \sim \Pr(\theta | \mathcal{D})}
    \left[
      \EE_{Y \sim \Pr(Y | do(a), \theta)}[U(Y)]
    \right]
  $$

- **Two types of uncertainty**
  - Inner expectation: aleatoric (outcome randomness given $\theta$)
  - Outer expectation: epistemic (parameter uncertainty from finite data)

- **Key advantage**: handles uncertainty rigorously
  - If the best action is robust across plausible parameters, we commit
  - If sensitive to parameter choices, run more experiments

* From Predictions to Causal Decisions

- **Prediction alone falls short**
  - Optimizes for accuracy, not business value
  - Ignores confounding and interventions

- **Causal inference adds mechanism understanding**
  - _Why_ do these factors matter?
  - What happens when we _intervene_ on them?

- **Decision theory adds rational action**
  - Rank actions by expected utility, not by prediction accuracy
  - Manage trade-offs (risk, multiple objectives) explicitly

- **Combination**: causal reasoning + Bayesian uncertainty + decision theory
  - Turns predictions into _robust_ actions

# The Causal AI Workflow in Practice

* Step 1: Clarify Business Outcomes

- **Problem**: most AI projects fail due to misalignment with business goals
  - Data scientists optimize for model accuracy
  - Business needs a decision

- **Questions to ask**
  - What process are you analyzing?
  - What is the desired outcome?
  - What are positive vs. negative outcomes?
  - What trade-offs exist (revenue vs. churn, cost vs. quality)?

- **Outcome**: a clear, measurable business objective
  - Not "predict X" but "maximize utility over outcomes"

* Step 2: Identify Interventions

- **Question**: what levers can we pull to achieve the outcome?

- **Examples** (e-commerce pricing)
  - Introduce new product?
  - Change price and adjust for demand elasticity?
  - Run sales vs. regular pricing?
  - Focus marketing on quality vs. value?

- **Key consideration**: what is _feasible_ to intervene on?
  - Can we actually change it?
  - What are costs and constraints?

- **Outcome**: a bounded set of candidate actions $\mathcal{A}$

* Step 3: Build a Causal DAG

- **Goal**: encode domain knowledge about relationships
  - What affects what?
  - Are effects direct or indirect (mediated)?
  - What confounders might exist?

- **Process**
  - Start simple: just the intervention and outcome
  - Add variables experts say matter
  - Distinguish observed from unobserved
  - Mark confounders and mediators

- **Benefits**
  - Communication tool for stakeholders
  - Guides data collection
  - Determines adjustment sets for causal inference

- **Example**: pricing decision
  - Intervention: price
  - Outcome: revenue (quantity sold $\times$ price)
  - Confounders: competitor offers, demand, seasonality
  - Mediators: quantity sold (post-treatment), customer perception

* Step 4: Collect and Prepare Data

- **Data aligned with model**
  - Measure variables in the DAG
  - Ensure temporal order matches causal direction
  - Handle missing values carefully

- **Quality matters**
  - Bias in training data propagates to model
  - Missing confounders distort effects

- **Transformation**
  - Encode categorical variables
  - Normalize or scale for algorithms
  - Align observed variables to DAG nodes

* Step 5: Estimate Causal Effects and Decide

- **Inference**: use causal inference methods
  - Back-door adjustment: control for confounders
  - Front-door adjustment: when confounders unobserved but mediators observed
  - Instrumental variables: leverage exogenous variation

- **Integrate utility**
  - For each action, compute expected outcome
  - Apply utility function to get expected value
  - Choose $a^* = \arg\max_a \EE[U(Y | do(a))]$

- **Validate robustness**
  - Sensitivity analysis: do conclusions hold under slightly different assumptions?
  - A/B test candidate action before full rollout

- **Deploy and learn**
  - Execute decision
  - Observe outcomes
  - Update model for next iteration

# Why Organizations Fail

* Problem 1: Confusing Correlation with Causation

- **Example**: low-priced products sell more
  - Correlation: price $\leftrightarrow$ quantity sold
  - Naive policy: lower all prices
  - Reality: demand is causally upstream; both price and sales respond to it
  - Result: revenue tanked because volume didn't increase enough

- **Example**: customers with high engagement churn less
  - Correlation: engagement $\leftrightarrow$ churn
  - Naive policy: force engagement (email campaigns, notifications)
  - Reality: low churn causes engagement, not the reverse
  - Result: spam drives more churn

- **Lesson**: correlation analysis without causal reasoning is dangerous

* Problem 2: Ignoring Feedback Loops

- **Recommendation feedback loops**
  - Algorithm recommends content $\to$ users engage $\to$ algorithm learns preferences $\to$ recommends more of same
  - Result: echo chambers, radicalization, polarization

- **Bias amplification in criminal justice**
  - Over-policed neighborhoods $\to$ more arrests $\to$ model learns neighborhood is high-crime $\to$ allocate more police
  - Feedback reinforces inequality indefinitely

- **Solution**: model feedback as part of the causal structure
  - Intervene on the right variable (e.g., diversify content, change policing)
  - Monitor for drift in model and world

* Problem 3: Distribution Shift

- **Your decision changes the world**
  - Model trained in 2020 on 2015--2020 data
  - By 2023, customer preferences evolved
  - Model accuracy drops because $\Pr(Y | X)$ changed

- **Why it happens**
  - Concept drift: relationships between variables shift
  - Covariate shift: input distribution changes
  - Label shift: outcome distribution changes

- **Solution**: causal models are more stable
  - Understand _mechanisms_, not just patterns
  - Can extrapolate to new contexts better
  - Mechanisms are often invariant; correlations are not

* Why Causal AI Matters: Summary

- **Prediction is not enough**
  - Correlation-based models cannot answer _what if_ questions
  - Confounding, feedback loops, and distribution shift all cause failure

- **Causality enables better decisions**
  - Understand mechanisms, not just patterns
  - Reason about interventions and counterfactuals
  - Detect when the world has changed

- **Bayesian decision theory makes it rigorous**
  - Rank actions by expected utility, not accuracy
  - Handle uncertainty (aleatoric and epistemic) explicitly
  - Learn from feedback loops

- **Key idea**: turn data into decisions, not just predictions
