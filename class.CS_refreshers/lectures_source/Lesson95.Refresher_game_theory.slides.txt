# Game Theory

## Foundations
* Game Theory: Definition

- **Definition**: _game theory_ is the mathematical study of _strategic
  interaction_ among rational decision-makers
  - Each agent's outcome depends on the actions of the others
  - Agents are _rational_, i.e., they act to maximize their own payoff

- **Key idea**: shift from _decision theory_ (one agent versus nature) to _many
  agents reasoning about each other_
  - _"What should I do, given that others are also reasoning about what I do?"_

- **Why it matters for ML**
  - Adversarial training (GANs), multi-agent RL, mechanism design, robustness
  - Any setting with several learning or optimizing agents is a game

* Historical Development and Key Figures

- **1928**: von Neumann proves the _minimax theorem_ for zero-sum games

- **1944**: von Neumann and Morgenstern, _"Theory of Games and Economic
  Behavior"_, founds the field

- **1950**: Nash defines the _Nash equilibrium_ and proves existence for finite
  games

- **1960s-70s**: Selten (subgame perfection) and Harsanyi (incomplete
  information) extend the theory

- **Nobel prizes**: Nash, Selten, Harsanyi (1994); Aumann, Schelling (2005);
  Hurwicz, Maskin, Myerson (2007, mechanism design)

- Game theory is a common language for strategic reasoning across many fields

* Elements of a Game

- **Definition**: a _game_ in normal form is a tuple $(N, \{S_i\}, \{u_i\})$
  - $N = \{1, \dots, n\}$ is the set of _players_
  - $S_i$ is the set of _strategies_ (actions) of player $i$
  - $u_i$ is the _payoff_ (utility) of player $i$, mapping a strategy profile to
    $\mathbb{R}$

- **Notation**
  - A _strategy profile_ is $s=(s_1,\dots,s_n)$
  - $s_{-i}$ denotes the strategies of _all players except_ $i$, so
    $s=(s_i,s_{-i})$

- **Key idea**: each player controls only $s_i$, but the payoff
  $u_i(s_i,s_{-i})$ depends on everyone

* Normal (Strategic) Form Representation

- **Definition**: the _normal form_ lists each player's payoff for every
  strategy profile as a _payoff matrix_
  - Row player picks a row, column player picks a column, _simultaneously_
  - Each cell holds the tuple $(u_1,u_2)$
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("P1 / P2", "Left", "Right"),

    ("Up", "(3, 2)", "(1, 1)"),
    ("Down", "(0, 0)", "(2, 3)"),

)
```

- **Remark**: the convention is _(row player payoff, column player payoff)_
- Best suited for _simultaneous_, one-shot games

* Extensive Form Representation

- **Definition**: the _extensive form_ represents a game as a _tree_, capturing
  the _order of moves_, choices, and information

- **Elements**
  - Nodes are decision points (whose turn it is)
  - Edges are actions
  - Leaves carry terminal payoffs $(u_1,u_2)$
  - _Information sets_ group nodes a player cannot tell apart

* Zero-Sum vs Non-Zero-Sum Games

- **Definition**: a game is _zero-sum_ if payoffs sum to a constant for every
  profile

  $$
  \sum_i u_i(s) = 0 \quad \text{for all } s
  $$
  - One player's gain is exactly another's loss, $u_1 = -u_2$
  - Purely _competitive_, with no room for mutual gain

- **Non-zero-sum**: the total payoff varies across profiles
  - Allows _cooperation_ and _mutual gains_ (or mutual losses)
  - E.g., Prisoner's Dilemma, trade

- **ML link**: GAN training is approximately a _zero-sum_ minimax game between
  generator and discriminator

## Rationality and Dominance
* Dominant Strategies

- **Definition**: strategy $s_i^*$ _strictly dominates_ $s_i'$ if it yields a
  higher payoff _regardless_ of what others do

  $$
  u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) \quad \forall s_{-i}
  $$

- A _dominant strategy_ dominates _all_ other strategies of player $i$
  - A rational player will always play it

- **Key idea**: if every player has a dominant strategy, the profile is a
  _dominant-strategy equilibrium_, the strongest solution concept

- **Example**: _"Defect"_ is the dominant strategy for each prisoner in the
  Prisoner's Dilemma

* Dominated Strategies and Iterated Elimination

- **Definition**: $s_i'$ is _strictly dominated_ if some other strategy always
  does strictly better
  - A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy

- **Algorithm**: _Iterated Elimination of Strictly Dominated Strategies_ (IESDS)
  1. Remove any strictly dominated strategy for any player
  2. In the reduced game, repeat
  3. Stop when no strategy is dominated

- **Pros**: for strict dominance the order of elimination does not change the
  result

- **Cons**: often does not shrink the game to a single outcome

* Rational Choice Theory and Its Limits

- **Definition**: _rational choice_ assumes each agent maximizes _expected
  utility_ given consistent preferences and beliefs
  - Preferences are complete and transitive (von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms)

- **Common knowledge of rationality**: everyone is rational, everyone knows that
  everyone is rational, and so on

- **Limitations**
  - Humans show _bounded rationality_ (limited computation, heuristics)
  - Framing, biases, and emotions cause systematic deviations
  - Multiple equilibria leave behavior under-determined

- **Relevance**: motivates _behavioral_ game theory and learning-based models

## Nash Equilibrium
* Nash Equilibrium: Definition and Intuition

- **Definition**: a profile $s^* = (s_i^*, s_{-i}^*)$ is a _Nash equilibrium_
  (NE) if no player can gain by deviating _unilaterally_

  $$
  u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \ge u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \quad \forall s_i, \ \forall i
  $$

- **Intuition**: a _self-enforcing_ agreement
  - Given what everyone else does, each player is already best responding
  - No _regret_ about one's own choice, holding others fixed

- **Remark**: a NE need not be _efficient_ (Prisoner's Dilemma) nor _unique_

- **Example**: (Defect, Defect) is the unique NE of the Prisoner's Dilemma

* Best Response and Reaction Functions

- **Definition**: the _best response_ of player $i$ to $s_{-i}$ is the set of
  payoff-maximizing strategies

  $$
  BR_i(s_{-i}) \defeq \arg\max_{s_i \in S_i} u_i(s_i, s_{-i})
  $$

- **Characterization**: $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium _iff_ every player best
  responds

  $$
  s_i^* \in BR_i(s_{-i}^*) \quad \forall i
  $$

- **Reaction function**: the best response viewed as a function of others'
  strategies
  - Nash equilibria are the _fixed points_ (intersections) of reaction functions

- **Use**: finding a NE reduces to solving the best-response conditions jointly

* Finding Pure-Strategy Nash Equilibria

- **Algorithm** (best-response underlining)
  1. In each column, mark the row player's best payoff
  2. In each row, mark the column player's best payoff
  3. Any cell marked on _both_ payoffs is a pure-strategy NE
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("P1 / P2", "Left", "Right"),
  rows: (
    ("Up", "(3*, 2*)", "(1, 1)"),
    ("Down", "(0, 0)", "(2*, 3*)"),
  ),
  caption: "Starred payoffs are best responses; (Up, Left) and (Down, Right) are pure NE",
  bold-first-col: true
)
```

- **Remark**: some games have _no_ pure NE (Matching Pennies), so we need mixed
  strategies

* Pure vs Mixed Strategy Equilibria

- A _pure strategy_ is a deterministic choice of a single action $s_i \in S_i$

- **Definition**: a _mixed strategy_ $\sigma_i$ is a probability distribution
  over actions

  $$
  \sigma_i \in \Delta(S_i), \quad \sigma_i(a) \ge 0, \ \sum_a \sigma_i(a) = 1
  $$
  - Payoffs become _expected_ payoffs
    $u_i(\sigma) = \EE_{a \sim \sigma}[u_i(a)]$

- **Indifference principle**: in a mixed NE, each player is _indifferent_ among
  all actions played with positive probability
  - This condition pins down the opponent's mixing probabilities

- **Key idea**: randomization makes a player _unpredictable_

* Existence and Uniqueness of Nash Equilibrium

- **Theorem** (Nash, 1950): every finite game, with finitely many players and
  actions, has at least one Nash equilibrium in _mixed_ strategies

- **Proof**
  1. The best-response correspondence maps the compact, convex set of mixed
     profiles to itself
  2. Apply _Kakutani's fixed-point theorem_, so a fixed point exists
  3. That fixed point is, by construction, a Nash equilibrium

- **Uniqueness**: not guaranteed
  - Many games have several equilibria (coordination games)
  - This motivates _equilibrium selection_ and _refinements_

## Canonical Games
* The Prisoner's Dilemma

- **Setup**: two suspects each choose to _Cooperate_ (stay silent) or _Defect_
  (betray); payoffs are negated prison years
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("P1 / P2", "Cooperate", "Defect"),
  rows: (
    ("Cooperate", "(-1, -1)", "(-3, 0)"),
    ("Defect", "(0, -3)", "(-2, -2)"),
  ),
  bold-first-col: true
)
```

- **Analysis**: _Defect_ strictly dominates for both, so the unique NE is
  (Defect, Defect)

- **Key idea**: individually rational choices yield a _collectively worse_
  outcome than (Cooperate, Cooperate)
  - The core tension between individual and group rationality

* Coordination Games and Battle of the Sexes

- **Setup**: both players prefer to coordinate but disagree on _which_ outcome
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("P1 / P2", "Opera", "Football"),
  rows: (
    ("Opera", "(2, 1)", "(0, 0)"),
    ("Football", "(0, 0)", "(1, 2)"),
  ),
  bold-first-col: true
)
```

- **Analysis**: two pure NE, (Opera, Opera) and (Football, Football), plus one
  mixed NE
  - Multiple equilibria create a _coordination problem_

- **Remark**: pure coordination underlies standards, language, and protocols

* Matching Pennies: No Pure Equilibrium

- **Setup**: a _zero-sum_ game; player 1 wins if the pennies match, player 2
  wins if they differ
```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("P1 / P2", "Heads", "Tails"),
  rows: (
    ("Heads", "(+1, -1)", "(-1, +1)"),
    ("Tails", "(-1, +1)", "(+1, -1)"),
  ),
  bold-first-col: true
)
```

- **Analysis**: no pure NE, since whoever is predictable loses
  - The unique NE is _fully mixed_, each plays Heads and Tails with probability
    $1/2$

- **Key idea**: mixed strategies are _necessary_, not optional

## Sequential Games
* Game Trees and Backward Induction

- **Setting**: sequential games of _perfect information_, where everyone sees
  prior moves

- **Algorithm** (_backward induction_)
  1. Start at the terminal nodes
  2. At each decision node the mover picks the action with the highest payoff
  3. Fold the value back up the tree until the root
```graphviz
digraph BackwardInduction {
  rankdir=TB;
  node [style=filled, fontname="Helvetica", fontsize=11];
  edge [fontname="Helvetica", fontsize=10];
  E [label="Entrant", fillcolor="#F4A6A6"];
  I [label="Incumbent", fillcolor="#A0D6D1"];
  out [label="(0, 2)", shape=box, fillcolor="#A6C8F4"];
  fight [label="(-1, -1)", shape=box, fillcolor="#A6C8F4"];
  accom [label="(1, 1)", shape=box, fillcolor="#B2E2B2"];
  E -> out [label="Stay out"];
  E -> I [label="Enter"];
  I -> fight [label="Fight"];
  I -> accom [label="Accommodate"];
}
```

- **Result**: the incumbent accommodates ($1 > -1$), so the entrant enters; the
  equilibrium path rules out the non-credible threat to _Fight_

* Subgame Perfect Equilibrium

- **Problem**: some Nash equilibria of sequential games rely on _non-credible
  threats_, i.e., actions no rational player would carry out

- **Definition**: a _subgame perfect equilibrium_ (SPE) is a profile that is a
  Nash equilibrium in _every subgame_
  - A _subgame_ is any node together with everything that follows it

- **Key idea**: the SPE is exactly what _backward induction_ finds
  - It refines NE by enforcing rationality _off_ the equilibrium path

- **Example**: _"I will start a price war"_ is not credible if the war also
  hurts the incumbent

* Credible Commitments and First-Mover Advantage

- **First-mover advantage**: moving first can _shape_ others' best responses
  - E.g., a _Stackelberg_ leader commits to a quantity and the follower reacts

- **Paradox of commitment**: _reducing_ your own options can _improve_ your
  outcome
  - Removing the temptation to deviate makes a threat or promise _credible_

- **Example**: burning bridges, warranties, doomsday devices

- **ML link**: _Stackelberg games_ model leader-follower learning, as in
  strategic classification and adversarial robustness

## Mixed Strategies and Uncertainty
* Computing Mixed Strategy Equilibria

- **Method** (indifference principle): choose your mixing probabilities so the
  _opponent_ becomes indifferent

- **Example** (Matching Pennies): let P1 play Heads with probability $p$
  - P2's expected payoff from Heads is $-p + (1 - p)$
  - P2's expected payoff from Tails is $p - (1 - p)$
  - Indifference gives $-p + (1 - p) = p - (1 - p) \implies p = 1/2$

- **Interpretation**: at equilibrium each player is indifferent, so any mix over
  the support is a best response

- **Remark**: computing a NE is hard in general, formally _PPAD-complete_

* Interpreting Mixed Strategies

- **Question**: what does it _mean_ to randomize a decision?

- **Interpretations**
  - _Deliberate randomization_: stay unpredictable (poker bluffing, tax audits)
  - _Population frequencies_: the fraction of a population playing each pure
    action
  - _Beliefs_: others' uncertainty about your action
  - _Purification_ (Harsanyi): a mixed NE is the limit of pure equilibria of a
    game with small private payoff shocks

- **Key idea**: the same equilibrium object supports several real-world readings

* Information Asymmetry

- **Definition**: an _information asymmetry_ arises when players hold different
  private information relevant to payoffs

- **Consequences**
  - _Adverse selection_: hidden _type_ before contracting (the used-car "lemons"
    problem)
  - _Moral hazard_: hidden _action_ after contracting (insurance)

- **Responses**
  - _Signaling_: the informed party reveals its type (education, warranties)
  - _Screening_: the uninformed party designs choices that separate types

- **ML link**: strategic agents may _manipulate_ features, leading to strategic
  classification and incentive-aware learning

## Zero-Sum Games
* Zero-Sum Games and the Minimax Theorem

- **Setting**: a two-player zero-sum game with $u_1 = -u_2 = u$

- **Theorem** (von Neumann, 1928): every finite two-player zero-sum game has a
  _value_ $v$ with

  $$
  \max_{\sigma_1} \min_{\sigma_2} u(\sigma_1, \sigma_2)
    = \min_{\sigma_2} \max_{\sigma_1} u(\sigma_1, \sigma_2)
    = v
  $$

- **Intuition**: it is safe to reveal your _mixed_ strategy, since the order of
  play does not change the guaranteed value

- **Computation**: solvable by _linear programming_, where LP duality yields the
  minimax

- **ML link**: robust optimization, adversarial training, GANs

* Correlated Equilibrium

- **Definition**: a _correlated equilibrium_ is a distribution over profiles,
  generated by a shared _signal_, such that no player wants to deviate from
  their recommendation

- **Intuition**: a trusted mediator (a traffic light) privately recommends
  actions
  - Following the recommendation is a best response given beliefs about others

- **Properties**
  - _Generalizes_ Nash equilibrium, since every NE is a correlated equilibrium
  - Can achieve a _higher_ average payoff than any NE
  - Computable by _linear programming_

- **ML link**: _no-regret_ learning dynamics converge to correlated equilibria

## Cooperative Game Theory
* Cooperative Games and Coalition Formation

- **Shift**: from _non-cooperative_ (individual strategies) to _cooperative_
  (binding agreements among groups)

- **Definition**: a _coalitional_ game is $(N, v)$, where $v(C)$ is the worth a
  coalition $C \subseteq N$ can secure
  - The _grand coalition_ is $N$; the question is how to split $v(N)$

- **Core questions**
  - Which coalitions _form_?
  - How is the joint value _divided_ fairly and stably?

- **ML link**: _Shapley values_ for feature attribution, incentives in
  _federated learning_

* The Core and the Shapley Value

- **Definition (Core)**: the set of allocations $x$ that no coalition can
  improve upon

  $$
  \sum_{i \in C} x_i \ge v(C) \ \ \forall C, \qquad \sum_{i \in N} x_i = v(N)
  $$
  - Captures _stability_, though the core can be empty

- **Definition (Shapley value)**: assigns to $i$ its _average marginal
  contribution_ over all orderings

  $$
  \phi_i = \sum_{C \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}}
    \frac{|C|! \, (n - |C| - 1)!}{n!}
    \, [\, v(C \cup \{i\}) - v(C) \,]
  $$
  - The _unique_ allocation satisfying efficiency, symmetry, null-player, and
    additivity

- **ML link**: SHAP explanations apply this to feature importance

* Bargaining Solutions

- **Problem**: two parties split a surplus; failure yields a _disagreement
  point_ $d$

- **Nash bargaining solution**: maximize the product of gains over $d$

  $$
  \max_{(u_1, u_2) \in F} (u_1 - d_1)(u_2 - d_2)
  $$
  - Uniquely characterized by _Pareto efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance_,
    and _independence of irrelevant alternatives_ (IIA)

- **Remark**: the _Kalai-Smorodinsky_ solution replaces IIA with a monotonicity
  axiom

- **Use**: wage negotiation, resource sharing, multi-agent surplus division

## Incomplete Information
* Bayesian Games

- **Definition**: a _Bayesian game_ adds private _types_ $\theta_i$ drawn from a
  common prior; payoffs depend on the type profile
  - A _strategy_ maps each type to an action, $s_i(\theta_i)$

- **Definition**: a _Bayes-Nash equilibrium_ has each type best respond in
  expectation over others' types

  $$
  s_i(\theta_i) \in \arg\max_{a_i}
    \EE_{\theta_{-i}} \big[ u_i(a_i, s_{-i}(\theta_{-i}); \theta) \big]
  $$

- **Harsanyi's insight**: incomplete information becomes _imperfect_ information
  by adding a "Nature" move that draws types

- **Use**: auctions with private valuations, uncertainty in multi-agent ML

* Signaling and Screening Games

- **Signaling**: the _informed_ party moves first to reveal its type through a
  costly action
  - _Separating equilibrium_: different types send different signals, so the
    type is revealed
  - _Pooling equilibrium_: all types send the same signal, so nothing is
    revealed
  - E.g., Spence's education model

- **Screening**: the _uninformed_ party moves first, offering a menu that makes
  types _self-select_
  - E.g., insurance deductibles, versioned pricing

- **Key idea**: costly, type-dependent actions make private information
  _credible_

* Mechanism Design Basics

- **Definition**: _mechanism design_ is _"inverse game theory"_, where we design
  the rules so that self-interested play yields a desired outcome

- **Goals**
  - _Incentive compatibility_ (IC): truth-telling is optimal
  - _Individual rationality_ (IR): participation is beneficial

- **Revelation principle**: any outcome achievable in equilibrium is achievable
  by a _truthful direct_ mechanism
  - This lets designers focus only on truthful mechanisms

- **Example**: the _VCG mechanism_ charges each agent its _externality_, making
  truthful reporting dominant

* Auction Theory

- **Formats**
  - _First-price sealed-bid_: the highest bidder pays their own bid
  - _Second-price_ (Vickrey): the highest bidder pays the _second_-highest bid
  - _English_ (ascending), _Dutch_ (descending), _all-pay_

- **Key result**: in a second-price auction, _truthful bidding_ ($bid = value$)
  is a _dominant_ strategy

- **Revenue equivalence theorem**: under standard assumptions all these formats
  give the same _expected revenue_

- **ML link**: online ad auctions, cloud pricing, learning to bid

## Evolutionary and Dynamic Games
* Evolutionary Game Theory

- **Shift**: replace _rationality_ with _selection_, so more successful
  strategies reproduce faster

- **Replicator dynamics**: a strategy's share grows with its _fitness_ relative
  to the population average

  $$
  \dot{x}_i = x_i \, \big[ f_i(x) - \bar{f}(x) \big]
  $$

- **Definition**: an _evolutionarily stable strategy_ (ESS) cannot be invaded by
  a small fraction of mutants
  - Every ESS is a Nash equilibrium, so ESS is a _refinement_

- **ML link**: population-based training, MARL, co-evolutionary search

* Repeated Games

- **Setup**: a _stage game_ is played repeatedly; players observe the history
  and can _condition_ on it

- **Key idea**: the _shadow of the future_ can sustain _cooperation_ that is
  impossible in the one-shot game
  - Deviation triggers _punishment_ in later rounds

- **Folk theorem**: with patient enough players (discount factor
  $\delta \to
  1$), essentially any individually rational payoff is a subgame
  perfect equilibrium

- **Example**: _tit-for-tat_ sustains cooperation in the repeated Prisoner's
  Dilemma

* Stochastic (Markov) Games

- **Definition**: a _stochastic game_ generalizes repeated games with _states_;
  actions yield rewards and cause _transitions_

  $$
  (\mathcal{S}, \{\mathcal{A}_i\}, \Pr(s' | s, a), \{r_i\}, \gamma)
  $$

- **Two special cases**
  - A single player reduces it to a _Markov Decision Process_ (MDP)
  - A single state reduces it to a _repeated game_

- **Solution**: a _Markov perfect equilibrium_ uses stationary, state-dependent
  strategies

- **ML link**: the formal foundation of _multi-agent reinforcement learning_

## Learning and Algorithmic Game Theory
* Learning in Games

- **Question**: how do agents _reach_ an equilibrium without computing it
  directly?

- **Dynamics**
  - _Fictitious play_: best respond to the empirical frequency of opponents'
    past actions
  - _No-regret_ (regret matching): minimize accumulated regret across actions
  - _Multiplicative weights_: reweight actions by their realized payoff

- **Convergence**
  - No-regret dynamics converge to the set of _correlated_ equilibria
  - In zero-sum games, average play converges to the _minimax_ value

- **ML link**: the training dynamics of GANs and of self-play

* Algorithmic Game Theory

- **Focus**: the _computational_ side of games, i.e., complexity, algorithms,
  and the efficiency of equilibria

- **Complexity**: computing a Nash equilibrium is _PPAD-complete_, believed
  intractable in general

- **Definition (Price of Anarchy)**: the ratio of the _worst_ equilibrium cost
  to the _optimal_ centralized cost

  $$
  \mathrm{PoA} =
    \frac{\max_{s \in NE} \mathrm{cost}(s)}{\min_{s} \mathrm{cost}(s)}
  $$
  - Quantifies the inefficiency of selfish behavior (e.g., traffic routing)

- **ML link**: designing learning systems that reach _good_ equilibria quickly

* Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)

- **Setting**: several RL agents learn _simultaneously_ in a shared, stateful
  environment, i.e., a stochastic game

- **Core challenges**
  - _Non-stationarity_: each agent's environment shifts as others learn
  - _Credit assignment_ across agents
  - _Exploration_ versus coordination
  - Equilibrium _selection_ among many

- **Approaches**: independent learners, centralized-training with
  decentralized-execution, self-play, opponent modeling

- **Successes**: AlphaGo and AlphaZero (self-play), Poker (CFR), Diplomacy

## Connections to Machine Learning
* Game Theory and Machine Learning: Connections

- **Adversarial ML**: GANs and adversarial examples are _minimax_ games between
  a model and an adversary

- **Multi-agent systems**: MARL is formalized as a _stochastic game_, and
  equilibria describe the joint behavior

- **Markets and mechanisms**: auctions, ad exchanges, and _incentive-aware_
  learning shape agent behavior

- **Attribution and cooperation**: _Shapley values_ explain models, and
  cooperative theory guides _federated learning_

- **Robustness**: minimax and _Stackelberg_ formulations yield worst-case
  guarantees

- **Key takeaway**: whenever multiple optimizers interact, ML _is_ game theory
