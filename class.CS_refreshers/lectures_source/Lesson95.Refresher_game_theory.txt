# Game Theory Refresher

## Introduction

* What is Game Theory?

- **Definition**: _Game theory_ is mathematical framework for analyzing strategic interactions between rational decision-makers, where each participant's outcome depends on decisions of others
  - Study of conflict and cooperation
  - Models situations with interdependent choices
  - Predicts outcomes under rationality assumptions

- **Key insight**: Individual rationality doesn't guarantee collectively optimal outcomes
  - Your best choice depends on what others choose
  - Need to anticipate others' thinking

- **Example**: Two companies choosing prices
  - Each wants maximum profit
  - But aggressive pricing by competitor forces price war
  - Both end up worse off than if they cooperated

- **Application areas**: Economics, political science, biology, computer science, military strategy, business


* Historical Development and Key Figures

- **Foundations** (1920s-1940s)
  - John von Neumann: Minimax theorem for zero-sum games
  - Émile Borel: Early work on strategic games

- **Nash Equilibrium** (1950)
  - John Nash: Proved existence of equilibrium in mixed strategies
  - Revolutionized analysis of non-zero-sum games

- **Refinements** (1960s-1980s)
  - Reinhard Selten: Subgame perfect equilibrium
  - John Harsanyi: Incomplete information games
  - Shared Nobel Prize (1994)

- **Modern developments** (1990s-present)
  - Behavioral game theory: bounded rationality
  - Evolutionary game theory: population dynamics
  - Algorithmic game theory: computational complexity


* Applications Across Disciplines

- **Economics**: Markets, auctions, pricing strategies, oligopoly competition
  - Example: Smartphone makers balancing innovation vs. cost

- **Political science**: Voting, negotiations, conflict resolution, policy making
  - Example: Legislative coalitions and bargaining

- **Biology**: Evolution, animal behavior, competition for resources
  - Example: Predator-prey dynamics, mating strategies

- **Computer science**: Network routing, resource allocation, algorithm design
  - Example: Internet routing protocols, blockchain consensus

- **Business strategy**: Entry decisions, product development, R&D investment
  - Example: When to enter new markets, patent racing

- **Everyday life**: Dating, job interviews, salary negotiation
  - Example: Job candidate deciding salary ask based on expected employer counteroffer


## Part I: Foundations

### Chapter 1: Basic Concepts

* Games and Payoffs

- **Definition**: A _game_ consists of players, strategies they can choose, and payoffs (outcomes) resulting from combinations of choices
  - Players: Decision-makers (firms, individuals, countries)
  - Strategies: All possible actions player can take
  - Payoffs: Numerical outcomes reflecting preferences (profit, utility, points)

- **Payoff matrix**: Table showing outcome for each combination of player choices
  ```
  Example: Matching Pennies
           Opponent: Heads    Opponent: Tails
  You: H      +1                 -1
  You: T      -1                 +1
  ```
  - Your payoff listed first, opponent's second

- **Key assumption**: Players have complete information about payoff structures
  - Each player knows everyone's payoffs
  - Players are rational (maximize own payoff)

- **Example**: Restaurant pricing game
  - Two restaurants competing on same street
  - Each chooses High or Low price
  - High price → high profit if competitor also charges high; low profit if undercut
  - Low price → moderate profit regardless of competitor


* Players and Strategies

- **Definition**: _Players_ are rational decision-makers in the game; _strategies_ are complete action plans available to each player

- **Strategy types**:
  - **Pure strategy**: Choose one specific action with certainty
    - Example: "Always choose Left"
  - **Mixed strategy**: Randomize over multiple actions with specific probabilities
    - Example: "Choose Left with probability 0.6, Right with probability 0.4"

- **Strategy space**: Set of all available strategies to a player
  - Can be finite (two choices: Cooperate, Defect)
  - Can be infinite (any real-valued price from 0 to $100)

- **Strategy profile**: Specification of one strategy for each player
  - Example in 2-player game: (Player 1 chooses Up, Player 2 chooses Left)
  - Determines the outcome

- **Example**: Chess game
  - Players: White and Black
  - Strategies: Incredibly large (roughly 10<SUP>120</SUP> possible games)
  - Strategy for White at first move: e4, d4, Nf3, ... (20 legal moves)


* Zero-Sum vs. Non-Zero-Sum Games

- **Zero-sum games**: One player's gain is exactly another's loss
  - Total payoff across all players = 0 (or constant)
  - Purely competitive, no mutual gain possible
  - Example: Chess (one player wins, other loses)

- **Non-zero-sum games**: Outcomes can be mutually beneficial or mutually harmful
  - Total payoff varies depending on choices
  - Mix of cooperation and competition possible
  - Example: Business deal (both parties can benefit)

- **Payoff matrix comparison**:
  ```
  Zero-Sum Example:
           B: Left    B: Right
  A: Up    (1,-1)    (-1,1)
  A: Down  (-1,1)    (1,-1)

  Non-Zero-Sum Example:
           B: Left    B: Right
  A: Up    (2,2)     (0,3)
  A: Down  (3,0)     (1,1)
  ```

- **Strategic implication**:
  - Zero-sum: Assume worst about opponent; pure adversarial thinking
  - Non-zero-sum: Opportunities for mutual gain; coordination matters


* Normal Form Representation

- **Definition**: _Normal form_ (or _strategic form_) displays game as matrix with players' strategies as rows/columns and payoffs in cells
  - Represents simultaneous-move games
  - Shows all strategic choices clearly

- **Structure for 2-player game**:
  ```
  Payoffs shown as (Row player's payoff, Column player's payoff)

          Player B: Left    Player B: Right
  P1: Up   (3,1)            (0,2)
  P1: Down (2,2)            (1,3)
  ```
  - Rows: Player 1 strategies
  - Columns: Player 2 strategies
  - Cell: (P1 payoff, P2 payoff)

- **Advantages**:
  - Compact, easy to visualize
  - Immediately see all outcomes
  - Facilitates equilibrium analysis

- **Limitations**:
  - Only for simultaneous-move games
  - Can be large for many strategies
  - Hides timing/sequential nature of play

- **Example**: Prisoners' Dilemma
  ```
            Cooperate  Defect
  Cooperate  (-1,-1)  (-3,0)
  Defect     (0,-3)   (-2,-2)
  ```


* Extensive Form Representation

- **Definition**: _Extensive form_ represents game as decision tree showing:
  - Sequence of moves (who moves when)
  - Information available at each decision point
  - Payoffs at terminal nodes (end of game)

- **Structure**:
  - Nodes: Decision points or chance events
  - Branches: Actions available
  - Terminal nodes: End outcomes with payoffs
  - Information sets: Nodes where player can't distinguish (doesn't know which node they're at)

- **Advantages**:
  - Captures sequential/dynamic nature
  - Shows timing: who moves first, second, etc.
  - Reveals information gaps (what player knows when)
  - Enables backward induction analysis

- **Limitations**:
  - Can become unwieldy for complex games
  - Harder to analyze than normal form for simultaneous moves

- **Example**: Entry game tree
  ```
  Incumbent firm decides: Fight or Accommodate
    If Accommodate → Entrant gets $3, Incumbent gets $1
    If Fight → Entrant decides: Stay or Exit
      If Exit → Entrant gets -$1, Incumbent gets $4
      If Stay → Both in price war: Entrant gets -$1, Incumbent gets $0
  ```
  - Extensive form shows Incumbent moves first, then Entrant responds
  - Normal form would hide this timing


### Chapter 2: Dominant Strategies and Rationality

* Dominant Strategies

- **Definition**: A strategy is _dominant_ if it yields higher payoff than any alternative strategy, regardless of what opponent does
  - "Best response to everything"
  - Player should always choose dominant strategy if one exists

- **Formal definition**: Strategy $s_i$ is dominant if for all strategies $s_i'$ of player $i$ and all strategy profiles $s_{-i}$ of opponents:
  $$u_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \geq u_i(s_i', s_{-i})$$
  with strict inequality for some $s_{-i}$

- **Example**: Matching Pennies does NOT have dominant strategy
  - If opponent chooses Heads, you prefer Tails (+1 vs -1)
  - If opponent chooses Tails, you prefer Heads (+1 vs -1)
  - No single strategy beats both

- **Example**: Prisoners' Dilemma HAS dominant strategy
  ```
            Cooperate  Defect
  Cooperate  (-1,-1)  (-3,0)
  Defect     (0,-3)   (-2,-2)
  ```
  - If opponent cooperates: Defect gives 0 > -1, so Defect is better
  - If opponent defects: Defect gives -2 > -3, so Defect is better
  - **Defect is dominant** for both players

- **Remark**: If every player has dominant strategy, solution is straightforward
  - Each plays dominant strategy
  - Outcome is predictable
  - But often leads to suboptimal collective result


* Dominated Strategies

- **Definition**: A strategy is _dominated_ if another strategy yields higher payoff regardless of what opponents do
  - Rational player should never choose dominated strategy
  - Can eliminate dominated strategies to simplify analysis

- **Formal definition**: Strategy $s_i'$ is dominated by $s_i$ if:
  $$u_i(s_i, s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i', s_{-i})$$
  for all strategy profiles $s_{-i}$ of opponents

- **Example**: Game with dominated strategy
  ```
            Left  Middle  Right
  Up       (3,1)  (2,0)  (1,2)
  Down     (2,1)  (4,0)  (3,2)
  ```
  - Up is dominated by Down
    - Down gives 2 vs Up gives 3 when others play Left: Down worse
    - Wait, let me recalculate: actually Up gives 3, Down gives 2
    - Against Left: Up=3, Down=2, so Up better
    - Against Middle: Up=2, Down=4, so Down better
    - Up is not dominated
  
  - Let me use clearer example:
  ```
            Left  Right
  Up       (2,1)  (1,1)
  Middle   (3,1)  (2,1)
  Down     (1,1)  (0,1)
  ```
  - Down is dominated by both Up and Middle
    - Down gives (1,0) against all; Up gives (2,1) which is better
    - Rational player never chooses Down

- **Iterative elimination**: Remove dominated strategies, repeat with reduced game
  - Sometimes eliminates all but equilibrium
  - Reveals strategic structure
