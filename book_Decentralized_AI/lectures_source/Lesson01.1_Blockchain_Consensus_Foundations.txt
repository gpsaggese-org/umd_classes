# Blockchain Consensus Foundations

## Agreement Without a Trusted Party

* Why Distributed Systems Need Consensus

- @Motivation@: many independent parties want to share one evolving record (e.g.,
  who owns what, what happened, in what order) with no central party controlling
  it

- @Problem@: _without a trusted intermediary_, how do _mutually distrusting nodes
  agree_ on a single, consistent history?
  - Nodes can be slow, offline, or actively malicious
  - Network messages can be delayed, dropped, or reordered

- @Key idea@: **consensus** is the mechanism that lets independent nodes converge
  on the same ledger despite these failures

- @Example@:
  - A bank's database is the single source of truth for account balances
  - A blockchain replaces the single database with thousands of independently
    operated copies that must all agree

* What a Blockchain Guarantees

// From data605/93/Blockchain
- @Definition@: a **blockchain** is _replicated, shared, synchronized data
  based on consensus_, letting multiple parties operate on data without
  trusting each other or a central authority
  - I.e., a distributed, append-only, immutable ledger

- @Characteristics@:
  - No single point of failure
  - No central authority to compromise or shut down
  - Designed to resist malicious tampering, up to a certain bound (e.g., a
    51% attack)

- @Variants@:
  - _Permissionless_: anyone can join as a node
    - E.g., Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana, Cardano
    - Need to reach consensus among anonymous, ever-changing participants
  - _Permissioned_: only vetted parties may participate
    - E.g., a proprietary chain

* The Double-Spending Problem

- @Problem@:
  - A digital token is just data
  - Data can be copied
  - Nothing stops the same coin from being sent to two different people at once

- @(Naive) Solution@: a trusted central ledger checks each payment against the
  account balance before approving it
  - Cons: reintroduces the single point of trust and failure a distributed
    ledger is trying to remove

// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.course.cs.Decentralized_finance.Harvey.Coursera.txt
- @Solution@: Bitcoin solved double-spending by making the entire
  transaction history public and ordering it through **consensus** rather
  than through a trusted party
  - Every node can independently verify who currently owns what

- @Key idea@: double-spending is fundamentally an ordering problem, whichever
  transaction is agreed to have happened first is the valid one

* Consensus Protocol: The Rules for Extending the Chain

::: columns
:::: {.column width=60%}
- @Definition@: a **consensus protocol** is the set of rules that determines
  _which blocks_ become part of the blockchain and _in what order_

- @Goal@: all honest nodes converge on the same chain, even though
  - Some nodes may be faulty or malicious
  - Messages are delayed or partial

- @Overview@: the rest of this lesson compares three families of consensus
  protocols
  1. **Proof-of-work**: propose via a computational lottery
  2. **Proof-of-stake**: propose via a stake-weighted lottery
  3. **BFT-style**: propose via leader rotation and explicit voting
::::

## Ledger and State Models

* Two Ways to Represent Ledger State

- @Motivation@: before nodes can agree on new blocks, they need a shared
  model of what "the current state" even means

- @Question@: does the ledger track discrete _units of money_ or a single
  _running balance_ per account?

- @Overview@: two dominant models emerged
  - **UTXO model** (Bitcoin-style): the ledger tracks unspent coin fragments
  - **Account model** (Ethereum-style): the ledger tracks one balance per
    address

- This choice affects privacy, parallelism, and what a smart contract platform
  can express, not just bookkeeping style

* UTXO Model: Definition

// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.course.cs.Decentralized_finance.Harvey.Coursera.txt
- @Definition@: an **Unspent Transaction Output (UTXO)** is a discrete,
  unspent chunk of currency created by a prior transaction and owned by
  whoever can sign for it
  - Aka "coin fragment"

- @Characteristics@:
  - A wallet's balance is the sum of all UTXOs it can spend, not a single
    stored number
  - A transaction consumes one or more UTXOs as _inputs_ and creates new
    UTXOs as _outputs_
  - Once spent, a UTXO is destroyed, it can never be reused

- @Example@: Bitcoin represents every balance as a collection of UTXOs

// TODO(ai_gp): Add picture

* UTXO Model: Worked Example

// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.course.cs.Decentralized_finance.Harvey.Coursera.txt
- @Example@: a wallet holds 3.21 BTC as three UTXOs: 3, 0.2, and 0.01 BTC
  - Goal: send 0.15 BTC to Bob
  - The wallet picks the 0.2 BTC UTXO as input (since the 0.01 BTC UTXO alone is
    not enough)
  - The transaction creates two new UTXOs: 0.15 BTC to Bob, 0.05 BTC in
    change back to the sender

- @Remark@: change is not automatic, unlike a bank account, a UTXO transaction
  must explicitly send any leftover value back to the sender as a new output

- @Intuition@: paying with UTXOs is like paying cash with bills, you cannot
  split a $20 bill in the register, you hand it over and get change back

* Account-Based Model: Definition

- @Definition@: in the **account model**, the ledger stores one running
  balance per address, and a transaction directly debits the sender's
  balance and credits the recipient's
  - Aka "balance model"
- @Characteristics@:
  - State is a simple key-value map: address $\to$ balance (and, for smart
    contracts, address $\to$ contract storage)
  - A **nonce** per account (a sequential counter) prevents replaying the
    same transaction twice
- @Example@: Ethereum represents every balance as a single number per
  address, updated in place

* UTXO vs Account: Tradeoffs

```{=typst}

    ("Smart contract fit", "Poor, no persistent account state", "Natural, contracts have storage"),
```

- @Key idea@: Bitcoin's UTXO model optimizes for simple, parallel, auditable
  payments, Ethereum's account model optimizes for expressive, stateful
  smart contracts

* Finality and Chain Reorganizations

::: columns
:::: {.column width=55%}
- @Definition@: **finality** is the point at which a block is considered
  permanent and can no longer be removed from the canonical chain
- @Problem@: two miners (or validators) can produce competing blocks at
  nearly the same height, temporarily forking the chain
- @Definition@: a **chain reorganization** ("reorg") happens when nodes
  switch to a different, now-heavier fork, discarding the blocks that lose
  - Transactions only in the discarded blocks become unconfirmed again
::::
:::: {.column width=42%}

- @Comparison@:
  - **Probabilistic finality** (e.g., Bitcoin): confidence in a block grows
    with each block built on top of it, but is never exactly 100%
  - **Absolute finality** (e.g., BFT-style protocols): once a supermajority
    votes for a block, it is finalized and provably cannot be reverted
// From paper Neu et al., "Ebb-and-Flow Protocols: A Resolution of the
// Availability-Finality Dilemma" (2021)
- @Remark@: modern protocols increasingly combine both, a fast, available
  probabilistic layer plus a slower, finalizing layer

## Proof-of-Work Consensus

* Proof-of-Work: The Mining Process

// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.course.cs.Decentralized_finance.Harvey.Coursera.txt
- @Algorithm@: to propose the next block, a **miner**:
  1. Collects unconfirmed transactions from the **mempool**
  2. Verifies each transaction is valid (correct signatures, no
     double-spend)
  3. Assembles the valid transactions into a block, hashed together in a
     **Merkle tree**
  4. Searches for a **nonce** such that the block's hash has enough leading
     zeros
- @Intuition@: finding the nonce is a brute-force lottery, there is no
  shortcut, only trying billions of guesses per second
- @Remark@: any other node can verify a found nonce instantly, checking a
  hash is cheap even though finding it is expensive

* Why Proof-of-Work Resists Tampering

- @Key idea@: **proof-of-work (PoW)** makes proposing a block expensive to
  do, but cheap to verify, and ties "the right to propose" to real-world
  computational cost
- @Definition@: under PoW, honest nodes always extend the **longest valid
  chain**, this is the tie-breaking rule that resolves competing forks
// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.crypto.DeFi_and_the_future_of_finance.Harvey.2021.txt
- @Remark@: proof-of-work is a computationally intensive lottery to decide
  which block gets added, participants agree the longest chain is the truth
- @Intuition@: rewriting history requires redoing the computational work for
  every block after the point you want to change, and outrunning everyone
  else's honest work at the same time

* The 51% Attack

- @Definition@: a **51% attack** occurs when a single actor (or coalition)
  controls a majority of a network's mining power, letting it outrace the
  honest chain and rewrite recent history
// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.course.cs.Decentralized_finance.Harvey.Coursera.txt
- @Characteristics@:
  - The attacker can double-spend and censor transactions
  - The attacker cannot forge signatures, create coins from nothing, or
    steal funds from addresses it does not control
- @Remark@: acquiring 51% of a large network's hash power costs billions of
  dollars, and using it would collapse trust (and price) in the asset being
  attacked
  - The only economically rational actor for such an attack is one trying to
    destroy the network's value, not profit from it

* Proof-of-Work: Pros and Cons

- @Pros@
  - Proven reliability, over a decade of continuous operation on Bitcoin
  - Predictable block times, difficulty self-adjusts if blocks come too
    fast or too slow
  - Fully permissionless, anyone with hardware can participate
// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.course.cs.Decentralized_finance.Harvey.Coursera.txt
- @Cons@
  - Enormous electricity consumption
  - Specialized hardware (ASICs) gives an advantage that centralizes mining
    into a few large pools
  - Low transaction throughput and high latency

* Proof-of-Work at Scale: The Throughput Ceiling

// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.course.cs.Decentralized_finance.Harvey.Coursera.txt

- @Key idea@: full redundancy (every node re-verifies everything) buys
  trustlessness at the cost of throughput, this ceiling is what motivates
  proof-of-stake and layer-2 scaling
- @Remark@: layer-2 scaling and sharding, which relax this bottleneck
  directly, are covered in Lesson 04.01

## Proof-of-Stake Consensus

* Proof-of-Stake: Definition

// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.crypto.DeFi_and_the_future_of_finance.Harvey.2021.txt
- @Definition@: in **proof-of-stake (PoS)**, validators lock up (_stake_)
  capital as collateral, and are chosen, roughly in proportion to their
  stake, to propose and validate the next block
- @Comparison@:
  - PoS replaces PoW's "who computes the most" with "who has staked the
    most" as the basis for the block-proposal lottery
  - No specialized hardware is needed, the "work" is capital at risk, not
    electricity spent
- @Example@: Ethereum moved from PoW to PoS in 2022 ("The Merge")

* Proof-of-Stake: Aligning Incentives

// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.course.cs.Decentralized_finance.Harvey.Coursera.txt
- @Positive incentive@: a validator that proposes and attests to valid
  blocks earns rewards from transaction fees and issuance
- @Negative incentive@: a validator caught approving an invalid or
  conflicting block has part of its stake destroyed
  - Aka "slashing"
- @Key idea@: the validators with the most value at risk are also the ones
  with the strongest incentive to keep the chain honest, incentives and
  security are aligned by design

* Proof-of-Stake vs Proof-of-Work

```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("Property", "Proof-of-work", "Proof-of-stake"),
  rows: (
    ("Resource spent", "Computation / electricity", "Staked capital"),
    ("51% attack cost", "Buy or rent majority hash power", "Acquire majority of staked tokens"),
    ("Energy use", "Very high", "Orders of magnitude lower"),
    ("Misbehavior penalty", "None, only wasted electricity", "Slashing of stake"),
    ("Centralization risk", "ASIC / mining-pool concentration", "Wealth concentration (\"rich get richer\")"),
  ),
  caption: "Proof-of-work vs proof-of-stake",
  col-widths: (1.3fr, 1.7fr, 1.7fr),
)
```

- @Remark@: the "rich get richer" critique (Matthew effect) is a genuine
  centralization pressure in PoS, but the claim that PoS is inherently less
  secure than PoW is generally considered debunked

* Other Consensus Mechanisms

- @Variants@: beyond plain PoW and PoS, several intermediate designs trade
  off different properties
  - **Delegated PoS**: token holders vote for a small set of delegates who
    do the validating
  - **Proof of authority**: a fixed set of pre-approved, identified
    validators
  - **Proof of capacity**: the lottery is weighted by disk space committed,
    not computation
  - **Proof of activity**: hybrid of PoW block proposal with PoS-style
    validator signing
// From notes/IN_PROGRESS.course.cs.Decentralized_finance.Harvey.Coursera.txt
- @Remark@: the broad industry direction over the past decade has moved
  from PoW toward PoS and its variants, primarily for energy and
  scalability reasons

## BFT-Style Consensus

* The Byzantine Generals Problem

- @Motivation@: PoW and PoS both use a computational or economic lottery to
  pick a proposer, a different family of protocols instead has known
  validators vote explicitly
- @Definition@: the **Byzantine Generals Problem** asks how a group of
  generals, some of whom may be traitors, can agree on a single battle plan
  by exchanging messages alone
  - A traitor can send different, contradictory messages to different
    generals
- @Key idea@: this is a stand-in for any distributed system where some nodes
  may fail arbitrarily, including lying, rather than just crashing

* Byzantine Fault Tolerance: Definition

- @Definition@: a protocol is **Byzantine fault tolerant (BFT)** if honest
  nodes still reach agreement despite up to $f$ nodes behaving arbitrarily
  (crashing, lying, colluding)
// From paper Castro et al., "Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance" (1999)
- @Fact@: classical BFT protocols guarantee safety and liveness only if the
  total number of nodes $n$ satisfies $n \geq 3f + 1$
  - E.g., tolerating 1 faulty node out of 4 requires all 4 to participate in
    every decision
- @Remark@: this bound is tight, no protocol can tolerate $f$ Byzantine
  nodes among $n \leq 3f$ participants

* Practical BFT: Voting to Agreement

::: columns
:::: {.column width=55%}
// From paper Castro et al., "Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance" (1999)
- @Algorithm@: **Practical BFT (PBFT)** reaches agreement in three message
  rounds per block
  1. _Pre-prepare_: the leader proposes a block to all validators
  2. _Prepare_: validators broadcast that they saw the same proposal
  3. _Commit_: once a validator sees $2f+1$ matching prepares, it commits
     and broadcasts that too
- @Cons@: communication cost grows quadratically with the number of
  validators ($O(n^2)$ messages), limiting PBFT to a few hundred validators
::::
:::: {.column width=42%}

- @Key idea@: a block is final the moment $2f+1$ commit messages are
  collected, no waiting for probabilistic confidence to build over many
  later blocks

* Tendermint: BFT for Blockchains

// From paper Buchman, "Tendermint: Byzantine Fault Tolerance in the Age
// of Blockchains" (2016)
- @Definition@: **Tendermint** adapts PBFT-style voting into a
  blockchain-native, round-based protocol with a rotating leader
- @Characteristics@:
  - Each round has a designated proposer, chosen by a deterministic,
    stake-weighted schedule
  - Validators vote in two phases (_prevote_, _precommit_), a block commits
    once it receives precommits from more than two-thirds of voting power
  - Finality is immediate and absolute, once committed, a block cannot be
    reverted without violating the $n \geq 3f+1$ assumption
- @Example@: Tendermint underlies Cosmos and its application-specific
  blockchains

* HotStuff: Linear BFT

::: columns
:::: {.column width=55%}
// From paper Yin et al., "HotStuff: BFT Consensus with Linearity and
// Responsiveness" (2019)
- @Problem@: PBFT-style all-to-all voting does not scale past a small
  validator set
- @Solution@: **HotStuff** pipelines the voting phases through the leader,
  using threshold signatures so each phase needs only $O(n)$ messages
  instead of $O(n^2)$
  - Aka "linear BFT"
- @Key idea@: HotStuff also achieves _responsiveness_, it commits as fast
  as the network actually allows, rather than waiting out a fixed timeout
- @Example@: HotStuff-style protocols underlie several modern chains, e.g.,
  Aptos and Diem's original design
::::

## Safety, Liveness, and Choosing a Model

* Two Guarantees Every Consensus Protocol Must Balance

- @Motivation@: PoW, PoS, and BFT are different mechanisms for the same
  underlying job, and each makes a different tradeoff between two formal
  guarantees
- @Definition@:
  - **Safety**: nothing bad happens, honest nodes never finalize two
    conflicting blocks
  - **Liveness**: something good eventually happens, the chain keeps making
    progress, new valid transactions eventually get included
- @Key idea@: a protocol can fail by violating either guarantee, and the two
  failures look very different

* Safety Failure: A Fork That Should Not Exist

- @Definition@: a **safety violation** happens when honest nodes finalize
  two different, conflicting blocks at the same height
- @Example@: if a BFT-style protocol committed a block with fewer than
  $2f+1$ genuine votes (e.g., because Byzantine nodes forged votes), two
  incompatible histories could both appear final
- @Remark@: safety violations are catastrophic, once something is "final",
  downstream systems (exchanges, contracts) act on it as permanent
- @Key idea@: correctly designed protocols prefer to halt rather than risk a
  safety violation, this is exactly what a liveness failure is

* Liveness Failure: The Chain Stalls

- @Definition@: a **liveness violation** happens when the network stops
  making progress, no new block gets finalized even though no safety rule
  was broken
- @Example@: in a BFT-style protocol, if network delays or a bad leader
  prevent $2f+1$ matching votes from ever assembling, the round times out
  and simply retries
- @Comparison@:
  - PoW/PoS (Nakamoto-style) protocols favor **liveness**, they always
    produce a next block, even under network partitions, at the cost of
    only probabilistic safety
  - Classical BFT protocols favor **safety**, they refuse to finalize a
    block until a genuine supermajority agrees, at the cost of stalling if
    that supermajority cannot be reached

* The Safety-Liveness Tradeoff

// From paper Garay et al., "The Bitcoin Backbone Protocol: Analysis and
// Applications" (2015)
- @Fact@: no consensus protocol can guarantee both perfect safety and
  perfect liveness under fully asynchronous network conditions with even
  one faulty node
  - This is a foundational distributed-systems result (the FLP
    impossibility result), blockchains work around it with timing or
    probabilistic assumptions
- @Key idea@: every consensus design in this lesson is really a choice of
  _which assumption to relax_
  - PoW/PoS relax: accept only probabilistic, not absolute, safety
  - BFT relaxes: accept a synchrony assumption (messages arrive within a
    known bound) and a bounded, known validator set

* Choosing a Consensus Mechanism

```{=typst}
#styled-table(
  headers: ("Setting", "Recommended family", "Why"),
  rows: (
    ("Open, permissionless, high-value network", "PoW or PoS", "No bound on who can join, probabilistic safety is acceptable"),
    ("Permissioned consortium or app-chain", "BFT-style (Tendermint, HotStuff)", "Known validator set, fast absolute finality matters"),
    ("Latency-sensitive settlement", "BFT-style", "Sub-second, provable finality vs minutes of confirmations"),
    ("Maximal decentralization, minimal trust", "PoW", "No stake concentration, purely external resource cost"),
  ),
  caption: "Matching a consensus mechanism to network requirements",
  col-widths: (1.8fr, 1.6fr, 2.2fr),
)
```

- @Key idea@: there is no universally best consensus mechanism, only a best
  mechanism for a given tradeoff between openness, finality speed, and
  trust assumptions

* Blockchain Consensus Foundations: Summary and Open Questions

- @Key takeaways@:
  - A **blockchain** is a replicated ledger that reaches agreement through
    **consensus**, not through a trusted intermediary
  - The **UTXO** and **account** models represent state differently,
    trading off privacy and parallelism against smart-contract
    expressiveness
  - **Proof-of-work** and **proof-of-stake** use a lottery (computational
    or stake-weighted) to pick a proposer, favoring liveness with
    probabilistic safety
  - **BFT-style** protocols (PBFT, Tendermint, HotStuff) use explicit
    voting among known validators to get absolute finality, at the cost of
    needing a bounded validator set
  - Every design answers the same underlying **safety vs liveness**
    tradeoff differently
- @Open questions@:
  - How should a network reconfigure its validator set over time without
    reintroducing a trusted party?
  - Can a protocol get BFT-style fast finality at Nakamoto-style
    permissionless scale?
  - How much of this consensus machinery still matters once AI agents, not
    only humans, are the ones proposing and validating transactions?
